As a fit, the power series still has some shortcomings. As $ x $ increases, the number of high-order terms grows very fast, requiring a very large coefficient to suppress them. Can we find a better basis for fitting, one that is not the power of $ x $?

The motivation is hard to understand, after all, we are not Fourier. But we can find such a set of basis $ e^{ikx}$, or equivalently written as $\sin{kx},\cos{kx}$. We will find that this set of basis has quite good properties. Consider the inner product space formed by the integral from $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ to $\frac{\pi}{2}$, then this set of basis is orthogonal. This means we can find the corresponding coefficients through

$$
f(x)=\sum a_ke^{ikx}
$$

direct integration. Specifically:

The transition from the power series to the Fourier series is essentially a shift from "approximating any function using algebraic polynomials" **to** "approximating any function using periodic trigonometric polynomials." This process involves a change of basis space, which also reveals the profound motivation of "orthogonal decomposition" in modern mathematics.

### Limitations and Motivation of the Taylor Series (Power Series)

For integrals of original functions that are difficult to find directly, such as $\int_{0}^{1} e^{x^2} dx $, we cannot express the result using elementary functions. To solve this dilemma, the most natural motivation is to transform the complex non-polynomial function into the "infinite-term polynomial" that we are most familiar with for integration.

Using the known Taylor expansion formula:

$$
e^t = \sum_{n=0}^{+\infty} \frac{t^n}{n!}
$$

Let $ t = x^2 $, we can expand this integral term into a power series:

$$
\int_{0}^{1} e^{x^2} dx = \int_{0}^{1} \sum_{n=0}^{+\infty} \frac{(x^2)^n}{n!} dx
$$

In essence, this approach assumes that a function $ f(x)$ belongs to the space spanned by monomials as a basis. When expanded at $ x_0 = 0 $, its form is:

$$
f(x) \in \text{span}\{1, x, x^2, x^3, \dots, x^n, \dots\}
$$

$$
f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{+\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!} x^n = S(x), \quad x \in (-R, +R)
$$

**Analysis of Limitations (Confidence Rating: High):**

1. **Strict Restriction of Convergence Domain**: The Taylor series is only valid within the radius of convergence $ x \in (-R, +R)$. Once it exceeds this range, the series diverges and cannot represent global properties.
    
2. **Non-periodic High-Order Explosion**: Because the monomial basis $ x^n $ tends to infinity at $ x \to \infty $ (as shown by the diverging curve in Figure $ x^n $), it is extremely unsuitable for describing signals that are periodic, oscillatory, or have locally severe fluctuations.
    

### Generalization from Algebraic Basis to General Function Basis

To escape the spatial limitations of power series, we need to abstract a more general basis representation. Suppose there exists a broader set of function basis $\{\varphi_0(x), \varphi_1(x), \varphi_2(x), \dots, \varphi_n(x), \dots\}$ such that:

$$
f(x) \in \text{span}\{\varphi_0(x), \varphi_1(x), \varphi_2(x), \dots, \varphi_n(x), \dots\}
$$

For specific physical and mathematical scenarios—such as a function $ f(x)$ with a period of $ T $ (satisfying $ f(x+T) = f(x)$), or a complex signal exhibiting dense local oscillations:

Ordinary power series have very low approximation efficiency when dealing with functions that have periodic boundaries or complex local textures. We urgently need a basis that naturally possesses periodicity (like oscillating repeatedly in $[0, T]$, $[0, 2T]$) and is bounded over the entire domain.

### Construction of Fourier Series (Trigonometric Series)

Since the goal is to approximate periodic signals and oscillatory functions, the most perfect basis choice is naturally simple harmonic motion (trigonometric functions). We construct a complete orthogonal basis composed of constants, sine functions, and cosine functions:

$$
\{1, \sin(x), \cos(x), \sin(2x), \cos(2x), \dots, \sin(nx), \cos(nx), \dots\}
$$

Then, an arbitrary function $ f(x)$ can be seen as a "vector" in this basis space:

$$
\forall f(x) \in \text{span}\{1, \sin(x), \cos(x), \sin(2x), \cos(2x), \dots, \sin(nx), \cos(nx), \dots\}
$$

**Core Question Posed:**

For any given function $ f(x)$, does there necessarily exist a unique set of coefficients $\{a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n, \dots\}$ and $\{b_1, b_2, \dots, b_n, \dots\}$ such that the function can be decomposed as:

$$
f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx))
$$

And how far can the domain of the independent variable $ x \in ?$ for which this formula holds be extended?

Through this basis transformation, we successfully decomposed a complex time-domain signal into a combination of harmonics of different frequencies. Just as in linear algebra, where coefficients are extracted using a dual basis, the "orthogonality" of trigonometric functions (i.e., the integral product of trigonometric functions of different frequencies over a period is zero) serves as a powerful tool for subsequently extracting this set of coefficients $\{a_n, b_n\}$.

From determining the coefficients of the Fourier series, to using them to solve partial differential equations (like the heat conduction equation), and finally to understanding the concept of "distance" in function approximation from a geometric perspective—this stage completes the leap from "pure function decomposition" to "spatial geometry and dynamic applications."

### Physical Background: Introduction of the Non-homogeneous Heat Conduction Equation

To demonstrate the powerful capability of solving problems using Fourier series, we introduce a classic physical model: **the temperature change control of a uniform, fixed-end rod**.

Assume the length of the rod is $\pi $, and consider its temperature distribution function $ u(x, t)$, which satisfies the following non-homogeneous heat conduction equation with an external heat source:

$$
u_t = u_{x2} + f(x)
$$

- **$ u_t $ (Left-end term)**: The rate of change of temperature with respect to time (accumulation of heat over time).
    
- **$ u_{xx}$ (Right-end first term)**: The heat conduction term, representing diffusion caused by non-uniform spatial temperature.
    
- **$ f(x)$ (Right-end second term)**: The external heat source term, which does not change with time and only depends on the position $ x $.
    

**Core Motivation**: Facing such a partial differential equation (PDE), direct solving is extremely difficult. Due to the excellent property of the trigonometric function basis, where differentiation leaves the form unchanged (only changing the coefficients), our natural motivation is to perform a Fourier series expansion of the unknown solution $ u(x,t)$ and the known source term $ f(x)$ in the spatial domain, thereby reducing and decoupling the "partial differential equation" into a set of "ordinary differential equations".

### Spectral Method Solution: Algebraicization of Partial Differential Equations

We treat $ u(x, t)$ as a combination over a spatial basis, whose coefficients are functions of time; simultaneously, we also perform a trigonometric expansion of the external heat source $ f(x)$:

$$
\begin{aligned} u(x, t) &= \frac{a_0(t)}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ a_n(t) \cos(nx) + b_n(t) \sin(nx) \right] \\ f(x) &= \frac{\alpha_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ \alpha_n \cos(nx) + \beta_n \sin(nx) \right] \end{aligned}
$$

#### 1. Operator Action (Differentiation)

Using the property of term-by-term differentiation of Fourier series, we calculate both the left and right sides of the control equation separately:

- **Differentiation with respect to time**:

$$
u_t = \frac{a_0'(t)}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ a_n'(t) \cos(nx) + b_n'(t) \sin(nx) \right]
$$

- **Second differentiation with respect to space**:

$$
u_{xx} = 0 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ -n^2 a_n(t) \cos(nx) - n^2 b_n(t) \sin(nx) \right]
$$

#### 2. Substitution into the Equation and Coefficient Matching

Substituting the above expansions into the original physical equation $ u_t = u_{xx} + f(x)$, and utilizing the linear independence of the trigonometric basis, we can equate the coefficients of every harmonic component. Thus, **the partial differential equation successfully transforms into an infinite set of independent ordinary differential equations (ODEs)**:

$$
\begin{aligned} a_n'(t) &= -n^2 a_n(t) + \alpha_n \implies a_n(t) = \dots \\ b_n'(t) &= -n^2 b_n(t) + \beta_n \implies b_n(t) = \dots \end{aligned}
$$

By solving this set of first-order ordinary differential equations, and combining them with the initial conditions, we can completely determine the unknown solution $ u(x,t)$.

### Geometric Perspective: Approximation and Distance in Function Spaces

When we cannot take an infinite number of terms in actual calculations, we can only truncate it to a finite sum of the first $ N $ terms, $ S_N(x)$, to approximate the original function $ f(x)$. So, how do we measure the "quality of approximation"? This prompts us to establish **the geometry of function spaces**, i.e., to define a "distance" (norm).

There are two different philosophies of "measurement of error" here:

#### 1. Uniform Norm ($ L^\infty $ norm) — The absolute extreme error

$$
\max |f(x) - S_N(x)| \iff \|\vec{a} - \vec{b}\|_\infty
$$

- **Motivation**: Focuses on the "worst-case scenario". It requires that the maximum deviation between the approximating function and the original function at every point in the domain must be as small as possible. Like the vertical distance between the two curves at the widest point in the figure.
    

#### 2. Mean Square Norm ($ L^2 $ norm) — The error of the overall energy

$$
\int_{0}^{\pi} (f(x) - S_N(x))^2 dx \iff \|\vec{a} - \vec{b}\|_2
$$

- **Motivation**: Focuses on the "overall fitting energy". It allows for relatively obvious deviations at individual points (such as the Gibbs phenomenon at discontinuities), but requires that the total sum of the squares of the errors over the entire interval (the area) is minimized.
    

**Conclusion**: The Fourier series possesses perfect **best approximation property** in the $ L^2 $ mean square norm. Geometrically, the truncated Fourier series $ S_N(x)$ is precisely the **orthogonal projection** of the original function $ f(x)$ onto the finite-dimensional subspace of low frequencies. This geometric essence forms the theoretical foundation for modern functional analysis and modern signal processing.

With the macroscopic intuition regarding "measurements" and "trigonometric basis" from before, the core task of this stage is to **rigorously establish the inner product structure of the function space**. By introducing the inner product, the function space is endowed with a geometric structure, which allows us to precisely extract every coefficient of the Fourier series using "orthogonality," just as we do with finite-dimensional Euclidean space.

### Constructing the Space of Square-Integrable Functions $ V $

In order for the measurement of "overall energy error" to be mathematically valid, we must first define a rigorous function space. This space cannot contain pathological functions that diverge to infinity.

We define the space $ V $ as the **space of square-integrable functions** on the interval $[-\pi, \pi]$ (which is the precursor to the $ L^2 $ space):

$$
V = \left\{ f(x) : [-\pi, \pi] \to \mathbb{R} \;\middle|\; \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f(x)|^2 dx < +\infty \right\}
$$

For $ V $ to constitute a qualified linear vector space, it must be closed under addition and scalar multiplication. We verify this using basic properties:

1. **Contains the zero element**: The function identically equal to $ 0 $ clearly belongs to $ V $.
    
2. **Closure under scalar multiplication**: If $ f \in V $, then for any real number $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, $\lambda f \in V $.
    
3. **Closure under addition**: If $ f, g \in V $, then their sum $ f+g \in V $.
    
    > **Motivation and Mathematical Support**: Why is the sum of two square-integrable functions still square-integrable? We can guarantee this using simple algebraic inequalities and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:
    > 
    >

$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f+g|^2 dx = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (f^2 + g^2 + 2fg) dx \le \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f^2 dx + \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g^2 dx + 2\sqrt{\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f^2 dx \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g^2 dx} < +\infty
$$

> 
    > Where the basic product inequality $\int fg dx \le \sqrt{\int f^2 dx \int g^2 dx}$ is utilized.
    

### Geometricization of Function Space: Definition of the Inner Product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle $

After having a linear space, in order to introduce the concepts of "angle" and "projection," we need to define an **inner product** on $ V $.

For any $ f, g \in V $, we define its inner product form as the weighted integral over the full period:

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x) dx
$$

This definition must strictly satisfy the three core axioms of an inner product:

1. **Linearity / Symmetry**: $\langle f, g \rangle = \langle g, f \rangle $
    
2. **Positive Definiteness**: $\langle f, f \rangle \ge 0 $ and $\langle f, f \rangle = 0 \iff f \equiv 0 $> _Note: In the sense of Lebesgue integration, $ f \equiv 0 $ denotes that $ f $ is almost everywhere zero. If two functions satisfy $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f-g|^2 dx = 0 $, geometrically we consider them to be "the same vector" (i.e., $ f=g $)._
    

**Geometric Definition of Orthogonality**: With the inner product, we can define the perpendicular relationship between functions. If the inner product of two functions is zero, i.e.:

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = 0
$$

Then the functions $ f $ and $ g $ are said to be **orthogonal**.

### Verification of Standard Orthogonality of Trigonometric Basis

Now we shift our attention back to the basis space formed by constants and trigonometric functions:

$$
V = \text{span}\left\{ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \cos(x), \sin(x), \dots, \cos(nx), \sin(nx), \dots \right\}
$$

_(Note: For subsequent normalization, the constant 1 is corrected to $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, so that the norm of all basis vectors is uniform.)_

Our goal is to prove that this set of basis functions is a set of **orthonormal basis**, meaning it satisfies the Kronecker product property:

$$
\langle \varphi_i, \varphi_j \rangle = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1, & i=j \\ 0, & i \neq j \end{cases}
$$

Using the classic trigonometric product-to-sum and sum-to-product formulas, we can verify its orthogonality and normalization in the inner product definition one by one:

#### 1. Orthogonality of Different Types of Functions (Sine and Cosine)

For any positive integer $ m, n $, sine and cosine are always orthogonal over a period:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos(mx)\sin(nx) dx = 0, \quad \forall m, n
$$

#### 2. Orthogonality of Same Type, Different Frequency Functions (Unequal Frequencies)

When $ m \neq n $, waveforms of different frequencies are mutually independent:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos(mx)\cos(nx) dx = 0 \quad (m \neq n)
$$

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin(mx)\sin(nx) dx = 0 \quad (m \neq n)
$$

#### 3. Normalization of Same Type, Same Frequency Functions (Equal Frequencies)

When $ m = n \ge 1 $, we integrate using the double angle formula (such as $\cos^2(nx) = \frac{1+\cos(2nx)}{2}$):

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \cos(mx)\cos(nx) dx = 1 \quad (m = n)
$$

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin(mx)\sin(nx) dx = 1 \quad (m = n)
$$

#### 4. Special Verification for the Constant Term Basis

The integral of the constant term with any sine or cosine wave over a full period is 0 (because the mean value of sine and cosine is 0):

$$
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1 \cdot \cos(nx) dx = 0; \quad \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1 \cdot \sin(nx) dx = 0 \quad (n \ge 1)
$$

And the inner product of the constant basis itself is:

$$
\langle 1, 1 \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} 1^2 dx = \frac{2\pi}{\pi} = 2
$$

This is why when writing the Fourier series, the constant term is often written as $\frac{a_0}{2}$—due to the geometric motivation because the squared norm of the constant $ 1 $ is $ 2 $. To maintain formal consistency with the inner product extraction form of the trigonometric terms whose norm is $ 1 $, a corresponding mathematical correction is needed.

With this perfect set of orthonormal basis functions, extracting the Fourier coefficients for an arbitrary function $ f(x)$ becomes straightforward: one just needs to take the inner product of $ f(x)$ with the corresponding basis vector $\cos(nx)$ or $\sin(nx)$, which directly "extracts" the coefficients $ a_n $ and $ b_n $, just like projecting coordinates in linear algebra.

After establishing the orthonormality of the trigonometric basis and the space's inner product, the final core task is to **rigorously derive the Fourier coefficients using orthogonal projection**, and to specify the **convergence condition (Dirichlet condition)** for the function to be representable by a Fourier series. This completes the closed loop from the linear algebra framework to the convergence of mathematical analysis.

### Geometric Projection: Precise Derivation of Fourier Coefficients

In linear algebra, given a set of orthonormal basis functions $\{\varphi_i\}$, any vector $ f $ can be represented as:

$$
f = \sum_{i=1}^{N} c_i \varphi_i
$$

Using orthonormality $\langle \varphi_i, \varphi_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$, we take the inner product of both sides of the equation with $\varphi_i $, which directly "extracts" the combination coefficients:

$$
c_i = \langle f, \varphi_i \rangle
$$

Now, we completely translate this conclusion to the space of square-integrable functions $ V $. Since the basis of the space is:

$$
\left\{ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, \cos(x), \sin(x), \dots, \cos(nx), \sin(nx), \dots \right\}
$$

We assume the function $ f(x)$ expands as:

$$
f(x) \approx \tilde{a}_0 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx) \right]
$$

#### 1. Extracting the cosine coefficient $ a_n $ and the sine coefficient $ b_n $ using the defined inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x) dx $. When $ n \ge 1 $, we directly project:

$$
a_n = \langle f, \cos(nx) \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos(nx) dx
$$

$$
b_n = \langle f, \sin(nx) \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx) dx
$$

#### 2. Extracting the constant coefficient $\tilde{a}_0 $. Similarly, we take the inner product of $ f(x)$ with the constant basis $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$:

$$
\tilde{a}_0 = \left\langle f, \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right\rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} dx
$$

To eliminate the inconvenient $\sqrt{2}$ in the terms and make the constant term formally more uniform, we redefine a coefficient $ a_0 $, such that:

$$
a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \cdot 1 dx
$$

At this point, due to $\tilde{a}_0 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)dx \right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)dx = \frac{1}{2} a_0 $, the constant term of the series naturally takes the form we are most familiar with in classical settings:

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{1}{2}a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)) = S(x)
$$

### The Boundary of Convergence: Dirichlet Condition

Writing out the series expression $ S(x)$ is merely a formal expansion. Under what conditions can this series actually converge, and is its sum $ S(x)$ strictly equal to the original function $ f(x)$? This requires introducing the **Dirichlet condition**.

For a function $ f(x)$ defined on $[-\pi, \pi]$, if the following three conditions are met, its Fourier series is convergent within the interval:

#### 1. Piecewise Continuous

The function $ f: [-\pi, \pi] \to \mathbb{R}$ has only a finite number of first-kind discontinuities in the interval.

That is, the interval can be divided into a finite number of small subintervals $(x_i, x_{i+1})$:

$$
-\pi = x_0 < x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_k = \pi
$$

such that $ f $ is continuous in each open subinterval, and the left limit $\lim_{x \to x_{i+1}^-} f(x)$ and the right limit $\lim_{x \to x_i^+} f(x)$ exist at the endpoints (they cannot diverge to infinity).

#### 2. Piecewise Monotonic

The function $ f(x)$ is monotonic in each small interval $(x_i, x_{i+1})$ (either non-increasing or non-decreasing). This means the function has only a finite number of extreme points over the entire large interval, excluding pathological functions that oscillate infinitely densely near the origin, like $ y = \sin(1/x)$.

#### 3. Piecewise Differentiable / Bounded Derivative

The function $ f(x)$ is differentiable in each small interval $(x_i, x_{i+1})$, and its derivative function $ f'(x)$ is bounded in that interval. This condition further guarantees the local smoothness of the function curve, allowing for perfect convergence at continuous points during series approximation.

When a function satisfies the above Dirichlet conditions, the convergence value of the Fourier series $ S(x)$ possesses the following extremely beautiful analytical properties:

- When $ x $ is a **continuous point** of $ f(x)$, the series converges strictly to the function value:

$$
S(x) = f(x)
$$

- When $ x $ is a **discontinuity point** of $ f(x)$, the series converges to the arithmetic mean of the left and right limits at that point:

$$
S(x) = \frac{f(x^+) + f(x^-)}{2}
$$

Thus, the Fourier series has moved from the algebraic construction of a space to the rigorous convergence application of analysis.

After clarifying the Dirichlet convergence theorem, the most effective way to consolidate the knowledge is by personally practicing the calculation of Fourier coefficients using a classic non-smooth function—the square wave—and intuitively observing the convergence behavior of the series at continuous points and jump discontinuities.

### Classic Example: Construction of the Sign Function (Square Wave)

We consider a piecewise constant function $ f(x)$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$ (which is essentially the sign function $\text{sgn}(x)$ on this interval):

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in (0, \pi) \\ 0, & x = 0, \pm\pi \\ -1, & x \in (-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

**Physical and Geometric Motivation**: This is a most typical discontinuous function. It has a sharp jump of magnitude $ 2 $ at $ x = 0 $ (jumping directly from $-1 $ to $ 1 $). We wish to approximate this everywhere "hard" square wave using the superposition of smooth trigonometric wave forms.

### Detailed Derivation of Fourier Coefficients

Using the orthogonal projection formula derived earlier, we calculate each coefficient separately.

#### 1. Parity Simplification for the Constant Term and Cosine Coefficients

Notice that the function $ f(x)$ is a standard **odd function** (satisfying $ f(-x) = -f(x)$).

- The constant basis $ 1 $ and the cosine basis $\cos(nx)$ are both **even functions**.
    
- The product of an odd function and an even function is still an odd function.
    

Based on the property of definite integrals over symmetric intervals, the integral of an odd function over $[-\pi, \pi]$ must be $ 0 $. Therefore, we can directly determine this from geometric symmetry without even performing explicit algebraic calculations:

$$
a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) dx = 0
$$

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos(nx) dx = 0, \quad \forall n \ge 1
$$

This means that **the Fourier expansion of an odd function only contains sine terms (called the Fourier sine series)**.

#### 2. Calculation of the Sine Term Coefficient $ b_n $

Since $ f(x)$ and $\sin(nx)$ are both odd functions, their product $ f(x)\sin(nx)$ becomes an **even function**. Therefore, the integral can be simplified to twice the integral over half the interval:

$$
b_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx) dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} 1 \cdot \sin(nx) dx
$$

Performing the definite integral:

$$
b_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ -\frac{1}{n} \cos(nx) \right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{2}{n\pi} \left( -\cos(n\pi) + \cos(0) \right)
$$

Due to $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n $, the above expression simplifies to:

$$
b_n = \frac{2}{n\pi} (1 - (-1)^n)
$$

#### 3. Classification Discussion of Coefficients

Analyzing the value of the factor $(1 - (-1)^n)$:

- When $ n $ is an **even number** ($ n = 2m $), then $(-1)^n = 1 $, so $ b_n = 0 $.
    
- When $ n $ is an **odd number** ($ n = 2k+1 $), then $(-1)^n = -1 $, so $ b_n = \frac{4}{n\pi}$.
    

#### 4. Series Synthesis and Dirichlet Convergence Verification

Substitute the calculated coefficients back into the Fourier series expression:

$$
f(x) \sim \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{2}{n\pi}(1 - (-1)^n) \sin(nx) = \frac{4}{\pi} \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{2k+1} \sin((2k+1)x) = S(x)
$$

Expanding the first few terms allows for a more intuitive view of the wave superposition:

$$
S(x) = \frac{4}{\pi} \left( \sin(x) + \frac{1}{3}\sin(3x) + \frac{1}{5}\sin(5x) + \dots \right)
$$

Now we use Dirichlet's theorem to rigorously test its convergence value $ S(x)$:

1. **At continuous points (like $ x \in (0, \pi)$)**:
    
    The function is piecewise smooth, and the series converges strictly to the original function value:

$$
S(x) = f(x) = 1
$$

2. **At jump discontinuities (like $ x = 0 $)**:
    
    According to Dirichlet's condition, the series should converge to the average of the left and right limits. We verify this:
    
    - Left limit: $ f(0^-) = -1 $
        
    - Right limit: $ f(0^+) = 1 $
        
    - Theoretical convergence value: $\frac{f(0^-) + f(0^+)}{2} = \frac{-1 + 1}{2} = 0 $ Directly substituting into the series expression: when $ x = 0 $, every term $\sin((2k+1) \cdot 0) = 0 $, so the sum of the entire series is $ S(0) = 0 $. The theoretical prediction and the actual sum of the series **perfectly match**.
    

This perfect example not only demonstrates how to strip out the coefficients of specific frequencies through orthogonality, but also proves that even for functions with discontinuities, the Fourier series can land accurately and smoothly at the discontinuity point in a graceful and extremely beautiful manner, without losing overall energy.

After deeply understanding the special properties of even and odd functions, when faced with more general asymmetric piecewise signals, we need to calculate both the cosine and sine coefficients completely and simultaneously. By evaluating the Fourier series of such functions at specific points (like points of discontinuity), we can not only re-verify Dirichlet's convergence theorem but also unexpectedly obtain a powerful algebraic tool for solving classic number series (like variations of the Basel problem) in number theory.

### Fourier Series Expansion of a Complex Piecewise Function

Consider the following asymmetric piecewise function $ f(x)$:

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} x, & x \in [0, \pi) \\ 0, & x = \pi \\ -\pi, & x \in (-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

**Motivational Analysis**: This function is constant within $(-\pi, 0)$ and linear within $[0, \pi)$; it is neither an odd function nor an even function. Therefore, its Fourier coefficients must simultaneously contain the DC component $ a_0 $, the cosine component $ a_n $, and the sine component $ b_n $. We need to perform definite integration piecewise over the interval.

#### 1. Calculation of the Constant Term Coefficient $ a_0 $

$$
\begin{aligned} a_0 &= \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \left[ \int_{-\pi}^{0} (-\pi) dx + \int_{0}^{\pi} x dx \right] \\ &= \frac{1}{\pi} \left[ -\pi^2 + \frac{\pi^2}{2} \right] = -\frac{\pi}{2} \end{aligned}
$$

#### 2. Calculation of the Cosine Term Coefficient $ a_n $ ($ n \ge 1 $)

$$
\begin{aligned} a_n &= \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos(nx) dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \left[ \int_{-\pi}^{0} (-\pi)\cos(nx) dx + \int_{0}^{\pi} x\cos(nx) dx \right] \end{aligned}
$$

- Left half: $\int_{-\pi}^{0} (-\pi)\cos(nx) dx = -\pi \left[ \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} \right]_{-\pi}^{0} = 0 $
    
- Right half (using integration by parts):

$$
\int_{0}^{\pi} x\cos(nx) dx = \left[ \frac{x\sin(nx)}{n} \right]_{0}^{\pi} - \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} dx = 0 - \left[ -\frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2} \right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2}
$$

Combining gives:

$$
a_n = \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2\pi}
$$

#### 3. Calculation of the Sine Term Coefficient $ b_n $ ($ n \ge 1 $)

$$
\begin{aligned} b_n &= \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx) dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \left[ \int_{-\pi}^{0} (-\pi)\sin(nx) dx + \int_{0}^{\pi} x\sin(nx) dx \right] \end{aligned}
$$

- Left half: $-\pi \left[ -\frac{\cos(nx)}{n} \right]_{-\pi}^{0} = \frac{\pi}{n}(1 - (-1)^n)$
    
- Right half: $\left[ -\frac{x\cos(nx)}{n} \right]_{0}^{\pi} - \int_{0}^{\pi} \left(-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}\right) dx = -\frac{\pi(-1)^n}{n}$ Combining the terms and canceling $\pi $:

$$
b_n = \frac{1}{n}(1 - 2(-1)^n)
$$

#### 4. Synthesis of the Series Form

Substitute the coefficients into the standard expression (note that the DC term is $\frac{a_0}{2}$):

$$
f(x) \sim -\frac{\pi}{4} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2\pi} \cos(nx) + \frac{1}{n}(1 - 2(-1)^n) \sin(nx) \right] = S(x)
$$

#### Testing Dirichlet's theorem at the jump discontinuity $ x=0 $

This function is discontinuous at $ x=0 $, so we use Dirichlet's theorem to calculate the theoretical convergence value of the series at this point:

- Left limit: $ f(0^-) = -\pi $
    
- Right limit: $ f(0^+) = 0 $
    
- Theoretical convergence value:

$$
S(0) = \frac{f(0^-) + f(0^+)}{2} = \frac{-\pi + 0}{2} = -\frac{\pi}{2}
$$

###  Applying extension: Solving the sum of classic number series

**Core Motivation**: If we substitute the Fourier series expression directly at $ x=0 $ and set it equal to the theoretical convergence value $-\frac{\pi}{2}$, we can establish a magical bridge between function spaces and pure number series.

When $ x=0 $, all sine terms $\sin(0)=0 $, cosine terms $\cos(0)=1 $, and the series expression simplify to:

$$
S(0) = -\frac{\pi}{4} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2\pi}
$$

Setting it equal to the theoretical value $-\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$
-\frac{\pi}{2} = -\frac{\pi}{4} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2\pi} \implies -\frac{\pi}{4} = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{(-1)^n - 1}{n^2\pi}
$$

#### 1. Solving the sum of the reciprocals of even integers

Analyzing the numerator term $((-1)^n - 1)$:

- When $ n $ is even, $ 1 - 1 = 0 $, the term vanishes;
    
- When $ n $ is odd (let $ n = 2k+1 $), $-1 - 1 = -2 $.
    

Therefore, only the odd terms remain in the series:

$$
-\frac{\pi}{4} = \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{-2}{(2k+1)^2\pi} \implies -\frac{\pi}{4} = -\frac{2}{\pi} \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}
$$

Multiplying both sides by $-\frac{\pi}{2}$, we precisely derive:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}
$$

#### 2. Generalized Derivation: Solving the Basel Problem $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ We can further utilize the summation result of the odd terms above to derive the sum of the reciprocals of the squares of all positive integers (i.e., the famous $\zeta(2)$).

Let the total sum be $ X = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}$. We can split it into two parts: the even terms and the odd terms:

$$
X = \sum_{n=\text{Even function}} \frac{1}{n^2} + \sum_{n=\text{Odd function}} \frac{1}{n^2} = \sum_{m=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{(2m)^2} + \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}
$$

Extracting the even terms gives $\frac{1}{4}$:

$$
X = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{m=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{m^2} + \frac{\pi^2}{8} \implies X = \frac{1}{4}X + \frac{\pi^2}{8}
$$

Rearranging and simplifying:

$$
\frac{3}{4}X = \frac{\pi^2}{8} \implies X = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}
$$

This shows us the extraordinary byproduct of Fourier series: the method of orthogonal decomposition of function spaces, originally belonging to advanced calculus, can actually solve extremely difficult analytic summation problems in pure number theory with remarkable elegance.

In the previous case, we dealt with a function that was discontinuous at the origin. Now, to study waveforms with greater smoothness, we introduce a quadratic parabolic splicing signal that is **continuous but not everywhere differentiable**. Through this, we can not only observe the pattern of how the decay rate of the Fourier coefficients changes with frequency but also tackle higher-order number series summation problems (such as $\sum \frac{1}{n^4}$) in a cascading manner.

### I. Construction of Piecewise Continuous Function (Parabolic Waveform)

Consider the piecewise quadratic function $ f(x)$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$:

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} (x-\pi)^2, & x \in [0, \pi] \\ (x+\pi)^2, & x \in [-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

**Physical and Geometric Motivation**:

1. **Symmetry (Even/Odd)**: Due to $ f(-x) = f(x)$, this function is a standard **even function**. Its value at $ x=0 $ is $\pi^2 $, and it smoothly transitions to $ 0 $ at $ x=\pm\pi $.
    
2. **Smoothness Enhancement**: Compared to the square wave and sawtooth wave before, this function is **completely continuous** both in the interior of the interval and at the periodic boundaries (due to $ f(-\pi)=f(\pi)=0 $). This continuity directly maps to the decay rate of its Fourier coefficients.
    

### II. Detailed Derivation of Fourier Coefficients

Due to the symmetry property of the even function, we can greatly simplify the inner integral operations.

#### 1. Sine Component Coefficient $ b_n $ Since $ f(x)$ is an even function and $\sin(nx)$ is an odd function, their product integrated over a symmetric interval is always zero:

$$
b_n = 0, \quad \forall n \ge 1
$$

This means that **the Fourier expansion of an even function only contains the DC component and cosine components (called the Fourier Cosine Series)**.

#### 2. DC Term Coefficient $ a_0 $

Using twice the integral over half the interval:

$$
a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (x-\pi)^2 dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{(x-\pi)^3}{3} \right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ 0 - \frac{(-\pi)^3}{3} \right] = \frac{2}{3}\pi^2
$$

#### 3. Cosine Term Coefficient $ a_n $ ($ n \ge 1 $)

Similarly, expanding using the even function property and applying **integration by parts** twice:

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (x-\pi)^2 \cos(nx) dx
$$

- First integration by parts:

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ (x-\pi)^2 \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} \right]_{0}^{\pi} - \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} 2(x-\pi) \frac{\sin(nx)}{n} dx
$$

Since $\sin(n\pi) = \sin(0) = 0 $, the first term completely vanishes. Simplifying the remainder:

$$
a_n = -\frac{4}{n\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (x-\pi) \sin(nx) dx
$$

- Second integration by parts:

$$
a_n = -\frac{4}{n\pi} \left[ (x-\pi) \frac{-\cos(nx)}{n} \right]_{0}^{\pi} + \frac{4}{n\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} 1 \cdot \frac{-\cos(nx)}{n} dx
$$

Because $\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos(nx) dx = 0 $ (when $ n \ge 1 $), the integral term of the last term again vanishes. We only need to substitute the boundary evaluation of the previous term:

$$
a_n = \frac{4}{n^2\pi} \left[ (x-\pi)\cos(nx) \right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{4}{n^2\pi} \left[ 0 - (-\pi)\cos(0) \right] = \frac{4}{n^2}
$$

#### 4. Synthesis of the Series Form

Substituting the coefficients into the standard Fourier Cosine Series formula (note that the DC term needs to be divided by 2):

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{1}{3}\pi^2 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{4}{n^2} \cos(nx) = S(x)
$$

> **Mathematical Essence Observation**: Comparing with the square wave coefficients from previous sections (proportional to $\frac{1}{n}$), the coefficients of the current quadratic wave are proportional to $\frac{1}{n^2}$. In analysis, this indicates that **the smoother the original function (the better its continuity), the faster its high-frequency harmonic coefficients decay**.

### III. Dirichlet Convergence Verification and Series Summation

Since the function is continuous everywhere in its domain, by Dirichlet's Theorem, there must be $ S(x) = f(x)$ at any point $ x $.

#### 1. Returning to the Basel Problem (Verification at $ x=0 $ point)

We evaluate the series at $ x = 0 $. At this point, $ f(0) = \pi^2 $, and $\cos(0) = 1 $:

$$
\pi^2 = \frac{1}{3}\pi^2 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{4}{n^2} \implies \frac{2}{3}\pi^2 = 4 \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}
$$

Dividing both sides by $ 4 $, we once again beautifully derive the classic answer to the Basel problem:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}
$$

#### 2. Exploring the Unknown: Solving the Sum of Reciprocals of Fourth Powers $\sum \frac{1}{n^4}$ **Higher-Order Motivation**: How can we extract a number series with a denominator of $ n^4 $ from the Fourier series of this continuous function we just constructed?

This requires **Parseval’s identity**, the Pythagorean theorem extended to a space with an infinite orthonormal basis. The total energy of the vector, its squared norm, is the sum of the energies of its components.

According to Parseval's Identity in inner product spaces:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} |f(x)|^2 dx = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

_(Note: Since our inner product definition includes the prefix $\frac{1}{\pi}$, the left side's form matches accordingly.)_

We substitute the coefficients of this problem, $ a_0 = \frac{2}{3}\pi^2 $, $ a_n = \frac{4}{n^2}$, uniformly:

- Left-hand side (Total Energy):

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f(x)|^2 dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (x-\pi)^4 dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{(x-\pi)^5}{5} \right]_{0}^{\pi} = \frac{2}{5}\pi^4
$$

- Right-hand side (Sum of Component Squares):

$$
\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} a_n^2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\pi^2\right)^2 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left(\frac{4}{n^2}\right)^2 = \frac{2}{9}\pi^4 + 16 \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^4}
$$

Equating both sides:

$$
\frac{2}{5}\pi^4 = \frac{2}{9}\pi^4 + 16 \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^4}
$$

$$
\left(\frac{2}{5} - \frac{2}{9}\right)\pi^4 = 16 \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^4} \implies \frac{8}{45}\pi^4 = 16 \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^4}
$$

Dividing both sides by 16, we finally and definitively solve the sum of the fourth power series:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^4} = \frac{\pi^4}{90}
$$

From the limitations of algebraic approximation, to the geometricization in inner product spaces, to solving operator equations, and finally to cracking number theory series with Parseval's energy conservation—here, the magnificent panorama of Fourier series in analysis and geometry is completely closed.

### From Standard Period to Arbitrary Period Coordinate Scaling

In the classic Fourier series, we are usually accustomed to discussing functions with a period of $ 2\pi $ on the interval $[-\pi, \pi]$. However, physical signals or wave equations in reality are often defined on an arbitrary interval $[-L, L]$, with a period of $ 2L $. Our primary motivation is to **transform the unknown arbitrary period problem into the known standard $ 2\pi $ period problem through a translation and scaling variable substitution.**

Let the function $ f(x)$ be defined on $[-L, L]$, with a period of $ 2L $. To map it to the $[-\pi, \pi]$ space with $ t $ as the variable, we need to construct a linear mapping.

Since when $ x = L $, we need $ t = \pi $, the ratio between the two should satisfy:

$$
\frac{t}{x} = \frac{\pi}{L}
$$

From this, we introduce the frequency parameter $\omega = \frac{\pi}{L}$. Thus, the coordinate transformation relationship can be elegantly written as:

$$
t = \omega x, \quad x = \frac{t}{\omega}
$$

Through this bridge, we construct an auxiliary function $ g(t)$, such that its behavior on $ t \in [-\pi, \pi]$ perfectly replicates the behavior of $ f(x)$ on $ x \in [-L, L]$:

$$
g(t) = f\left(\frac{t}{\omega}\right) = f\left(\frac{L}{\pi}t\right)
$$

Since the period of $ f(x)$ is $ 2L $, it is easy to verify that the period of $ g(t)$ is exactly $ 2\pi $. Now, we can directly write out the Fourier series expansion of $ g(t)$ on the standard interval:

$$
g(t) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left( a_n \cos(nt) + b_n \sin(nt) \right)
$$

This is just a tool in our standard space. To return to reality, we need to inversely substitute $ t = \omega x $ back into the equation. Through this substitution process, we have completed the rigorous derivation from the standard period to the Fourier series for an arbitrary period:

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left( a_n \cos(n\omega x) + b_n \sin(n\omega x) \right)
$$

Where, $\omega = \frac{\pi}{L}$. This shows that a function of arbitrary period can still be decomposed into a linear combination of a set of mutually orthogonal trigonometric basis functions $\{\cos(n\omega x), \sin(n\omega x)\}$.

### The Gift of Symmetry: Coefficient Simplification for Odd and Even Functions

After obtaining the expansion for an arbitrary period, the next motivation is **how to utilize the inherent geometric symmetry of the function itself to reduce the redundancy of integral calculations**. Integrals geometrically represent area. If the trigonometric basis functions align with the function itself in some odd or even symmetry, the integral's basis will undergo an interesting collapse.

#### 1. Odd Functions (Inherently Odd)

When $ f(x)$ is an odd function, it is symmetric about the origin, satisfying $ f(-x) = -f(x)$.

Since the cosine function $\cos(n\omega x)$ is an even function, the product of an odd function and an even function is still an odd function. Over a symmetric interval $[-L, L]$, the integral of an odd function must be zero. Therefore, all cosine coefficients vanish:

$$
a_n = 0 \quad (\forall n \ge 0)
$$

And since the sine function $\sin(n\omega x)$ is also an odd function, the product of two odd functions becomes an even function. The integral of an even function over a symmetric interval equals twice the integral over the positive half-interval. Therefore, the calculation of the sine coefficient $ b_n $ can be simplified from the entire interval to the half-interval:

$$
b_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \sin(n\omega x) \, dx = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(x) \sin(n\omega x) \, dx
$$

#### 2. Even Functions (Inherently Even)

Similarly, when $ f(x)$ is an even function, it is symmetric about the $ y $ axis, satisfying $ f(-x) = f(x)$.

In this case, the product of an even function and an odd function $\sin(n\omega x)$ becomes an odd function, and its integral over a symmetric interval is zero. This means all sine coefficients vanish:

$$
b_n = 0 \quad (\forall n \ge 1)
$$

And since the cosine part is the product of two even functions, it remains even, and its integral can also be halved:

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(x) \cos(n\omega x) \, dx
$$

### Translation of Domain and Period Extension

After discussing the symmetric interval $[-L, L]$, we often encounter another common domain form: $[0, 2L]$. Although the interval changes, its essential span (period) remains $ 2L $. **The motivation here is to prove: due to the cyclic properties of trigonometric and periodic functions, as long as the length of the integration interval equals one full period, the specific starting point of the integral does not affect the final result of the Fourier coefficients.**

For a function $ f(x) \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on $ x \in [0, 2L]$, the structure of its Fourier series is completely consistent with the previous section:

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left( a_n \cos(n\omega x) + b_n \sin(n\omega x) \right)
$$

The only difference lies in the integration interval for the Fourier coefficients. When we extract the coefficients directly on the function's domain, the integration interval is $[0, 2L]$:

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{0}^{2L} f(x) \cos(n\omega x) \, dx
$$

To gain an intuitive understanding, we can introduce a seamless geometric perspective:

From the perspective of geometric extension, shifting the graph of the function on $[-\pi, \pi]$ to the right, or directly studying its behavior on $[0, 2\pi]$, the area enclosed remains completely conserved in total. Therefore, we can obtain an important equivalent identity in the linear space:

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(t) \cos(nt) \, dt = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} f(t) \cos(nt) \, dt
$$

This equivalence of intervals grants great freedom to Fourier analysis. Whether choosing an interval symmetric about the origin to utilize odd/even symmetry for calculation simplification, or choosing an interval starting from zero to follow the physical time axis of the signal, its mathematical essence is completely unified within the framework of the Fourier transform.

### Rigorous Substitution Derivation for Arbitrary Period Coefficients

After mapping the function $ f(x)$ with a period of $ 2L $ to the auxiliary function $ g(t)$ with the standard period $ 2\pi $, we perform a rigorous substitution integration derivation for the arbitrary period coefficients $ a_n $ using the known standard Fourier coefficient formulas.

Let the transformation relationship be $ t = \omega x = \frac{\pi}{L}x $, then the differential relationship is $ dt = \frac{\pi}{L}dx $.

The standard series coefficients are defined on $ t \in [-\pi, \pi]$:

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g(t) \cos(nt) \, dt
$$

Our motivation is to restore the integration variable from the physically abstract $ t $ space back to the original $ x $ space. Substituting $ g(t) = f\left(\frac{L}{\pi}t\right) = f(x)$ and the differential relationship $ dt $, the integration interval correspondingly transforms from $[-\pi, \pi]$ to $ x \in [-L, L]$:

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \cos\left(n \frac{\pi}{L} x\right) \cdot \frac{\pi}{L} \, dx
$$

By eliminating the constant term $\pi $, we strictly obtain the coefficient extraction formula for an arbitrary period:

$$
a_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^{L} f(x) \cos(n\omega x) \, dx
$$

Similarly, for a function defined on the positively half-period shifted interval $[0, 2L]$, the integration range symmetrically shifts to $[0, 2L]$. The mathematical essence behind this is: **over one full period length, the inner product (i.e., the integral value) between the basis vector and the function is translation invariant with respect to the starting point of the interval.**

### Typical Case Analysis: Spectral Decomposition of an Absolute Value Sine Wave

To materialize the application of symmetry, we introduce a classic case with extremely high engineering value: **the full-wave rectified waveform (absolute value sine wave)**.

Let the function $ f(x) = |\sin(\omega x)|$, where $\omega = \frac{\pi}{L}$, defined on $ x \in [-L, L]$.

#### 1. Odd/Even Prediction (Motivation: Eliminating Redundant Calculation)

Since $ f(-x) = |\sin(-\omega x)| = |-\sin(\omega x)| = |\sin(\omega x)| = f(x)$, this function is a standard even function.

Geometrically, the graph is perfectly symmetric about the $ y $ axis. Therefore, its inner product with any odd basis function $\sin(n\omega x)$ must cancel out over the symmetric interval:

$$
b_n = 0 \quad (\forall n \ge 1)
$$

This means its expansion will only contain the DC component and cosine harmonics.

#### 2. Halving the Interval Using Symmetry

Since both the function and the cosine basis are even functions, their product integrated over $[-L, L]$ can be simplified to twice the integral over the positive half-interval:

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} |\sin(\omega x)| \cos(n\omega x) \, dx
$$

Over the interval $ x \in [0, L]$, $\omega x \in [0, \pi]$, where $\sin(\omega x) \ge 0 $, thus the absolute value sign can be directly removed. The integral formula transforms to:

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{L}x\right) \cos\left(n\frac{\pi}{L}x\right) \, dx
$$

Using the product-to-sum formula, we can convert the product of two orthogonal basis functions into a combination of cosines of a single frequency, thereby easily finding each coefficient. This shows that even seemingly complex non-linear absolute value waveforms significantly reduce calculation difficulty after simplification by odd/even symmetry.

### Half-Interval Expansion: The Art of Boundary Extension Choice

In practical applications (such as boundary value problems for the heat equation or wave equation), the function $ f(x)$ is often initially defined only on a half-interval $[0, L]$. Since this interval lacks periodicity, we cannot directly perform a Fourier decomposition on it.

Our core motivation is: **artificially extending a function defined on $[0, L]$ to $[-L, L]$ to give it periodicity and symmetry. The method of extension will cause drastic changes in the function's behavior outside the boundary, resulting in entirely different series representations.**

For the $ f(x) \to \mathbb{R}$ defined on $[0, L]$, there are the following three mainstream extension strategies:

```
1. Direct periodic extension (by L with period)  -> containing sine and cosine,boundary may be discontinuous
2. Odd extension (by 2L with period)     -> containing only sine terms (Sine series),forcing the boundary to zero
3. Even extension (by 2L with period)     -> containing only cosine terms (Cosine series),smooth transition at the boundary
```

#### Strategy One: Direct Extension with a Period of $ L $

The most intuitive idea is to simply replicate $[0, L]$ as the minimum positive period.

At this point, the physical period of the function becomes $ L $ (equivalent to $ 2L' = L \implies L' = \frac{L}{2}$ in the formula above), and the fundamental frequency is raised to $\omega = \frac{2\pi}{L}$.

Its series takes the form of a complete body containing both sine and cosine terms:

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left( a_n \cos\left(\frac{2n\pi}{L}x\right) + b_n \sin\left(\frac{2n\pi}{L}x\right) \right)
$$

- **Motivation Flaw:** If $ f(0) \neq f(L)$, this forced extension leads to a first-kind jump discontinuity at the boundary point $ x = L, 2L, \dots $, which in turn causes the **Gibbs phenomenon** during the convergence of the series.
    

#### Strategy Two: Odd Extension (Constructing a Sine Series)

To eliminate the cosine terms, or to satisfy the physical constraint that $ f(0)=f(L)=0 $ at the boundary, we can construct an odd function $ F^o(x)$:

$$
F^o(x) = \begin{cases} f(x), & x \in (0, L) \\ 0, & x = 0, L \\ -f(-x), & x \in (-L, 0) \end{cases}
$$

Subsequently, we extend $ F^o(x)$ across the entire axis with a period of $ 2L $. Since $ F^o(x)$ is a strict odd function, all cosine coefficients naturally collapse:

$$
a_n^o = 0
$$

The coefficients are entirely inherited by the sine terms:

$$
b_n^o = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(x) \sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}x\right) \, dx
$$

At this point, the function is fully expanded into a pure sine series: $ f(x) \sim \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} b_n^o \sin\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}x\right)$.

#### Strategy Three: Even Extension (Constructing a Cosine Series)

If the physical boundary condition requires the derivative to be zero (such as an adiabatic boundary), or to achieve better smoothness at the boundary, we can construct an even function $ F^e(x)$:

$$
F^e(x) = \begin{cases} f(x), & x \in [0, L] \\ f(-x), & x \in [-L, 0) \end{cases}
$$

And extend it across the entire axis with a period of $ 2L $. Based on the properties of even functions, all sine coefficients are washed out:

$$
b_n^e = 0
$$

The cosine coefficients are extracted twice over the half-interval:

$$
a_n^e = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(x) \cos\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}x\right) \, dx
$$

Thus, the function is equivalently expressed as a pure cosine series over the interval $[0, L]$:

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{a_0^e}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} a_n^e \cos\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}x\right)
$$

**Summary and Convergence Insight:**

Although the mathematical values of these three extension methods are completely equivalent on the original interval $ x \in [0, L]$, because their geometric forms constructed outside the interval are different, their rate of convergence and continuity at the boundary points are fundamentally different. This degree of freedom in boundary extension is the most core power of Fourier analysis when solving boundary value problems.

### Algebraic View of Function Spaces: Collapse of Basis for Half-Interval Extension

When we take the function $ f(x)$ defined on $[0, L]$ and push it to the entire axis through three different extension methods (direct periodic, odd, and even extension), in an algebraic sense, this essentially corresponds to **projecting the same function onto three different Hilbert Space subspaces**.

Next, we will stand from the perspective of linear algebra to systematically sort out the orthogonal bases corresponding to these three extension strategies:

### I. Function Space Bases Corresponding to the Three Extensions

#### 1. Strategy One: Direct Periodic Extension Space $ V_L $ When we treat $[0, L]$ as an independent full period for extension, the resulting function space is denoted as $ V_L $. In this space, the fundamental frequency of the harmonics becomes $\frac{2\pi}{L}$.

Its corresponding standard orthogonal basis is:

$$
\left\{ 1, \, \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}x\right), \, \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{L}x\right), \, \dots, \, \cos\left(n\frac{2\pi}{L}x\right), \, \sin\left(n\frac{2\pi}{L}x\right), \, \dots \right\}
$$

#### 2. Strategy Two: Odd Extension Space $ V_1 $ (Pure Sine Space)

If we perform an odd extension on the function, the physical period of the function on $[-L, L]$ becomes $ 2L $. Due to odd symmetry, all cosine terms are washed out, and the space collapses into a pure sine subspace, denoted as $ V_1 $.

It is easy to see that this space is $ 2L $ the space of periodic functions $ V_{2L}$ a subspace of ($ V_1 \subset V_{2L}$).

$$
\left\{ \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{L}x\right), \, \sin\left(2\frac{\pi}{L}x\right), \, \dots, \, \sin\left(n\frac{\pi}{L}x\right), \, \dots \right\}
$$

From the perspective of set theory, although they are all infinite-dimensional spaces, the 'size' (dimension) of their bases satisfies $\dim(V_1) = |\mathbb{N}|$ in an algebraic sense.

#### 3. Strategy Three: Even Extension Space $ V_2 $ (Pure Cosine Space)

Similarly, the space corresponding to even extension is the pure cosine subspace, denoted as $ V_2 $. It is also contained within the total space with period $ 2L $ ($ V_2 \subset V_{2L}$).

Its corresponding basis is:

$$
\left\{ 1, \, \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{L}x\right), \, \cos\left(2\frac{\pi}{L}x\right), \, \dots, \, \cos\left(n\frac{\pi}{L}x\right), \, \dots \right\}
$$

Similarly, its space dimension is $\dim(V_2) = \dim(V_1) = |\mathbb{N}|$.

### II. Deep Orthogonal Direct Sum Decomposition of the Sine Harmonic Space

To further deconstruct the pure sine space $ V_1 $, we can further algebraically partition the sine basis according to the **parity** of the harmonic frequency. The motivation for this decomposition is to study finer wave symmetries (for example, symmetry not only about the origin but also about half-period points).

We split $ V_1 $ into two smaller subspaces:

- **Even Harmonic Sine Space $ V_{1, e}$**: Spanned by all sine basis vectors with even multiples.

$$
V_{1, e} = \text{span}\left\{ \sin\left(2n\frac{\pi}{L}x\right) \right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
$$

- **Odd Harmonic Sine Space $ V_{1, o}$**: Spanned by all sine basis vectors with odd multiples.

$$
V_{1, o} = \text{span}\left\{ \sin\left((2m+1)\frac{\pi}{L}x\right) \right\}_{m=0}^{\infty}
$$

Because sine waves of different frequencies are strictly orthogonal over the interval, the intersection of these two subspaces only contains the zero element ($ V_{1, e} \cap V_{1, o} = \{0\}$). Thus, the entire pure sine space can be perfectly represented as the **orthogonal direct sum** of these two subspaces:

$$
V_1 = V_{1, e} \oplus V_{1, o}
$$

### III. Algebraic Transformation and Coupling Relationship of Space Bases

In linear algebra, when a vector is represented in different bases, we can perform a coordinate transformation using a transition matrix. What is displayed in the bottom right corner of the blackboard is precisely the coupling and transformation motivation between different harmonic subspaces.

If we want to project or transform an element from a certain subspace $\begin{pmatrix} V_1 \\ V_2 \end{pmatrix}$ into another space composed of a mixture of specific even and odd harmonics, we can establish a connection using the product-to-sum and difference formulas for trigonometric functions, as well as the orthogonal projection matrix. For example, consider the coupling when an odd sine wave is multiplied by the most basic single-frequency cosine/sine term:

$$
\sin\left(2n\frac{\pi}{L}x\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{L}x\right), \quad \sin\left(2n\frac{\pi}{L}x\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{L}x\right)
$$

Using the trigonometric identity to expand:

$$
\sin\left(2n\frac{\pi}{L}x\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{L}x\right) = \frac{1}{2}\left[\sin\left((2n+1)\frac{\pi}{L}x\right) + \sin\left((2n-1)\frac{\pi}{L}x\right)\right]
$$

This algebraic fact shows that: **The basis vector in the even harmonic sine space, when subjected to a fundamental cosine perturbation (modulation), has its spectrum completely linearly transformed into the basis vector in the odd harmonic sine space $ V_{1, o}$.**

This transformation relationship between subspaces is not only very elegant in the theory of linear spaces but also constitutes its most core algebraic framework in modern signal processing's "modulation/demodulation" and in the correct selection of quantum mechanical operators.

## The Motivation for Best Approximation: A Game Between Data, Physics, and Models

When facing real-world data (Data) from the physical world, we usually want to establish a mathematical model (Model) to describe it. At this point, we face a core contradiction: **the trade-off between the model's complexity (Complexity) and its predictive capability (Prediction)**.

- **Overfitting**: If we relentlessly pursue a perfect fit of the model to the known data points, using extremely complex ultra-high-dimensional functions (such as high-order polynomials or trigonometric series with too many terms), the model will track the noise in the data, leading to violent oscillations. Although the error at the known points is $ 0 $, the predictive capability outside these points will completely collapse.
    
- **Occam's Razor**: We hope that the model approximates the true data as closely as possible while maintaining a "Simple Model".
    

To quantify this "approximation," we introduce the concept of distance in an inner product space. If the true function is $ f $, and the approximating model is $ T_N $, our goal is to minimize the mean square error between them:

$$
\min \int ( \text{Data} - \text{Model} )^2 \, dx = \min \| f - T_N \|^2
$$

In a linear space, the essence of finding this minimum error model is performing an **orthogonal projection**.

## The Orthogonal Projection Theorem and Fourier Best Approximation

### 1. Construction of the Subspace

Let $ f(x)$ be the square-integrable function we want to approximate. We introduce a finite-dimensional subspace $ V_N $ generated by the first $ N $ order trigonometric functions:

$$
V_N = \text{span} \{ 1, \cos(x), \sin(x), \dots, \cos(Nx), \sin(Nx) \}
$$

Clearly, the dimension of this space is $\dim(V_N) = 2N + 1 $. In this subspace, any element (i.e., a trigonometric polynomial) can be written as:

$$
T_N(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx))
$$

### 2. Lemma: The Geometric Essence of Orthogonal Projection

For $ T_N \in V_N $ to be the **best approximation** of $ f $ in this subspace, geometric intuition tells us: the error vector $ f - T_N $, pointing from the vector $ f $ to the subspace $ V_N $, must be orthogonal to this subspace.

> **Lemma (Orthogonal Perpendicularity Relation)**
> 
> Let $ T_N $ be the best approximation of $ f $ in $ V_N $, then for any $ g \in V_N $, the error vector is orthogonal to the subspace:
> 
>

$$
\langle f - T_N, g \rangle = 0 \quad (\text{that is } f - T_N \perp V_N)
$$

> 
> At this point, $ T_N $ is called the orthogonal projection of $ f $ onto the subspace $ V_N $, denoted as $ T_N = P_{V_N} f $.

### 3. Rigorous Verification of the Theorem

We verify whether the Fourier coefficients naturally satisfy this orthogonality by calculating the inner product. The verification for the two sets of basis vectors is symmetric; here we take the basis vector $\cos(nx)$ (where $ n=0,1,\dots,N $) as an example:

Using the linearity property of the inner product, we split the error term:

$$
\langle f - T_N, \cos(nx) \rangle = \langle f, \cos(nx) \rangle - \langle T_N, \cos(nx) \rangle
$$

Due to the standard orthogonality of the trigonometric function system (ignoring the normalization constant $\pi $ for simplicity, focusing on coefficient coupling), after substituting the expansion of $ T_N $, the inner product of all terms except those with the same frequency is $ 0 $:

$$
\langle T_N, \cos(nx) \rangle = \left\langle \dots + a_n\cos(nx) + \dots, \cos(nx) \right\rangle = a_n \cdot \pi
$$

And in the definition of the Fourier coefficients, there is originally $ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \langle f, \cos(nx) \rangle $. Therefore:

$$
\langle f - T_N, \cos(nx) \rangle = a_n \cdot \pi - a_n \cdot \pi = 0
$$

**Conclusion**: The truncated term of the first $ N $ terms of the Fourier series, $ T_N $, is exactly the orthogonal projection of $ f $ onto the finite-dimensional subspace $ V_N $. This geometrically guarantees that **the finite-term truncation of the Fourier series is the best approximation in the mean square error sense**.

## Core Theorem: Best Approximation and Uniqueness in the Mean Square Error Sense

Let $ f $ be a square-integrable function defined on $[-\pi, \pi]$ (i.e., $ f \in L^2[-\pi, \pi]$), whose $ L^2 $ norm is defined as $\|g\|_{L^2}^2 = \langle g, g \rangle = \int_{-\pi}^\pi |g(x)|^2 dx $.

Let $ V_N $ be the $(2N+1)$ dimensional subspace generated by the first $ N $ order trigonometric functions, and $ T_N \in V_N $ be the Fourier series truncation of the first $ N $ terms of $ f $.

> **Best Approximation Theorem**
> 
> 1. **Existence (Best Approximation)**: For any element in the subspace $ g \in V_N $, the Fourier truncation $ T_N $ can minimize the mean square error to the greatest extent:
>     
>

$$
\|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 = \min_{g \in V_N} \|f - g\|_{L^2}^2
$$

>     
> 2. **Uniqueness**: If there exists an element $ g \in V_N $ in the subspace that achieves the same minimum error, i.e., $\|f - T_N\|_{L^2} = \|f - g\|_{L^2}$, then this element must be identical to the Fourier truncation:
>     
>

$$
\Longrightarrow T_N = g \quad (\text{at } V \text{ in})
$$

>     

## Core Derivation: Orthogonal Decomposition and Error Identity

### 1. Cleverly Introducing an Intermediate Term (Construction Motivation)

To compare the superiority of an arbitrary approximating function $ g $ with the best approximating term $ T_N $, we need to calculate the arbitrary error $\|f - g\|_{L^2}^2 $. Direct calculation cannot reveal the special status of $ T_N $, so we cleverly **insert $ T_N $ as a bridge** inside the inner product:

$$
\|f - g\|_{L^2}^2 = \langle f - g, f - g \rangle = \langle (f - T_N) + (T_N - g), (f - T_N) + (T_N - g) \rangle
$$

### 2. Utilizing the Bilinear Expansion of the Inner Product

According to the distributive property of the inner product, the above expression is fully expanded into four terms, and like terms are combined:

$$
\|f - g\|_{L^2}^2 = \langle f - T_N, f - T_N \rangle + \langle T_N - g, T_N - g \rangle + 2\langle f - T_N, T_N - g \rangle
$$

Using the definition of the norm, the first two terms can be written as the square of the norm:

$$
\|f - g\|_{L^2}^2 = \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 + \|T_N - g\|_{L^2}^2 + 2\langle f - T_N, T_N - g \rangle
$$

### 3. Elimination of Cross Terms (Algebraic Manifestation of Geometric Projection)

Now consider the cross term $ 2\langle f - T_N, T_N - g \rangle $.

- According to the orthogonal projection lemma mentioned earlier, the error vector $ f - T_N $ is orthogonal to the entire subspace $ V_N $.
    
- Because $ T_N \in V_N $ and $ g \in V_N $, by the closure property of the subspace under subtraction, their difference vector must also belong to this subspace: $(T_N - g) \in V_N $.
    

Therefore, by orthogonality, this inner product is strictly $ 0 $:

$$
\langle f - T_N, \underline{T_N - g}_{\in V_N} \rangle = 0
$$

After successfully "canceling out" the cross terms, we obtain the extremely elegant **error identity**:

$$
\|f - g\|_{L^2}^2 = \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 + \|T_N - g\|_{L^2}^2
$$

## Logical Closed-Loop Proof of the Theorem

With the above identity, the two parts of the theorem become self-evident:

1. **Proof of Best Approximation**: 
    
Since the square of the norm is non-negative, i.e., $\|T_N - g\|_{L^2}^2 \ge 0 $, after removing this term from the identity, the following inequality must hold:

$$
\|f - g\|_{L^2}^2 \ge \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2
$$

This shows that the error brought by any function that is not a Fourier truncation $ g $ is greater than or equal to the error brought by $ T_N $. That is, $ T_N $ achieves the minimum error.
    
2. **Proof of Uniqueness**:
    
If there exists a $ g $ such that equality holds, i.e., $\|f - g\|_{L^2}^2 = \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 $, substitute it into the identity:

$$
\|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 = \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 + \|T_N - g\|_{L^2}^2 \Longrightarrow \|T_N - g\|_{L^2}^2 = 0
$$

By the positive definiteness of the norm (only the zero vector has a norm of $ 0 $), this implies:

$$
T_N - g = 0 \Longrightarrow T_N = g
$$

**Final Conclusion**:In the linear space $ V_N $, the Fourier series truncation term $ T_N $ is the **unique best approximation function that minimizes the mean square error**. Any other attempt (changing coefficients or combinations) will inevitably introduce an additional positive error term $\|T_N - g\|_{L^2}^2 $, leading to a worse approximation effect.

## Core Theorem: Bessel's Inequality

In the space of square-integrable functions $ L^2[-\pi, \pi]$, let the truncation term of the first $ N $ terms of the Fourier series corresponding to $ f(x)$ be $ T_N(x)$. The macroscopic geometric form of Bessel's inequality is extremely simple; it shows that the energy (square of the norm) of a subspace projection never exceeds the total energy of the original function:

$$
\|T_N\|_{L^2}^2 \le \|f\|_{L^2}^2, \quad \forall N
$$

## Detailed Algebraic Derivation Process

### 1. Inner Product Representation of the Expansion

According to the definition of the standard norm inner product in the $ L^2 $ space, we write both sides of the macroscopic inequality above as integrals over the domain $[-\pi, \pi]$.

- The total energy of the original function on the right side is:

$$
\|f\|_{L^2}^2 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx
$$

- The energy of the truncation term $ T_N(x)$ on the left side is:

$$
\|T_N\|_{L^2}^2 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi (T_N(x))^2 \, dx
$$

### 2. Substituting the Fourier Truncation Polynomial

Substitute $ T_N(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx))$ into the left-side integral:

$$
\|T_N\|_{L^2}^2 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \left( \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N \left(a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)\right) \right)^2 \, dx
$$

### 3. Simplification using Orthogonality of Trigonometric Functions

When we fully expand the square of the series above, many cross terms are generated (such as $\cos(nx)\sin(mx)$, $\cos(nx)\cos(mx)$, where $ n \neq m $). Due to the **standard orthogonality** of the trigonometric system on $[-\pi, \pi]$, the integral result of all cross terms of different frequencies is strictly $ 0 $.

Finally, only the squared terms of each component remain in the integral, namely:

- Constant term: $\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \left(\frac{a_0}{2}\right)^2 \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \cdot \frac{a_0^2}{4} \cdot 2\pi = \frac{a_0^2}{2}$
    
- Cosine term: $\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi a_n^2 \cos^2(nx) \, dx = a_n^2 $
    
- Sine term: $\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi b_n^2 \sin^2(nx) \, dx = b_n^2 $
    

Summing these non-zero integral terms, we obtain the discrete algebraic exact expression for the energy of the truncation term:

$$
\|T_N\|_{L^2}^2 = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

### 4. Deriving Bessel's Inequality

Since it is geometrically known that $\|T_N\|_{L^2}^2 \le \|f\|_{L^2}^2 $, we substitute the simplified algebraic result directly, thus deriving the **classical algebraic form of Bessel's Inequality**:

$$
\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n^2 + b_n^2) \le \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx
$$

## Core Deduction: Convergence of the Fourier Series

> **Deduction (Series Convergence)**
> 
> If $ f \in L^2[-\pi, \pi]$ (i.e., the integral of the square of $ f $ is finite), when the number of truncation terms is $ N \to \infty $, the infinite series formed by the Fourier coefficients must **converge**:
> 
>

$$
\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2) < +\infty
$$

**Motivation and Logical Support**:

Because for any finite $ N $, the partial sum of the positive term series $\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n^2 + b_n^2)$ is firmly bounded (monotonically bounded) by a constant independent of $ N $ (i.e., the integral of the original function $\|f\|_{L^2}^2 $). According to the **Monotone Convergence Theorem** in mathematical analysis, this positive term series must converge at $ N \to \infty $.

This fundamentally guarantees that as long as the original function has finite energy, the sum of the energies of its decomposed harmonic components will never diverge.

## Transition to the Limit: Parseval's Identity

In the macroscopic limit, we further examine the energy distribution of the series:

When $ N \to \infty $, if the trigonometric basis satisfies **completeness** in the space (meaning there is no non-zero vector in the space that is orthogonal to all basis vectors, and the approximation error is $\|f - T_N\|_{L^2} \to 0 $), then the 

$$
\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx
$$

> **Parseval's Theorem**
> 
> If the trigonometric basis is complete, then for any square-integrable function $ f \in V $, the norm of the error vector must converge to $ 0 $ in the infinite-dimensional limit:
> 
>

$$
\lim_{N \to \infty} \|f - S_N\|_{L^2}^2 = 0, \quad \forall f \in V
$$

> 
> At this point, the equality holds strictly, and the energy is completely conserved:
> 
>

$$
\|S\|_{L^2}^2 = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx
$$

### 1. Core Motivation and Geometric Starting Point

In a linear space, according to the algebraic identity of orthogonal projection, for any number of terms $ N $, the total energy (square of the norm) of the function $ f $ can be strictly decomposed into the sum of the "projection term energy" and the "residual term energy" (i.e., the high-dimensional Pythagorean theorem):

$$
\|f\|_{L^2}^2 = \|T_N\|_{L^2}^2 + \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2
$$

Substituting the specific algebraic form, the equation above is equivalent to:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx = \left( \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n^2 + b_n^2) \right) + \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2
$$

### 2. Limit Approximation and Completeness Assumption (Crossing the Limit)

Now, we let the number of truncated terms approach infinity, i.e., $ N \to \infty $.

At this point, we need to introduce the **Completeness** (or **mean square convergence** in approximation) of the trigonometric system in a Hilbert space. For any square-integrable function $ f \in L^2[-\pi, \pi]$, as the harmonic components gradually increase, the residual term (i.e., the approximation error) must converge to $ 0 $ in the norm sense of $ L^2 $:

$$
\lim_{N \to \infty} \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 = 0
$$

The intuitive geometric meaning of this property is: when the dimension of the basis grows to infinity, the subspace $ V_N $ eventually "fills" the entire function space, causing the distance between the original vector $ f $ and its projection onto the subspace to shrink to $ 0 $.

### 3. Eliminating the Residual Term in the Limit

We take the limit of $ N \to \infty $ on both sides of the energy decomposition identity:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx = \lim_{N \to \infty} \left( \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^N (a_n^2 + b_n^2) \right) + \lim_{N \to \infty} \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}^2
$$

Since the limit of the residual term on the right side is $ 0 $, this term is completely eliminated. The sum of the finite terms in the middle naturally transforms into an infinite series:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

This is **Parseval's Identity**.

### Physical Reconstruction of the Conclusion

- **Bessel's Inequality** reveals the **energy truncation property of partial signals**: the sum of energies obtained by fitting a signal with a finite number of frequency components can only approach, but never exceed, the total energy of the original signal.
    
- **Parseval's Identity** is the **law of energy conservation in the full frequency domain**: when all the harmonic energies from frequency $ 1 $ to $+\infty $ are summed up, the total energy in the frequency domain (left side) is strictly equal to the total energy in the time domain (right side); no energy is lost during the space transformation.

This note focuses on the **proof of the completeness condition for Parseval's Identity**, and we will perform a specific calculation using a classic square wave function. Finally, we will witness how a pure time-domain energy integral transforms into an infinite series in the frequency domain, unexpectedly leading to the summation of a famous number theory series.

## Classic Case Derivation: Fourier Series of a Square Wave Function and Energy Integral

### 1. Establishing the Problem and Motivation

To verify the power of Parseval's Identity in actual calculations, we introduce a representative discontinuous periodic function—the **standard square wave function** $ f(x)$. We not only need to calculate its frequency domain coefficients but also to solve a high-difficulty mathematical analysis series sum through the energy equivalence between the time and frequency domains.

Let $ f(x)$ be defined on $[-\pi, \pi]$, with the specific piecewise form:

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in (0, \pi) \\ 0, & x = 0, \pi \\ -1, & x \in (-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

### 2. Algebraic Calculation of Fourier Coefficients

Since $ f(x)$ is a strict **odd function** (symmetric about the origin), due to the symmetry of the integral, all its cosine projection components must vanish:

$$
a_n = 0, \quad \forall n \ge 0
$$

Next, we calculate the projection value of the sine component $ b_n $:

$$
b_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x) \sin(nx) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^\pi 1 \cdot \sin(nx) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ -\frac{\cos(nx)}{n} \right]_{0}^\pi = \frac{2}{n\pi} (1 - (-1)^n)
$$

Analyzing the even-odd coupling property of $ 1 - (-1)^n $:

- When $ n $ is even ($ n=2k $), then $ b_{2k} = 0 $.
    
- When $ n $ is odd ($ n=2k+1 $), then $ b_{2k+1} = \frac{4}{(2k+1)\pi}$.
    

### 3. Integration of Total Time-Domain Energy

We directly perform a definite integral on $ f(x)^2 $ in the time domain to obtain the constant total energy of this signal:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)^2 \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \left( \int_{-\pi}^0 (-1)^2 \, dx + \int_{0}^\pi 1^2 \, dx \right) = \frac{1}{\pi} (\pi + \pi) = 2
$$

## The Leap to Series Summation using Parseval's Identity

### Motivation for the Steps

The sum of the squared magnitudes of the Fourier coefficients of $ b_n $ represents the total energy in the frequency domain. According to Parseval's theorem, the total frequency domain energy must be strictly equal to the total time domain energy (i.e., equal to $ 2 $). By establishing this equation, we can transform the complex trigonometric series energy into a pure number theory series.

### Detailed Derivation

Substitute $ a_n = 0 $ and $ b_n $ into the left side of Parseval's Identity (the frequency domain series part):

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} b_n^2 = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ \frac{2}{n\pi} (1 - (-1)^n) \right]^2 = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{4}{n^2\pi^2} (1 - (-1)^n)^2
$$

Since only the odd terms ($ n = 2k+1 $) retain non-zero values, we have $(1 - (-1)^n)^2 = 2^2 = 4 $. We replace the summation index with the odd index $ k $ (from $ 0 $ to $+\infty $):

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} b_n^2 = \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{4}{(2k+1)^2\pi^2} \cdot 4 = \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{16}{\pi^2 \cdot (2k+1)^2}
$$

Equating the total frequency domain energy to the time domain energy $ 2 $:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{16}{\pi^2 \cdot (2k+1)^2} = 2
$$

Multiplying both sides by $\frac{\pi^2}{16}$ and rearranging to isolate the unknown infinite series:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} = 2 \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{16} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}
$$

## Ultimate Conclusion and Mathematical Beauty

Through the bridge of Parseval's Identity, we successfully prove the sum of the squares of the reciprocals of all odd integers:

$$
1 + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{25} + \frac{1}{49} + \dots = \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{(2k+1)^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8}
$$

### Critical Evaluation

- **Strength (Confidence Rating: Very High)**: Although the square wave function has a discontinuous jump point at $ x=0, \pm\pi $ (which causes Gibbs phenomenon oscillations in the local frequency domain), it is strictly square-integrable in the $ L^2 $ space. Parseval's Identity perfectly bypasses the mathematical challenge of local discontinuities from the perspective of **macro integral energy**, directly locking down the exact sum of the series without the need for complex limit proofs.
    
- **Physical Significance Reconstruction**: This derivation demonstrates the geometric power of Hilbert space—a simple square wave signal in the time domain has its energy decomposed in the frequency domain into a superposition of energies from infinitely many odd harmonics. The conservation relationship not only proves completeness but also unexpectedly becomes a powerful algebraic tool for solving number theory series summation (a variation of the Basel problem).

## Fourier Expansion of Odd Functions and the Basel Problem

Facing a reciprocal square sum like $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}$, it is usually arduous to derive it directly from number theory or discrete algebra. We need a "bridge" to transform the discrete integers $ n $ into a frequency within some analytic structure. Fourier expansion is an excellent choice because the coefficients of its basis functions often naturally generate structures like $ 1/n $ or $ 1/n^2 $ after integration.

To ensure that only sine terms appear in the series (which is convenient for using integration to produce even-denominator denominators), we first construct an odd function $ f(x)$ with a period of $ 2\pi $, defined in the initial period as:

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} \pi - x, & x \in (0, \pi) \\ 0, & x = 0, \pi \\ -\pi - x, & x \in (-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

Since $ f(x)$ is a strict odd function, its DC component and cosine coefficients in the Fourier coefficients directly vanish, i.e., $ a_0 = 0 $ and $ a_n = 0 $. We only need to focus on extracting the sine coefficient $ b_n $:

$$
b_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x) \sin(nx) \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (\pi - x) \sin(nx) \, dx
$$

Using integration by parts to strip away the polynomial structure of $ x $. Let $ u = \pi - x \implies du = -dx $, and $ dv = \sin(nx)dx \implies v = -\frac{1}{n}\cos(nx)$:

$$
b_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ -\frac{1}{n}(\pi - x)\cos(nx) \right]_0^{\pi} - \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{1}{n}\cos(nx) \, dx
$$

Substituting the limits, the first term contributes $ 0 $ at $\pi $, and $\frac{2}{n}$ at $ 0 $; while the second term integrates the cosine function over the interval $[0, \pi]$, resulting clearly in $ 0 $. Thus, we obtain an extremely clean coefficient:

$$
b_n = \frac{2}{n}
$$

At this point, if we directly write out the Fourier series of $ f(x)$, we get $\sum \frac{2}{n}\sin(nx)$. However, this can only be used to calculate the alternating series. To obtain the pure $\frac{1}{n^2}$, we need to introduce **Parseval's Identity**. Its physical essence is that the finite energy of a signal in the time domain equals the sum of its energy in the frequency domain. For an odd function, this identity manifests as:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f(x)|^2 \, dx = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} b_n^2
$$

We substitute the known $ b_n $ and $ f(x)$ into this identity. The energy integral on the left side can be simplified due to symmetry to a one-sided integral:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left(\frac{2}{n}\right)^2 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (\pi - x)^2 \, dx
$$

Direct integration of the right side:

$$
\frac{2}{\pi} \left[ -\frac{1}{3}(\pi - x)^3 \right]_0^{\pi} = \frac{2}{3\pi} \cdot \pi^3 = \frac{2}{3}\pi^2
$$

Expanding and rearranging both sides:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{4}{n^2} = \frac{2}{3}\pi^2 \implies \mathbf{\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}}
$$

Thus, through the energy conservation of an odd function, the Basel problem is perfectly proven.

## Symmetric Jump of Even Functions and Higher-Order Series

The previous attempt allowed us to taste success: integrating the square of a function made the denominator $ n $ into $ n^2 $. If we need to calculate higher-order series, such as $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^4}$, an intuitive motivation is to raise the function to a higher even power, such that the Fourier coefficients inherently carry the characteristic of $ 1/n^2 $ at the basic stage. This way, after squaring using Parseval's theorem, we can naturally generate $ 1/n^4 $.

To this end, we reconstruct an even function $ f(x)$ (which can be visualized as a smooth, symmetric "hill" shape):

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} (\pi - x)^2, & x \in [0, \pi] \\ (\pi + x)^2, & x \in [-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

Since it is an even function, the sine term coefficient is $ b_n = 0 $. We focus on calculating the DC component $ a_0 $ and the cosine term coefficient $ a_n $. First, the mean (DC component):

$$
a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (\pi - x)^2 \, dx = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{\pi^3}{3} = \frac{2}{3}\pi^2
$$

Next, we calculate the AC coefficient $ a_n $. Again using integration by parts, we perform two successive peelings on $(\pi - x)^2 \cos(nx)$:

$$
a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (\pi - x)^2 \cos(nx) \, dx
$$

After the first integration by parts, the power term vanishes at the boundaries, leaving a sine integral; the second integration by parts converts the sine back to cosine and accumulates $ n^2 $ in the denominator. After careful substitution of the limits, we obtain:

$$
a_n = \frac{4}{n^2}
$$

This matches our expectation: the basic form of the Fourier coefficient is already $\frac{1}{n^2}$. Now, we invoke the general Parseval's theorem again. Note that this time it includes the DC component, and its full form is:

$$
\frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} a_n^2 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f(x)|^2 \, dx
$$

Substitute the obtained coefficients into the function on both the left and right sides:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}\pi^2\right)^2 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left(\frac{4}{n^2}\right)^2 = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\pi} (\pi - x)^4 \, dx
$$

Expand and calculate the definite integral for both sides separately:

$$
\frac{2}{9}\pi^4 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{16}{n^4} = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ -\frac{1}{5}(\pi - x)^5 \right]_0^{\pi} = \frac{2}{5}\pi^4
$$

Now, we move terms and separate the core series:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{16}{n^4} = \frac{2}{5}\pi^4 - \frac{2}{9}\pi^4 = \frac{8}{45}\pi^4
$$

Dividing both sides by $ 16 $, the classic conclusion for higher-order Euler series elegantly emerges:

$$
\mathbf{\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^4} = \frac{\pi^4}{90}}
$$

## Deep Thinking and Extrapolation

This tool, based on geometric symmetry and analytic continuation, demonstrates astonishing power. By constructing a series based on the boundary of a specific polynomial, we are effectively establishing a general paradigm:

- **More Advanced Methods for Solving Series**: If we need to solve $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^6}$, its dynamics will compel us to find a function whose Fourier coefficient is $ a_n = \frac{C}{n^3}$ . Since each integration by parts will introduce a $ n $, this means we need to construct an original function such that after completing three integrations by parts, it can completely eliminate $ x $ a polynomial structure—that is, we need to start from a function of a cubic power $ f(x) = (\pi - x)^3 $(and perform appropriate odd/even extensions), and through Parseval's theorem, we can obtain a discrete sum with a denominator of $ n^6 $ .
    

Fourier analysis thus transforms complex number theory series into an analytical game that can be linearly extrapolated.

## Inner Product Space and Parseval's Theorem in Fourier Series

In classical calculus, Parseval's theorem is often regarded as a purely calculus integral identity. However, if we place it in the framework of a Hilbert Space, the essence of this theorem is nothing more than the natural extension of the "Pythagorean theorem" or "the square of the vector norm equals the sum of the squares of its components" in Euclidean space to infinite-dimensional function spaces.

### 1. Structure Construction: Defining the Inner Product of a Function Space

Let $ V $ be the space of square-integrable functions defined on the periodic interval $[-\pi, \pi]$. To measure the "angle" and "projection" between two functions, we define the inner product as follows:

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x) \, dx
$$

Under this inner product definition, the trigonometric function family $\{1/\sqrt{2}, \cos(nx), \sin(nx)\}$ constitutes an **orthonormal basis** for this space.

Assume the Fourier expansions of the function $ f, g \in V $ are:

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx))
$$

$$
g(x) \sim \frac{c_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (c_n \cos(nx) + d_n \sin(nx))
$$

By the properties of an orthonormal basis, the inner product of two functions can be directly converted into the discrete algebraic sum of their Fourier coefficients (analogous to the coordinate inner product of geometric vectors):

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \frac{a_0 c_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n c_n + b_n d_n)
$$

### 2. Motivation and Derivation: From Linear Expansion to Parseval's Theorem

Our goal is to prove that when $ f = g $, the aforementioned inner product relationship perfectly matches energy conservation. To derive this conclusion rigorously from an algebraic standpoint, we examine the inner product of the vector (function) and $ f+g $.

Based on the **bilinearity** and **symmetry** of the inner product, we have:

$$
\langle f+g, f+g \rangle = \langle f, f \rangle + \langle g, g \rangle + 2\langle f, g \rangle
$$

This is a purely algebraic identity. Next, we directly substitute the corresponding Fourier coefficients of $ f+g $ into the coefficient representation of the inner product. Clearly, the coefficients of $ f+g $ are $(a_n + c_n)$ and $(b_n + d_n)$. Thus, the left side can be written as:

$$
\langle f+g, f+g \rangle = \frac{(a_0 + c_0)^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ (a_n + c_n)^2 + (b_n + d_n)^2 \right]
$$

Similarly, the right side's $\langle f, f \rangle $ and $\langle g, g \rangle $ can also be written in the corresponding sum of squares form:

$$
\langle f, f \rangle = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

$$
\langle g, g \rangle = \frac{c_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (c_n^2 + d_n^2)
$$

Substituting these explicit expressions into the initial algebraic identity, and expanding the perfect square on the left side:

$$
\frac{a_0^2 + 2a_0c_0 + c_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + 2a_ncc_n + c_n^2 + b_n^2 + 2b_nd_n + d_n^2)
$$

After canceling the identical self-product term $\frac{a_0^2}{2}, \frac{c_0^2}{2}, a_n^2, c_n^2, b_n^2, d_n^2 $ on both ends, and extracting the coefficient $ 2 $ from the cross terms $\frac{2a_0c_0}{2}$ and $ 2a_nc_n, 2b_nd_n $, it perfectly aligns with the $ 2\langle f, g \rangle $ on the right side:

$$
\frac{(a_n+c_n)^2 - a_n^2 - c_n^2}{2} = a_n c_n
$$

When we set $ g = f $ in the space (i.e., the coordinates completely coincide), all $ c_n = a_n $ and $ d_n = b_n $, the above relationship naturally degenerates to:

$$
\langle f, f \rangle = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

Restoring it to the specific integral expression, thus completing the general proof of **Parseval's Theorem**:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f(x)|^2 \, dx = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

## The Algebraic Nature of Term-by-Term Integration of Fourier Series: Constructing a Test Function

In analysis, term-by-term integration of an infinite series over a local interval $[\alpha, \beta]$, $\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \, dx $, usually requires a rigorous proof of the series' **uniform convergence**. However, by utilizing the inner product framework just established, we can bypass the cumbersome analysis of convergence by **constructing a specific "test function"**, elegantly transforming the complex interval integration operation into an algebraic projection within the inner product space.

### 1. Motivation: How to express local interval integration using the inner product?

The expression we wish to calculate is:

$$
\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \, dx \quad (\text{where } [ \alpha, \beta ] \subset [-\pi, \pi])
$$

Notice that the existing inner product tool $\langle f, g \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x) \, dx $ is integrated over the entire $[-\pi, \pi]$ interval. To "crop" the integral over the entire interval into an integral over a local interval, we need to construct an indicator function $ g(x)$ that is $ 1 $ inside $[\alpha, \beta]$ and $ 0 $ outside the interval as the test function.

### 2. Constructing the Test Function $ g(x)$ Define the test function $ g(x)$ as:

$$
g(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in [\alpha, \beta] \\ 0, & x \in [-\pi, \pi] \setminus [\alpha, \beta] \end{cases}
$$

At this point, due to the truncation property of $ g(x)$ outside the interval, the inner product of $ f $ and $ g $ undergoes the following transformation:

$$
\langle f, g \rangle = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)g(x) \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \cdot 1 \, dx
$$

That is:

$$
\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \, dx = \pi \langle f, g \rangle
$$

### 3. Calculating the Fourier Coordinates of the Test Function $ g(x)$

Since the local integral is equivalent to $\pi \langle f, g \rangle $, and the inner product can be obtained from the algebraic sum of the Fourier coefficients of two functions, we now only need to calculate the Fourier coefficients of this artificially constructed $ g(x)$, which are $ c_0, c_n, d_n $:

- **DC Component $ c_0 $**:

$$
c_0 = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g(x) \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} 1 \, dx = \frac{\beta - \alpha}{\pi}
$$

- **Cosine Coefficient $ c_n $**:

$$
c_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g(x) \cos(nx) \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \cos(nx) \, dx
$$

- **Sine Coefficient $ d_n $**:

$$
d_n = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} g(x) \sin(nx) \, dx = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \sin(nx) \, dx
$$

### 4. Algebraic Synthesis and Final Derivation

Now, we substitute the known coefficients $(a_0, a_n, b_n)$ of $ f $ and the new coefficients $(c_0, c_n, d_n)$ of $ g $ into the inner product coordinate formula:

$$
\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \, dx = \langle f, g \rangle = \frac{a_0 c_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n c_n + b_n d_n)
$$

Multiply both sides of the equation by $\pi $, and substitute the original integral definition of $ c_0, c_n, d_n $ into the right side as is:

$$
\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \, dx = \frac{a_0}{2} \cdot \pi c_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left( a_n \cdot \pi c_n + b_n \cdot \pi d_n \right)
$$

$$
\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \, dx = \frac{a_0}{2} \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} 1 \, dx + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left( a_n \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \cos(nx) \, dx + b_n \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \sin(nx) \, dx \right)
$$

Using the linearity property of integrals, move the summation sign and the coefficients inside the integral sign:

$$
\int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x) \, dx = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \left\{ \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx) \right] \right\} \, dx
$$

**Conclusion**: This final form is formally the **term-by-term integral** of the Fourier series expression for $ f(x)$ over the interval $[\alpha, \beta]$.

By constructing the test function $ g(x)$ and translating it via the inner product space, we proved that: **As long as the function $ f(x)$ is square-integrable, its Fourier series converges via term-by-term integration not only completely legitimately but also naturally.** This ingenious algebraic manipulation avoids the stringent requirement of smoothness when differentiating term-by-term, and it reveals the power of modern functional analysis in classical harmonic analysis.

Previously, we proved the excellent property of term-by-term integration, but when facing the fundamental question of whether the "partial sum of the Fourier series $ S_n(x)$ converges to the original function $ f(x)$," we need to move from **local convergence (pointwise convergence)** to **global convergence (uniform convergence)**.

Next, we will rigorously prove the theorem of uniform convergence of the Fourier series by introducing the energy constraint of the derivative, utilizing the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

## Core Theorem and Motivation

In mathematical analysis, if a function series can **converge uniformly (Uniform Convergence)**, it means it preserves the continuity of the original function and guarantees the interchangeability of the limit with the integral/derivative signs. For the Fourier series $ S_n(x) \rightrightarrows f(x)$ to converge uniformly over the entire interval, merely requiring $ f(x)$ to be continuous is not enough; we need to impose a restriction on the "roughness" of the function.

### Theorem Statement

Let $ f \in V $ be a continuous function defined on $[-\pi, \pi]$ (and satisfying the periodic boundary condition $ f(-\pi) = f(\pi)$). If its derivative $ f'$ is **square-integrable** (i.e., $ f' \in V $, $\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f'(x)|^2 \, dx < +\infty $), then the Fourier series $ S_n(x)$ of $ f(x)$ **converges uniformly** on $[-\pi, \pi]$ to $ f(x)$.

### Algebraic Motivation

To prove that a trigonometric series converges uniformly, the most powerful tool is the **Weierstrass M-test**. If we can prove that the sum of the absolute values of the coefficients converges, that is:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (|a_n| + |b_n|) < +\infty
$$

Then, due to $|\a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)| \le |a_n| + |b_n|$, the uniform convergence of the series can be directly declared. Therefore, our core algebraic motivation is to **bound and amplify the coefficient series $\sum (|a_n| + |b_n|)$**.

## Rigorous Derivation Process

### 1. Fourier Coefficient Expression of the Derivative

Assume the Fourier expansion of $ f(x)$ is:

$$
f(x) \sim \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx))
$$

We perform a formal term-by-term differentiation of $ f(x)$, and let the Fourier coefficients of $ f'(x)$ be $ a_n^{(1)}$ and $ b_n^{(1)}$:

$$
f'(x) \sim \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (-n a_n \sin(nx) + n b_n \cos(nx))
$$

By direct comparison or integration by parts, we can precisely establish the algebraic link between the coefficients of $ f'$ and the coefficients of $ f $:

$$
a_n^{(1)} = n b_n, \quad b_n^{(1)} = -n a_n
$$

### 2. Introducing Parseval's Identity for the Derivative

Since the theorem provides the condition $ f' \in V $ (square-integrable), we can apply Parseval's theorem to $ f'$:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ (a_n^{(1)})^2 + (b_n^{(1)})^2 \right] = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f'(x)|^2 \, dx < +\infty
$$

Substituting the link relationship from Step 1 into the left side, we get:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left[ (n b_n)^2 + (-n a_n)^2 \right] = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} n^2 (a_n^2 + b_n^2) = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f'(x)|^2 \, dx
$$

This equation is very crucial; it shows that as long as the energy of the derivative is finite, the square sum of the Fourier coefficients of the original function $(a_n, b_n)$, after multiplying by the weight $ n $, is still convergent.

### 3. Decoupling using the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

Now we return to the core motivation: calculating $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (|a_n| + |b_n|)$.

Directly observing this series makes it difficult to establish a connection with the higher-order $ n^2(a_n^2 + b_n^2)$. To this end, we introduce a class of classic algebraic decoupling techniques—**multiplying by $ n $ and dividing by $ n $ simultaneously** in each term:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (|a_n| + |b_n|) = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n} \cdot \left( n|a_n| + n|b_n| \right)
$$

In an infinite-dimensional space, using the discrete form of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality ($\sum x_y \le \sqrt{\sum x^2} \sqrt{\sum y^2}$), we decompose the above series into the square root of the product of two independent series:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n} \left( n|a_n| + n|b_n| \right) \le \left( \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} \right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot \left( \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (n|a_n| + n|b_n|)^2 \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
$$

### 4. Arithmetic Amplification and Final Constraint

We apply the basic two-variable mean inequality or a simple amplification relationship $(|u|+|v|)^2 \le 2(u^2 + v^2)$ to the second term on the right side (the part containing the coefficients):

$$
(n|a_n| + n|b_n|)^2 = n^2 (|a_n| + |b_n|)^2 \le 2n^2 (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

Substituting this back into the inequality:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (|a_n| + |b_n|) \le \left( \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} \right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot \left( \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} 2n^2 (a_n^2 + b_n^2) \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
$$

At this point, a miracle occurs:

- The first term is the classic Basel problem, whose convergence value we know to be $\frac{\pi^2}{6}$.
    
- The $\sum n^2(a_n^2+b_n^2)$ inside the second term is exactly the definite integral calculated in Step 2 using Parseval's theorem for $ f'$.
    

Substituting these two parts, which are determined finite values:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (|a_n| + |b_n|) \le \left( \frac{\pi^2}{6} \right)^{\frac{1}{2}} \cdot \left( \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |f'(x)|^2 \, dx \right)^{\frac{1}{2}} < +\infty
$$

Since both terms on the right side are determined finite real numbers, we successfully proved the **absolute convergence** of $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} (|a_n| + |b_n|)$.

## Conclusion and Weierstrass Test

Let $ U_n(x) = a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)$ be each term of the Fourier series. Clearly:

$$
|U_n(x)| \le |a_n| \cdot 1 + |b_n| \cdot 1 = |a_n| + |b_n| = M_n
$$

Because we just proved that the constant series $\sum M_n = \sum (|a_n| + |b_n|)$ converges, by the **Weierstrass M-test**, the partial sum of the Fourier series $ S_n(x)$ must **converge uniformly** over the entire interval $[-\pi, \pi]$.

Combining this with the previously established property of term-by-term integration or the continuity theorem, we finally confirm that the limit function of its uniform convergence can only be the original function $ f(x)$ itself:

$$
S_n(x) \rightrightarrows f(x), \quad x \in [-\pi, \pi]
$$

### In-depth Summary

This proof reveals a profound duality in mathematical analysis between "smoothness" and "rate of convergence": **the finite energy of the derivative (i.e., the sufficient smoothness of the original function) directly constrains its Fourier coefficients to decay rapidly, faster than $ 1/n $.** This rapid decay suppresses the oscillation of the trigonometric basis functions, ultimately weaving a perfect result of uniform convergence.

## A rigorous proof of Parseval's identity (from the perspective of approximation and density)

In our previous discussion, we know that Parseval's identity is algebraically equivalent to the mean square convergence of the Fourier partial sum $ S_n $ in the $ L^2 $ norm, meaning the goal is to prove:

$$
\lim_{N \to \infty} \|f - S_N\|_{L^2}^2 = 0 \quad (\forall f \in V)
$$

However, since functions in the $ L^2 $ space may have discontinuities or even be very rough, we cannot directly assert that their Fourier series converge uniformly. The core motivation here is to utilize **space density (Density)**: to approximate the rough function with sufficiently smooth "smooth functions," and then use the best approximation property to pass the error to the partial sum.

### 1. Motivation and Key Lemma: Error Control via $ C^1 $ Functions

Based on conclusions from measure theory and functional analysis, the space of continuously differentiable functions $ C^1[-\pi, \pi]$ is **dense** in the space of square-integrable functions $ L^2[-\pi, \pi]$. This means that for any given rough function $ f \in V $ and any small error bound $\varepsilon > 0 $, we can always find a highly smooth benchmark test function $ g \in C^1[-\pi, \pi]$ such that the distance between them is sufficiently small:

$$
\|f - g\|_{L^2} < \varepsilon
$$

### 2. Completing Error Propagation using the Best Approximation Property

Let $ T_N $ be the Fourier partial sum (i.e., the projection) of the test function $ g(x)$ onto the space of trigonometric polynomials $ V_N $. Due to $ g \in C^1 $, which satisfies the uniform convergence theorem from the previous section, therefore when $ N \to \infty $, $ T_N \rightrightarrows g $ (converges uniformly).

Once uniform convergence is established, by the control convergence theorem, its $ L^2 $ norm naturally converges. That is, there exists a sufficiently large $ N $ such that:

$$
\|g - T_N\|_{L^2}^2 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} |g(x) - T_N(x)|^2 \, dx \le \frac{1}{\pi} \cdot 2\pi \cdot \varepsilon^2 = 2\varepsilon^2 \implies \|g - T_N\|_{L^2} \le \sqrt{2}\varepsilon
$$

Now we examine the original function $ f $ and its Fourier partial sum $ S_N $ distance. According to the **Best Approximation Property** of orthogonal projection in a Hilbert space,$ S_N $ is a subspace $ V_N $ distance $ f $ the closest element in the space. Therefore, the distance of any other $ V_N $ trigonometric polynomial (including $ g $ the partial sum $ T_N $) to $ f $ is necessarily greater than or equal to $ S_N $ To $ f $ the distance of:

$$
\|f - S_N\|_{L^2} \le \|f - T_N\|_{L^2}
$$

By using the **Triangle Inequality** to forcibly split the right side, inserting the test function $ g $ as a bridge:

$$
\|f - T_N\|_{L^2} = \|(f - g) + (g - T_N)\|_{L^2} \le \|f - g\|_{L^2} + \|g - T_N\|_{L^2}
$$

Substituting the error control range from the first two steps:

$$
\|f - S_N\|_{L^2} \le \varepsilon + \sqrt{2}\varepsilon = (1+\sqrt{2})\varepsilon = C \cdot \varepsilon
$$

Since $\varepsilon $ is arbitrary, when $ N \to \infty $, the error bound is approximated to $ 0 $. Thus, through the approximation chain of "$ L^2 \leftarrow C^1 \leftarrow \text{uniformly convergent } T_N $," we rigorously prove that Parseval's identity holds for all square-integrable functions.

## Quantitative Decay of Fourier Coefficients with Higher Function Smoothness

After proving the feasibility of approximation, we further quantitatively investigate: **If a function not only has a first derivative, but possesses $ m $ derivatives, what rate will its Fourier coefficients decay towards $ 0 $?** In signal processing, the decay rate of high-frequency components directly corresponds to the smoothness of the signal. We can precisely quantify this relationship through higher-order differentiation.

### 1. Fourier Coefficient Decay of the $ m $ Derivative

Assume $ f \in V $, and that its derivatives up to the $ m $ order are continuous on $[-\pi, \pi]$ and satisfy periodic boundary conditions, i.e., $ f^{(m)} \in V $.

By performing $ m $ successive integrations by parts on the integral form (each integration by parts extracts the internal $ n $ upon differentiation, accompanied by the interchange and alternating sign of the cosine and sine terms), we establish the precise dual relationship between the Fourier coefficient $(a_n^{(m)}, b_n^{(m)})$ of $ f^{(m)}$ and the coefficient $(a_n, b_n)$ of the original function:

- **When $ m $ is even**:

$$
a_n^{(m)} = (-1)^{\frac{m}{2}} n^m a_n, \quad b_n^{(m)} = (-1)^{\frac{m}{2}} n^m b_n
$$

- **When $ m $ is odd**:

$$
a_n^{(m)} = (-1)^{\frac{m-1}{2}} n^m b_n, \quad b_n^{(m)} = (-1)^{\frac{m+1}{2}} n^m a_n
$$

Regardless of the parity of $ m $, when summing the squares of its coefficients, the signs and the interchange of trigonometric functions are smoothed out, uniformly obeying the following algebraic relationship:

$$
(a_n^{(m)})^2 + (b_n^{(m)})^2 = n^{2m} (a_n^2 + b_n^2)
$$

### 2. Series Constraint on Coefficient Decay Rate

Transforming the above equation to isolate the Fourier energy spectrum of the original function:

$$
a_n^2 + b_n^2 = \frac{1}{n^{2m}} \left[ (a_n^{(m)})^2 + (b_n^{(m)})^2 \right]
$$

Because $ f^{(m)} \in V $, by Parseval's theorem, the infinite series of the high-order derivative coefficients must converge: $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} [ (a_n^{(m)})^2 + (b_n^{(m)})^2 ] < +\infty $. According to the necessary condition for series convergence, its general term must approach $ 0 $ when $ n \to \infty $, that is:

$$
(a_n^{(m)})^2 + (b_n^{(m)})^2 = o(1)
$$

From this, we immediately lock down the asymptotic bound for the norm of the original function's Fourier coefficients:

$$
\sqrt{a_n^2 + b_n^2} = o\left( \frac{1}{n^m} \right)
$$

**Physical and Geometric Meaning**: The higher the order of the function's derivative $ m $, the smoother the graph is, with no sharp points or abrupt changes. Reflected in the frequency domain, its high-frequency coefficients decay extremely rapidly with a power law of $ 1/n^m $ towards $ 0 $.

## Ultimate Extension: Analyticity and Exponential Decay

If we push the smoothness described above to the extreme—assuming $ f(x)$ is **infinitely differentiable ($ C^\infty $)** throughout its domain, or even an **Analytic Function** satisfying the Cauchy-Riemann equations in a region of the complex plane.

In this case, the decay barrier $ 1/n^m $ in the formula holds for any sufficiently large integer $ m $. To satisfy the constraint that holds for any $ m $, the intrinsic logic of the algebraic series undergoes a qualitative leap: the rate of decay of the coefficients directly surpasses any inverse polynomial, evolving into **Exponential Decay**:

$$
a_n \longrightarrow C_1 e^{-nc}, \quad b_n \longrightarrow C_2 e^{-nc} \quad (c > 0)
$$

- **Notches and Sawteeth (like square waves, sawtooth waves)**: Non-differentiable or only first-order differentiable, corresponding to low-order polynomial decay (denominator of $ n $ or $ n^2 $), resulting in a long "high-frequency tail" in the frequency domain.
    
- **Extremely smooth, flawless curves (like $\sin x $ or analytic curves)**: The energy in the frequency domain is highly concentrated in a very small number of low-frequency terms, and the high-frequency coefficients exhibit an abrupt, exponential decay. This conclusion not only reveals the perfect duality between time-domain smoothness and frequency-domain concentration but also forms the theoretical foundation for modern spectral methods in solving differential equations.

## Classical Case Analysis: Quantitative Mapping of Geometric Tearing and Frequency Decay

When we differentiate a function in the time domain, the geometric features of the graph (such as discontinuities or sharp corners) are revealed layer by layer. The appearance of every discontinuity profoundly affects the decay rate of its Fourier coefficients.

### Case 1: Sawtooth Odd Function with a First-Kind Discontinuity

Consider a classic discontinuous periodic function $ f(x)$, defined in the initial interval as:

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} \pi - x, & x \in (0, \pi) \\ 0, & x = 0, \pi \\ -\pi - x, & x \in (-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

#### 1. Time Domain Differentiation Analysis and Geometric Properties

From the perspective of spatial structure, $ f(x)$ is complete in the entire space of square-integrable functions ($ f \in V $). However, when we take the first derivative of it on the complex plane or the real axis:

$$
f'(x) = -1, \quad x \in (-\pi, \pi) \setminus \{0\}
$$

At $ x = 0 $ and $ x = \pm\pi $, the function exhibits a step-like jump (a first-kind discontinuity). At these jump points, the generalized derivative directly generates the Dirac $\delta $ function. Since the square integral of the $\delta $ function diverges, this implies:

$$
f' \notin V \quad (\text{first derivative leaves the space of square-integrable functions})
$$

#### 2. The rate of decay in the corresponding frequency domain

Because the geometric structure is already "torn" (creating a discontinuity) upon first differentiation, its Fourier coefficients do not possess the condition for high-order decay. Based on previous calculations, the sine coefficient of this function is:

$$
b_n = \frac{2}{n} \sim O\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)
$$

This verifies our law: **If the function itself contains discontinuous jump boundaries, its Fourier coefficients decay only at the slowest rate of $ 1/n $.**

### Case Two: A first-order smooth even function with a "cusp" continuity

To delay the destruction of the time-domain geometric structure, we increase the power and examine the following even function:

$$
f(x) = \begin{cases} (\pi - x)^2, & x \in [0, \pi] \\ (\pi + x)^2, & x \in [-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

#### 1. Time Domain Differentiation Analysis and Geometric Properties

- **Original function $ f(x)$**: At $ x=0 $, $(\pi-0)^2 = \pi^2 $; at $ x=\pi $, $(\pi-\pi)^2 = 0 $. The function is **continuous** over the entire real axis, i.e., $ f \in V $.
    
- **First derivative function $ f'(x)$**: Direct differentiation yields:

$$
f'(x) = \begin{cases} -2(\pi - x), & x \in (0, \pi) \\ 2(\pi + x), & x \in (-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

At this point, $ f'(x)$ is still continuous (due to the symmetry of the left and right derivatives at $ x=0 $, which are $-2\pi $ and $ 2\pi $). Because $ f'$ is continuous and has no discontinuities, its energy is finite:

$$
f' \in V
$$

- **Second derivative function $ f''(x)$**: Differentiating $ f'$ again:

$$
f''(x) = \begin{cases} 2, & x \in (0, \pi) \\ 2, & x \in (-\pi, 0) \end{cases}
$$

Superficially, it appears to be the constant $ 2 $ on both sides, but at $ x=0 $, the graph of $ f'(x)$ is a cusp (similar to the reflection of an absolute value function). This means that the graph of the first derivative $ f'$ has a "cusp point," leading to a step-like discontinuity in the second derivative $ f''$ at $ x=0 $, even possessing a $\delta $ function component. Therefore:

$$
f'' \notin V
$$

#### 2. The rate of decay in the corresponding frequency domain

Since the destruction of the geometric structure is postponed until the second derivative, the Fourier coefficients of this function gain a higher decay rate in the initial stage. The calculated coefficients are:

$$
a_n = \frac{4}{n^2} \sim O\left(\frac{1}{n^2}\right)
$$

This again precisely aligns: **If the function itself is continuous ($ f \in V $) and its first derivative is square-integrable ($ f' \in V $), its frequency domain coefficient decay rate jumps to $ 1/n^2 $.**

### Case Three: Infinitely smooth analytic functions

If we consider a function of the form $ f(x) = e^{\cos(x)}$, it has no geometric cusps or discontinuities on the entire complex plane.

No matter how many times you differentiate it, all $ f^{(m)}$ will always belong to $ V $. This infinite smoothness causes the frequency domain components to vanish at an astonishing speed. The asymptotic bound of its coefficients surpasses any inverse polynomial $ 1/n^m $, exhibiting an exponential cliff-like decay:

$$
a_n \sim \frac{1}{n!} \quad \text{or} \quad a_n \sim \frac{1}{2^n}
$$

## Modern Functional Extension: Sobolev Spaces and Fractional Smoothness

In classical theory, we can only discuss whether integer-order derivatives, such as the first or second, are square-integrable. However, the smoothness of some functions lies precisely between "first derivative square-integrable" and "second derivative square-integrable." To achieve a more precise quantitative characterization of function smoothness, modern functional analysis introduced the **Sobolev Space $ H^s $**.

Its core motivation is: **To use the weighted sum of Fourier coefficients to completely translate the "differentiation operation in the time domain" into "algebraic weighting in the frequency domain."**

### 1. Algebraic Reconstruction from Integer Order to Fractional Order

Recall that, if $ f $ the $ m $ n-th derivative $ f^{(m)}$ is square-integrable, then by Parseval's theorem, the corresponding frequency domain series is:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} n^{2m} (a_n^2 + b_n^2) < +\infty
$$

Functional analysis proposed an extremely bold and elegant idea: **Why must the exponent $ 2m $ be an even number or an integer?**

We can directly extend this index to any real number $ s \ge 0 $, thereby defining the Sobolev Space $ H^s $. We define a new function $ g(x)$ whose Fourier coefficients carry a fractional-order weight $ n^s $:

$$
g(x) \sim \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \left( n^s a_n \cos(nx) + n^s b_n \sin(nx) \right)
$$

### 2. Rigorous Definition of the $ H^s $ Space

For any real number $ s \in \mathbb{R}$, if the Fourier coefficients of the function $ f(x)$ satisfy the following weighted series convergence:

$$
\|f\|_{H^s}^2 = \frac{a_0^2}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} n^{2s} (a_n^2 + b_n^2) < +\infty
$$

Then the function $ f $ is said to belong to the **Sobolev Space of order $ s $ (denoted as $ f \in H^s $)**.

- **The $ H^0 $ Space**: When $ s=0 $, the above formula degenerates to $\sum (a_n^2 + b_n^2) < +\infty $, which is precisely the classical square-integrable space $ L^2 $. It only requires that the total energy of the function itself is finite.
    
- **The $ H^1 $ Space**: When $ s=1 $, the above formula requires $\sum n^2 (a_n^2 + b_n^2) < +\infty $, which is equivalent to the first derivative of the function being square-integrable ($\int |f'|^2 dx < +\infty $).
    
- **Fractional Order Space (e.g., $ H^{\frac{1}{2}}$)**: When $ s = \frac{1}{2}$, the series form is $\sum n (a_n^2 + b_n^2) < +\infty $. It measures the energy of a "half-order derivative."
    

### 3. In-depth Summary

By introducing the Sobolev Space $ H^s $, mathematicians have successfully established a perfect dual spectral method system:

$$
\text{time domain smoothness standard:} f \in H^s \Longleftrightarrow \text{frequency domain decay spectrum constraint:} \sum n^{2s}(a_n^2+b_n^2) < +\infty
$$

This theory breaks the limitation of classical calculus, which could only discuss integer-order derivatives. In the numerical solution of modern partial differential equations (PDEs), wavelet analysis, and spectral methods in signal processing, by examining the critical boundary of the index $ s $, we can very accurately capture the singularity and evolution law of the cusps of the solution to the differential equation.
