## Linear Constant Coefficient Differential Equations

### 1. Mapping of Operator Polynomials

When we face a $ n $ order homogeneous linear differential equation with constant coefficients:

$$
a_n y^{(n)} + a_{n-1} y^{(n-1)} + \dots + a_1 y' + a_0 y = 0
$$

It is actually discussing a polynomial $ P(D) = \sum_{i=0}^n a_i D^i $ of the differential operator $ D = \frac{d}{dx}$.

The so-called characteristic equation $ P(\lambda) = 0 $ essentially treats the operator $ D $ as a variable, abstracting the differential equation into an algebraic equation in the space of operators: $ P(D)y = 0 $.

### 2. Decomposition of Root Subspace and Kernel Space

According to the fundamental theorem of algebra, a polynomial $ P(\lambda)$ can be uniquely factored into a product of linear factors over the complex domain:

$$
P(\lambda) = \prod_{j=1}^k (\lambda - \lambda_j)^{m_j}
$$

Correspondingly to the differential operator, there is $ P(D) = \prod_{j=1}^k (D - \lambda_j)^{m_j}$.

Since these operator factors $(D - \lambda_j)^{m_j}$ commute pairwise, according to the **Kernel Subspace Decomposition Theorem** (or cyclic subspace decomposition), the solution space of the differential equation $ V = \ker(P(D))$ can be decomposed into a direct sum of several mutually independent subspaces:

$$
V = \ker((D - \lambda_1)^{m_1}) \oplus \ker((D - \lambda_2)^{m_2}) \oplus \dots \oplus \ker((D - \lambda_k)^{m_k})
$$

Each subspace here $\ker((D - \lambda_j)^{m_j})$ is precisely the manifestation of the **root subspace** in the function space.

### 3. Mechanism for Generating Basis Vectors

In each root subspace $\ker((D - \lambda_j)^{m_j})$, what we need to find are all the functions that are annihilated by $(D - \lambda_j)^{m_j}$.

- When it is a $ m_j = 1 $ (single root), the subspace is spanned by the characteristic vector $ e^{\lambda_j x}$.
    
- When it is a $ m_j > 1 $ (repeated root), simple characteristic vectors are insufficient to fill the dimension, thus giving rise to **generalized characteristic vectors**.
    

It can be proven by the shifting formula $ D - \lambda = e^{\lambda x} D e^{-\lambda x}$ that the basis for this subspace is exactly $\{e^{\lambda_j x}, x e^{\lambda_j x}, \dots, x^{m_j-1} e^{\lambda_j x}\}$. This is completely consistent with the logic of handling repeated eigenvalues in Jordan canonical form in linear algebra.

So, in fact, what a constant coefficient linear differential equation needs to do is only perform polynomial decomposition. If polynomial decomposition can handle it, the differential equation is solved.

### Example
For example $ f'''-3f'+2f=0 $, that is, considering the decomposition of $ D^3-3D+2 $, decomposing it into $(D-1)^2(D-2)$, then there is $ f\in Ker(D-1)^2 \oplus Ker(D+2)$, so $ Ker(D-1)^2 $ is $ c_1e^x+c_2xe^x $, $ Ker(D+2)$ is $ c_3e^{-2x}$, and the sum is $ c_1e^x+c_2xe^x+c_3e^{-2x}$, which is the general solution.

### Another Understanding

We previously considered the solution of linear constant coefficient differential equations from the perspective of linear algebra. In fact, we can consider the so-called characteristic equation without considering linear algebra. Although it is almost the same in reality, it can be understood from a different angle.

Consider the second-order linear recurrence relation:

$$
a_{n+2}+c_1a_{n+1}+c_2a_n=0
$$

We will not solve it, but we will solve the geometric progression, so we thought of constructing $ b_n=a_{n+1}+ka_n $. What we hope for is:

$$
b_{n+1}=qb_n
$$

Or equivalently

$$
a_{n+2}+xa_{n+1}=y(a_{n+1}+a_n)
$$

This way, by comparing it with the original equation, we can solve $ x,y $, thereby finding the general term of $ b_n $, and then working backward to find $ a_n $.
Can we do something similar for differential equations?:

$$
y''+c_1y'+c_2y=0
$$

Second-order ones are not, but first-order ones are, so we construct $ h=y'-ky $, and we hope to obtain:

$$
h'-qh=0
$$

That is

$$
(y'-ky)'-q(y'-ky)=0
$$

Similarly, by comparing with the original equation, we can solve for $ k,q $, thereby obtaining the form of $ y'+ky $, and then we just need to solve a first-order equation.
Let's calculate it specifically, using

$$
k+q=-c_1,
qk=c_2
$$

we know that $ q,k $ is the same as the corresponding equation $ x^2+c_1x+c_2 $, which is the so-called characteristic equation's two roots $\lambda_1,\lambda_2 $.
First, consider the case where the two roots are different.
For such an equation, naturally, one might think of direct separation of variables and integration:

$$
\frac{(y'-ky)'}{y'-ky}=q
$$

Integrating and simplifying gives

$$
y'-ky=Ce^{qx}
$$

Then multiply by the factor $ e^{-kx}$:

$$
(e^{-kx}y)'=Ce^{(q-k)x}
$$

Integrating gives

$$
e^{-kx}y=C_1e^{(q-k)x}+C_2'
$$

So there is

$$
y=C_1e^{qx}+C_2e^{kx}
$$

It is not difficult to find the two roots of the characteristic equation, thus the general solution of $ y $:

$$
y=C_1e^{-\lambda_1x}+C_2e^{-\lambda_2x}
$$

If the two roots are the same, let it be $\lambda $.

$$
(y'-\lambda y)'-\lambda(y'-\lambda y)=0
$$

Similarly, there is

$$
y'-\lambda y=Ce^{\lambda x}
$$

The same applies to $ e^{-\lambda x}$:

$$
(e^{-\lambda x}y)'=C
$$

Thus we get

$$
y=Cxe^{\lambda x}
$$

This is consistent with the result we obtained when considering linear operators.
#### Particular Solution

In linear operators, we specifically consider the general calculation of the particular solution, which involves considering the inverse of the differential operator, something that the simple person has not studied.
Here, we can obtain the particular solution in a more formal way by considering:

$$
y''+c_1y'+c_2y=f(x)
$$

We repeat our old trick, writing it as:

$$
(y'-\lambda_1y)'-\lambda_2(y'-\lambda_1y)=f(x)
$$

This reduces to a first-order differential equation, so we multiply by the integrating factor $ e^{-\lambda_2x}$, and let $ u=y'-\lambda_1 y $:

$$
(e^{-\lambda_2x}u)'=f(x)e^{-\lambda_2x}
$$

Integrating gives:

$$
y'-\lambda_1y=e^{\lambda_2x}(\int f(x)e^{-\lambda_2x}dx+C)
$$

One more time:

$$
(e^{-\lambda_1x}y)'=RHS\cdot e^{-\lambda_1x}
$$

If the two roots are different, then integrate:

$$
y=e^{\lambda_1x}(\int(e^{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)x}\int f(x)e^{-\lambda_2x}dx+C)dx+C')
$$

Separating the constant gives:

$$
y = e^{\lambda_1x} \left( \int e^{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)x} \left( \int f(x)e^{-\lambda_2x} dx \right) dx + C_1 \int e^{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)x} dx + C_2 \right)
$$

This also gives the general form of the solution, but guessing the solution is still more comfortable.
If the two roots are the same, then the integral of the second term becomes $ C_1x $.

The integral of the first term looks a bit ugly and is not symmetric, but in fact, it can be simplified through calculation. We write it as a definite integral:

$$
\int_{a}^{x} \left( \int_{a}^{t} f(s) e^{-\lambda_1 s} ds \right) e^{(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)t} dt \cdot e^{\lambda_2 x}
$$

Integration by parts:

$$
= \left( \left. \int_{a}^{s} f(s) e^{-\lambda_1 s} ds \cdot \frac{e^{(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)t}}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2} \right|_{t=a}^{t=x} - \int_{a}^{x} f(t) e^{-\lambda_1 t} \cdot \frac{e^{(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)t}}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2} dt \right) e^{\lambda_2 x}
$$

$$
= \left( \int_{a}^{x} f(s) e^{-\lambda_1 s} ds \cdot \frac{e^{(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)x}}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2} + \int_{0}^{x} \frac{f(t) e^{-\lambda_2 t}}{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1} dt \right) e^{\lambda_2 x}
$$

**Final form:**

$$
= \left( \int_{a}^{x} \frac{f(t) e^{-\lambda_1 t}}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2} dt \right) e^{\lambda_1 x} + \left( \int_{a}^{x} \frac{f(t) e^{-\lambda_2 t}}{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1} dt \right) e^{\lambda_2 x}
$$

**① $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2 $**

$$
y(x) = \left( \int^x \frac{f(t) e^{-\lambda_1 t}}{\lambda_1 - \lambda_2} dt + C_1 \right) e^{\lambda_1 x}

+ \left( \int^x \frac{f(t) e^{-\lambda_2 t}}{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1} dt + C_2 \right) e^{\lambda_2 x}
$$

**② $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda $**

$$
y(x) = \left( \int^x f(t) \cdot t e^{-\lambda t} dt + C_1 \right) e^{\lambda x}
+ \left( \int^x f(t) e^{-\lambda t} dt + C_2 \right) x e^{\lambda x}
$$

This is a very standard and very generalized solution, but it is obviously not easy to calculate or memorize. Observing the form, we find:

$$
\text{Correspondence for the Homogeneous Part}:c_1\phi_1(x)+c_2\phi_1(x)
$$

$$
\text{Correspondence for the Non-homogeneous Part}:D_1(x)\phi_1(x)+D_2(x)\phi_2(x)
$$

### Euler's equation

$$
\sum_i a_ix^iy^{(i)}=0
$$

Where $ a_i $ is a constant. A differential equation of this form is called an Euler equation.
Euler's equation has a nice property. Consider $ z(x)=y(-x)$. Then, the even terms do not cancel, but the odd terms cancel because both $ z'$ and $ x $ have an extra negative sign, so they cancel out. That is to say, $ z $ also satisfies the equation, which means that we only need to consider the case of $ x>0 $.

Then what function value is greater than $ 0 $? The natural idea is $ e^x $. Let $ x=e^t $, then $ xy'=e^t\frac{dy}{dx}=e^t\frac{dy}{dt}\frac{dt}{dx}$. Noticing $\frac{dt}{dx}=e^{-t}$, we get $ xy'=\frac{dy}{dt}$, so beautiful. We consider the second term, let $ D = \frac{d}{dt}$, then $ x^2 \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = D(D-1)y $. By mathematical induction, we can get $ x^k \frac{d^ky}{dx^k} = D(D-1)\dots(D-k+1)y $. In this way, it returns to a constant coefficient linear equation.

For example, we consider

$$
x^2y''+\frac{3}{2}xy'-y=0
$$

Then after the substitution, it becomes

$$
(D(D-1)+\frac{3}{2}D-1)y=0
$$

After simplification, we get

$$
(D^2+\frac{1}{2}D-1)y=0
$$

This ugly equation cannot be solved. In summary, we find two roots $\lambda_i $, and then

$$
y=C_1e^{\lambda_1t}+C_2e^{\lambda_2t}
$$

But this is not $ y(x)$. Substituting $ t=lnx$

$$
$ y(x)=C_1x^{\lambda_1}+C_2x^{\lambda_2}
$$

But this requires $ x>0 $. Using what was said before, it is enough to add the absolute value of $ x $

$$
y(x)=C_1|x|^{\lambda_1}+C_2|x|^{\lambda_2}
$$

Correspondingly, if there are repeated roots, we get

$$
y(x)=C_1x^{\lambda}+C_2ln|x|x^{\lambda}
$$

### Bernoulli's equation

$$
y'+P(x)y=Q(x)y^n
$$

If there is no $ y^n $, it is a standard first-order non-homogeneous equation, so naturally, we think of dividing by $ y^n $, getting

$$
y^{-n}y'+P(x)y^{1-n}=Q(x)
$$

We find that $ 1-n $ and $-n $ differ by $ 1 $, which is related to taking the first derivative, so we perform the variable substitution $ z=y^{1-n}$, then

$$
z'+P(x)z=Q(x)
$$

We get the form of a standard first-order non-homogeneous equation.

## First-order ODE Group

How to solve $ n $ coupled first-order differential equations?

$$
y_1'=F_1(x,y_1,y_2,...,y_n)
$$

$$
...
$$

$$
y_n'=F_n(x,y_1,y_2,...,y_n)
$$

For convenience of description, we can denote $\vec{y}=(y_1,...,y_n)^T $, and similarly $\vec{F}=(F_1,...,F_n)^T $.

The general solution takes the form

$$
y_1(x)=\varphi_1(x,C_1,...C_n)
$$

Because we need to solve $ n $ equations, there are $ n $ constants.
The judgment of independence is still the determinant

$$
|\frac{D(\vec{\varphi})}{D(\vec{C})}|
$$

The existence and uniqueness condition changes accordingly, requiring that the Lipschitz condition is satisfied for $(x,\vec{y})\in \Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}\times \mathbb{R}^n $, for every $ F_i $, and for every $ y_i $.

### Why study systems of differential equations

We consider the $ n $ order differential equation, which can actually be transformed into a system of first-order ODEs by a change of variables. By setting $ u_i=y^{(i)}$, we get

$$
u_1'=u_2
$$

$$
...
$$

$$
u_{n-1}'=u_n
$$

$$
u_n=F(x,u_0,u_1...),\text{That is, the original differential equation}
$$

This means that to solve the $ n $ order equation, it is sufficient to solve such a system of equations. Therefore, an $ n $ order equation has $ n $ constants, which is actually because it corresponds to $ n $ first-order equations. If we change the $\varphi $ of the condition of constant independence above to the corresponding $ y^{(n)}$, we find that it is the same as the condition of constant independence for the $ n $ order differential equation.

For the existence and uniqueness condition, we look at $\vec{F}$:

$$
\vec{F}(x, \vec{y}) =  (y_2 \ y_3 \ \cdots \ G(x, y_1, \dots, y_n))^T
$$

Then the corresponding Jacobian matrix is

$$
\frac{D(\vec{F})}{D(\vec{y})} = \begin{pmatrix} 
0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \\ 
G_{y_1} & G_{y_2} & G_{y_3} & \cdots & G_{y_n} 
\end{pmatrix}
$$

This matrix $\in C^1 $ is a sufficient condition for Lipschitz continuity (bounded derivative), so we only need the continuity of the derivatives in the bottom row.

The existence and uniqueness condition for a second-order differential equation requires $ p,q $ to be continuous, which is actually the corresponding derivative.

### Various forms

#### 1. General Form

The topmost level is a highly abstract nonlinear vector equation:

$$
\vec{y}' = \vec{F}(x, \vec{y})
$$

- **Meaning**: The rate of evolution of the system $\vec{y}'$ depends on some complex nonlinear combination of the current independent variable $ x $ and the state vector $\vec{y}$.
    

#### 2. Linearization

By introducing the "**linear**" constraint, the equation degenerates into a system of linear differential equations with variable coefficients:

$$
\vec{y}' = A(x)\vec{y} + \vec{f}(x)
$$

- **Structure**: 
    
    - $ A(x)$ is the coefficient matrix, whose elements vary with the independent variable $ x $.
        
    - $\vec{f}(x)$ is the non-homogeneous term (or forcing term).
        
- **Logic**: At this point, the influence of the state $\vec{y}$ on the rate of evolution is linear.
    

#### 3. Constant Coefficients

Further introducing the "**constant coefficients**" constraint, the equation degenerates into the easiest form to solve:

$$
\vec{y}' = A\vec{y} + \vec{f}(x)
$$

- **Core Change**: The matrix $ A $ no longer varies with $ x $, but is a constant matrix.
    
- **Value**: This is the area where linear algebra intervenes the deepest. The general solution can be written directly by calculating the **eigenvalues** and **eigenvectors** (such as the Jordan canonical form) of the matrix $ A $.
