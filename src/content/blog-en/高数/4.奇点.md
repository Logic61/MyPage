## Green's theorem encounters singularities, and Gauss's theorem?

Naturally. And it's even more interesting—because the "holes" in three dimensions are much more numerous than in two.

### Singularities in Gauss's Theorem: Point Sources and Divergence

Consider the three-dimensional vector field:

$$
\vec{F} = \frac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|^3} = \left(\frac{x}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}, \frac{y}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}, \frac{z}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\right)
$$

This is the electric field produced by a point charge in electrostatics (Coulomb field). Directly calculating the divergence:

$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{x}{r^3}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{y}{r^3}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(\frac{z}{r^3}\right)
$$

where $ r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$. Differentiating the first term:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{x}{r^3}\right) = \frac{1}{r^3} - \frac{3x^2}{r^5}
$$

The sum of the three terms is:

$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{3}{r^3} - \frac{3(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{r^5} = \frac{3}{r^3} - \frac{3r^2}{r^5} = 0
$$

So, the divergence is zero everywhere outside the origin. This seems to perfectly match Gauss's theorem—if the region does not contain the origin, the flux is zero.

But what if it is a closed surface enclosing the origin? Take the unit sphere $ S $, and the outward normal vector is $\hat{n} = \frac{\vec{r}}{r}$:

$$
\vec{F} \cdot \hat{n} = \frac{\vec{r}}{r^3} \cdot \frac{\vec{r}}{r} = \frac{r^2}{r^4} = \frac{1}{r^2} = 1 \quad (\text{On the unit sphere})
$$

So the flux:

$$
\iint_S \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_S 1 \, dS = 4\pi
$$

**A contradiction appears**: the divergence is zero everywhere, but the flux is not zero. Gauss's theorem fails here.

### Why does it fail? — The nature of singularities

Similar to the two-dimensional case, the problem lies at the origin. $\vec{F}$ is undefined at the origin and does not satisfy the condition $ C^1 $ required by Gauss's theorem. The handling is similar: remove a small sphere around the origin $ B_\epsilon $, and apply Gauss's theorem on $ V \setminus B_\epsilon $:

$$
\iiint_{V \setminus B_\epsilon} (\nabla \cdot \vec{F}) \, dV = \iint_{\partial V} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S} + \iint_{S_\epsilon^-} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S} = 0
$$

So the flux through the outer surface equals the flux through the inner sphere (after adjusting the direction):

$$
\iint_{\partial V} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_{S_\epsilon} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S} = 4\pi
$$

This is independent of $\epsilon $! That is, **no matter what shape the surface enclosing the origin has, the flux is always $ 4\pi $**.

### From "Hole" to "δ-function"

Where does this constant $ 4\pi $ come from? It tells us: at the origin, the divergence "hides" an infinite spike. It cannot be described by traditional functions, but we can introduce the **Dirac delta function**:

$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = 4\pi \delta^3(\vec{r})
$$

Here, $\delta^3 $ is the three-dimensional delta function, satisfying:
- $\delta^3(\vec{r}) = 0 $ when $\vec{r} \neq 0 $-$\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^3} \delta^3(\vec{r}) \, dV = 1 $

Thus, Gauss's theorem in a region containing a singularity recovers to:

$$
\iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \vec{F}) \, dV = \iiint_V 4\pi \delta^3(\vec{r}) \, dV = 4\pi = \iint_{\partial V} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S}
$$

This is the differential form of **Gauss's Law** in physics: $\nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}$. The charge density of a point charge is a delta function.

### Classification and Intuition of Singularities

Singularities are not limited to points. Recall the $\vec{F} = \left(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right)$ in two dimensions, whose singularity is at the **origin** (a point). In three dimensions, there are also:

- **Line singularities**: For example, a vortex field around the z-axis, where the singularity is a line.
- **Surface singularities**: Not very common in physics, but constructible in mathematics.

Key intuition: **A singularity is a "source" or a "sink"**. In two dimensions, the curl of $\vec{F}$ "hides" $ 2\pi $ at the origin; in three dimensions, the divergence of the Coulomb field "hides" $ 4\pi $ at the origin. These constants are the **topological charge** of the field—it does not depend on the shape of the surface, only on whether the surface "encloses" the singularity.

### Singularities in Stokes' Theorem

Since both Green's and Gauss's theorems have singularity issues, what about Stokes' theorem? Of course, it has them too. Consider a surface passing through a singularity, such as a hole in the plane $ z=0 $. In this case, one needs to "cut out" the singularity from the surface, similar to Green's theorem, or use a more ingenious method—considering whether the boundary of the surface winds around the singularity.

In fact, the two-dimensional $\vec{F} = \left(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right)$ can be seen as a special case of the three-dimensional vector field $\vec{F} = \left(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \frac{x}{x^2+y^2}, 0\right)$. Its curl is in the $ z $ direction:

$$
\nabla \times \vec{F} = \left(0, 0, \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) = (0, 0, 0) \quad (\text{Outside the origin})
$$

So the circulation around any closed curve enclosing the origin is $ 2\pi $. This is completely consistent with the conclusion of Green's theorem.

### Summary: A Unified View of Singularities

| Dimension | Field Type | Singularity Manifestation | Conserved Quantity |
|------|--------|----------|--------|
| 2D | Source-free field $\vec{F}$| Point singularity | Circulation $= 2\pi \times $ Winding Number |
| 3D | Source-free field $\vec{F}$| Point singularity | Flux $= 4\pi \times $ Charge Number |
| 3D | Curl-free field $\vec{F}$| Line singularity | Circulation $= 2\pi \times $ Winding Number |

The core implication of singularities is: **local properties (divergence/curl being zero) cannot determine global properties**. The topological structure of the field—how it "winds around" the singularity—is what determines the value of the integral. This is also why, in more advanced mathematics, we introduce **cohomology** to systematically study these problems.
