We want to solve the infinite series:

$$
S(1) = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = ?
$$

To solve it, we introduce an auxiliary power series function $ S(x)$:

$$
S(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{x^n}{n^2}
$$

- **Radius of convergence**: $ R = 1 $
    
- **Domain of convergence**: $[-1, 1]$ In the open interval $(-1, 1)$, differentiate term by term with respect to $ S(x)$:

$$
S'(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{n x^{n-1}}{n^2} = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{x^{n-1}}{n}, \quad x \in (-1, 1)
$$

To conveniently eliminate the $ n $ in the denominator, multiply both sides by $ x $:

$$
x S'(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{x^n}{n}, \quad x \in (-1, 1)
$$

Differentiate both sides again with respect to $ x $:

$$
(x S'(x))' = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} \frac{n x^{n-1}}{n} = \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} x^{n-1}
$$

Notice that the right side is an infinite geometric series with the first term $ 1 $ and common ratio $ x $, which converges when $|x| < 1 $:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} x^{n-1} = \sum_{n=0}^{+\infty} x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}
$$

From this, we obtain the closed-form equation:

$$
(x S'(x))' = \frac{1}{1-x}
$$

Now we need to use integration to get from $(x S'(x))'$ step by step to restore $ S(x)$.
On both sides of the above equation, from $ 0 $ to $ x $ perform definite integration:

$$
x S'(x) = \int_{0}^{x} \frac{1}{1-t} \, dt
$$

Using the substitution method (let $ u = 1-t, \, du = -dt $) we get:

$$
x S'(x) = \Big[ -\ln(1-t) \Big]_{0}^{x} = -\ln(1-x), \quad x \in (-1, 1)
$$

Move $ x $ to the right side:

$$
S'(x) = -\frac{1}{x} \ln(1-x)
$$

Integrate again from $ 0 $ to $ x $ to recover the function $ S(x)$:

$$
S(x) = \int_{0}^{x} -\frac{1}{t} \ln(1-t) \, dt
$$

Because $ S(x)$ is continuous at $ x=1 $ the point, so we can find the value of $ 1 $by approaching from the left $ S(1)$ the left limit:

$$
\lim_{x \to 1^-} S(x) = \lim_{x \to 1^-} \int_{0}^{x} -\frac{1}{t} \ln(1-t) \, dt
$$

$$
S(1) = \int_{0}^{1} -\frac{1}{t} \ln(1-t) \, dt
$$

However, for this integral, we can see that the function value at the point $ 1 $ tends to infinity, which contradicts the familiar Riemann integral and is called the generalized integral.
# Improper Integral
###  Classification of Improper Integrals

Improper integrals (also known as improper integrals) are mainly divided into two major types:

- **Infinite Integral (Integration region $\infty $)**: The integration interval is an infinite interval (e.g., $[a, +\infty)$, $(-\infty, b]$, or $(-\infty, +\infty)$).
    
- **Singular Integral (Function $\infty $)**: Although the integration interval is finite, the integrand approaches infinity near certain points (singular points) within the interval.
    

### Analogy between Integrals and Series

Intuitive correspondence between definite/improper integrals and finite/infinite series:

$$
\begin{aligned} \int_{a}^{A} f(x) \, dx \quad &\longleftrightarrow \quad \sum_{n=1}^{N} a_n \\ \bigg\downarrow A \to +\infty, \ x \longleftrightarrow n \quad & \qquad\quad \bigg\downarrow N \to +\infty \\ \int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx \quad &\longleftrightarrow \quad \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} a_n \quad (S_n \to S) \end{aligned}
$$

> **Intuitive understanding**: The definite integral over a finite interval corresponds to the finite partial sum of a series $ S_n $; when the upper limit tends to infinity, the improper integral essentially corresponds to the summation and convergence of an infinite series.
# Infinite Integral
### Strict definition of the improper integral

#### 1. Prerequisites

Let the function $ f(x)$ be defined on the interval $[a, +\infty)$, and be integrable on any finite interval $[a, A]$.

#### 2. Convergence and Definition

If the limit

$$
\lim_{A \to +\infty} \int_{a}^{A} f(x) \, dx
$$

exists (let its value be $ I $), then the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ is said to **converge**.

In this case, the value of the improper integral is defined as this limit:

$$
\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx = I
$$

#### 3. Divergence

If the above limit does not exist, the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ is said to **diverge**.

### Calculation Examples

#### Example 1

Find the convergence and value of the improper integral $\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx $.

$$
\lim_{A \to +\infty} \int_{1}^{A} \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx = \lim_{A \to +\infty} \left. -\frac{1}{x} \right|_{1}^{A} = \lim_{A \to +\infty} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{A} \right) = 1
$$

**Conclusion**: The improper integral **converges**, and its value is $ 1 $.

#### Example 2

Find the convergence and value of the improper integral $\int_{0}^{+\infty} e^{-x} \, dx $.

$$
\int_{0}^{A} e^{-x} \, dx = \left. -e^{-x} \right|_{0}^{A} = 1 - e^{-A} \xrightarrow{A \to +\infty} 1
$$

**Conclusion**: The improper integral **converges**, and its value is $ 1 $.

#### $ p $Convergence of Improper Integrals

Discuss the convergence of the integral $\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx \quad (p \in (0, +\infty))$:

1. When $ p \in (0, 1]$, the improper integral **diverges**.
    
2. When $ p > 1 $, the improper integral **converges**.
    

### Cauchy Criterion for Convergence of Improper Integrals

**Theorem (Cauchy Criterion)**:

A necessary and sufficient condition for the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ to converge is: $\forall \varepsilon > 0 $, $\exists X_{\varepsilon} \ge a $, such that for any $ A_1, A_2 > X_{\varepsilon}$, we have:

$$
\left| \int_{A_1}^{A_2} f(x) \, dx \right| \le \varepsilon
$$

> **Series Analogy**: This perfectly corresponds to the Cauchy criterion for the convergence of a series $\left| \sum_{k=n}^{m} a_k \right| < \varepsilon $. When the interval is far enough, the contribution of the tail of the integral can be neglected.

### Properties and Misconceptions of Improper Integrals

#### 1. Linearity

If both $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ and $\int_{a}^{+\infty} g(x) \, dx $ converge, then for any constant $ c_1, c_2 $:

$$
\int_{a}^{+\infty} (c_1 f(x) + c_2 g(x)) \, dx \quad \text{converges}
$$

#### 2. Intuitive Misconception Regarding "The Integrand Tending to $ 0 $"

- **Question**: If $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ converges, must $ f(x)$ necessarily tend to $ 0 $ when $ x \to +\infty $?
    
- **Conclusion and Graphical Intuition**: **Not necessarily**.
    
    A classic counterexample suggests: We can construct a function that has infinitely many increasingly narrow "spikes" at infinity (for example, at every integer $ n $, the spike height is $ 1 $, but the width is $\frac{1}{2^n}$).
    
    In this case, although $ f(x) \not\to 0 $ (the limit may not even exist) when $ x \to +\infty $, because the area of the spikes (i.e., the integral contribution) decays geometrically, the entire improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ is still **convergent**.

## The Comparison Test

### Absolute Convergence and Conditional Convergence

Similar to the series of terms, the improper integral of an alternating function is classified into the following two cases based on the integrability of its absolute value:

1. **Absolute Convergence**
    
    If the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} |f(x)| \, dx $ converges, it is called a proper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ **absolutely convergent**.
    
2. **Conditional Convergence**
    
    If the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ converges, but $\int_{a}^{+\infty} |f(x)| \, dx $ diverges, it is called a improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ to be **conditionally convergent**.
    

> **Graphical Intuition**: The positive and negative areas cancel each other out, allowing the original integral to converge; but if you fold the entire negative half-axis up to take the absolute value, the area accumulates too quickly, which can lead to divergence, constituting conditional convergence.

### The Boundedness Theorem for Improper Integrals of Non-negative Functions

For an integrand that is always non-negative, its integral has the property of being monotonically increasing, so its convergence is completely equivalent to its boundedness.

**Theorem**:

Let $ f(x) \ge 0, \ x \in [a, +\infty)$.

Then the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ converges $\Longleftrightarrow $ $\exists M > 0 $, such that:

$$
\int_{a}^{A} f(x) \, dx \le M, \quad \forall A \ge a
$$

> **Core Logic**: The definite integral of a non-negative function, $ F(A) = \int_{a}^{A} f(x) \, dx $, is a monotonically increasing function of $ A $. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem, as long as it is bounded above, the limit must exist when $ A \to +\infty $.

### Comparison Test for Improper Integrals of Non-negative Functions

Similar to the series of terms, we also have the Comparison Test:

Suppose that for two non-negative functions, the following holds when $ x \to +\infty $:

$$
0 \le f(x) \le g(x)
$$

Or consider their limit form (Limit Comparison Test):

$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = k
$$

- By using a benchmark function $ g(x)$ with known convergence or divergence (usually $ p $-integral $\frac{1}{x^p}$), we can "amplify" or "shrink" it to determine the convergence status of the target function $ f(x)$.

#### Application Example of the Comparison Test

##### Example 1

Analyze the convergence of the improper integral $\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{x^2}{x^4 + \sin^2(x)} \, dx $.

Due to $\sin^2(x) \ge 0 $, it is obvious that the following inequality relationship holds on the interval $[1, +\infty)$:

$$
0 \le \frac{x^2}{x^4 + \sin^2(x)} \le \frac{x^2}{x^4} = \frac{1}{x^2}
$$

Since the benchmark $ p $-integral $\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx $ converges (at which point $ p = 2 > 1 $), by the **Comparison Test** for non-negative functions, the original integral **converges**.

##### Example 2

Analyze the convergence of the improper integral $\int_{0}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx $.

We can split the integration interval into $[0, 1]$ and $[1, +\infty)$. Since the integrand is continuous on the finite interval $[0, 1]$, the definite integral over this part must exist; therefore, we only need to examine the infinite limit part $\int_{1}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx $.

When $ x \ge 1 $, we have $ x^2 \ge x $, which leads to the inequality:

$$
e^{-x^2} \le e^{-x}
$$

Since $\int_{1}^{+\infty} e^{-x} \, dx $ is known to converge, by the **Comparison Test**, the original integral $\int_{0}^{+\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx $ also **converges**.

### Abel's Test

#### 1. Classic Example

Discussing the convergence of the improper integral of an alternating function $\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{\sin(x)}{x^p} \, dx $:

- **When $ p > 1 $**: Due to $\left| \frac{\sin(x)}{x^p} \right| \le \frac{1}{x^p}$, the original integral **absolutely converges**.
    
- **When $ p \in (0, 1]$**: The original integral **conditionally converges**. To systematically prove the convergence of the product of a monotonically decreasing function tending to $ 0 $ and an oscillating function, more advanced tests are required.
    

#### 2. Theorem: Abel's Test

Consider the integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x)g(x) \, dx $. If the following two conditions are met:

1. $ f(x)$ is **monotonic and bounded** on $[a, +\infty)$;
    
2. The improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} g(x) \, dx $ **converges**;
    

Then the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x)g(x) \, dx $ **converges**.

### III. Mathematical Proof and Derivation of the Theorem

There are two mainstream calculus proof paths:

#### Path A: Using the Second Mean Value Theorem for Integrals (combined with the Cauchy Criterion)

According to the Cauchy Criterion for improper integral convergence, we need to prove that for $ A_1, A_2 $ sufficiently large, $\left| \int_{A_1}^{A_2} f(x)g(x) \, dx \right| \le \varepsilon $ is maintained.

Since $ f(x)$ is monotonic on $[A_1, A_2]$, by the **Second Mean Value Theorem for Integrals**, $\exists \xi \in [A_1, A_2]$, which implies:

$$
\left| \int_{A_1}^{A_2} f(x)g(x) \, dx \right| = \left| f(A_1) \int_{A_1}^{\xi} g(x) \, dx + f(A_2) \int_{\xi}^{A_2} g(x) \, dx \right|
$$

- Since $ f(x)$ is bounded, that is $|f(x)| \le K $;
    
- Since $\int_{a}^{+\infty} g(x) \, dx $ converges, by the Cauchy criterion, when $ A_1, A_2 $ is sufficiently large, the integrals of its subintervals $\int_{A_1}^{\xi} g(x) \, dx $ and $\int_{\xi}^{A_2} g(x) \, dx $ can both be made arbitrarily small.
    
Therefore, it can be shown that the entire integral approaches $ 0 $, which satisfies the condition for convergence.
    

#### Path B: Using Integration by Parts (Further exploration for the differentiable case)

Assume that $ f(x)$ has a continuous derivative $ f'(x)$ on the interval, and without loss of generality, let $ f(x) \downarrow f $ and $ f'(x) \le 0 $.

Let $ G(x) = \int_{a}^{x} g(t) \, dt $. Since $\int_{a}^{+\infty} g(x) \, dx $ converges, we know that the limit of $ G(x)$ exists and is bounded as the variable approaches infinity, meaning there exists a constant $ M $ such that $|G(x)| \le M $.

Perform **integration by parts** on the original expression:

$$
\int_{a}^{A} f(x)g(x) \, dx = \left. f(x)G(x) \right|_{a}^{A} - \int_{a}^{A} f'(x)G(x) \, dx = f(A)G(A) - f(a)G(a) - \int_{a}^{A} f'(x)G(x) \, dx
$$

Now, take the limit of $ A \to +\infty $ and analyze term by term:

1. **First term $ f(A)G(A)$**: When $ A \to +\infty $, the limit of $ f(A)$ exists (by the Monotone Convergence Theorem), and the limit of $ G(A)$ exists, so the limit of this term must exist.
    
2. **Last term integral**: Examine its absolute value to verify absolute convergence:

$$
\int_{a}^{A} |f'(x)G(x)| \, dx \le M \int_{a}^{A} |f'(x)| \, dx
$$

Since $ f'(x) \le 0 $ is known, we have $|f'(x)| = -f'(x)$. Substituting this into the integral gives:

$$
M \int_{a}^{A} -f'(x) \, dx = M \Big| \int_{a}^{A} f'(x) \, dx \Big| = M |f(a) - f(A)| = \tilde{M}
$$

Since $ f(A)$ is bounded as the variable approaches infinity, this integral term is controlled by a constant $\tilde{M}$ (it is bounded). Since the upper limit of the integral of a non-negative function is bounded, this integral must converge.
    

**Summary**: The limits of all terms after integration by parts exist at $ A \to +\infty $, thus completely proving the correctness of Abel's test.

Using Abel's test, it is not difficult to know the convergence of the generalized integral $ p $.

### Example of Divergence

When $ p \in (0, 1]$, the absolute value integral of the generalized integral diverges:

$$
\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{|\sin(x)|}{x^p} \, dx = +\infty
$$

To prove its divergence, it is sufficient to prove that the definite integral has no upper bound when the upper limit is $ A \to +\infty $.

#### Derivation and Amplification Steps

##### 1. Shrinking the interval

Let the upper limit $ A $ take a specific discrete value $ N\pi $ (where $ N $ is a sufficiently large positive integer), and shrink the lower limit from $ 1 $ to $\pi $ (since the integral over $[1, \pi]$ is a finite constant, omitting it does not affect the determination of convergence or divergence):

$$
\int_{1}^{A} \frac{|\sin(x)|}{x^p} \, dx \ge \int_{\pi}^{N\pi} \frac{|\sin(x)|}{x^p} \, dx
$$

##### 2. Interval Decomposition (Discretization into a Series)

Using the additivity property of definite integrals, decompose $[\pi, N\pi]$ into a sum of $ N-1 $ small intervals $[k\pi, (k+1)\pi]$, each of length $\pi $:

$$
= \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} \int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi} \frac{|\sin(x)|}{x^p} \, dx
$$

##### 3. Denominator Amplification (Overall Shrinking)

On every subinterval $ x \in [k\pi, (k+1)\pi]$, since $ x^p $ is monotonically increasing, to extract the variable $ x $ from the denominator, we replace the denominator with its maximum value at the right endpoint of the interval $((k+1)\pi)^p $.

As the denominator increases, the entire fraction decreases, thus obtaining the inequality:

$$
\ge \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} \frac{1}{((k+1)\pi)^p} \int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi} |\sin(x)| \, dx
$$

##### 4. Calculating the Area of a Sine Function's Period

Note that the integral of the absolute value of the sine function $|\sin(x)|$ over any half-period interval of length $\pi $ is a fixed constant $ C $:

$$
C = \int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi} |\sin(x)| \, dx = \int_{0}^{\pi} \sin(x) \, dx = \left. -\cos(x) \right|_{0}^{\pi} = 1 - (-1) = 2
$$

Substitute $ C = 2 $ into the formula above, and factor out the constant term:

$$
= \frac{2}{\pi^p} \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} \frac{1}{(k+1)^p}
$$

- When $ p \in (0, 1]$, according to the convergence/divergence principle for the $ p $-series (or as the famous harmonic series when $ p=1 $), the series $\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{(k+1)^p}$ is **divergent**, and its sum is $+\infty $.
    
- Since the shrinking lower bound approaches infinity at $ N \to +\infty $, by the Squeeze Theorem (Comparison Test), the original absolute value integral must diverge.
    

**Final Conclusion**: When $ p \in (0, 1]$, the generalized integral $\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{\sin(x)}{x^p} \, dx $ is not absolutely convergent. Combined with the conclusion of Abel's test mentioned earlier, it is **conditionally convergent** in this interval.

### Generalized Integral on the Entire Real Line

For cases where both the upper and lower limits of integration are infinity, its definition is similar to the one-sided improper integrals $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ and $\int_{-\infty}^{b} f(x) \, dx $.

**Definition**:

A necessary and sufficient condition for the generalized integral $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ to **converge** ($\Longleftrightarrow $) is: when any base point is chosen on the real axis (usually $ 0 $), and it is split into two parts, both parts must **independently converge**. That is:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx \text{ converges} \Longleftrightarrow \int_{-\infty}^{0} f(x) \, dx \text{ converges} \quad \text{and} \quad \int_{0}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx \text{ converges}
$$

In this case, the value of the integral is defined as the sum of the limits obtained by calculating the two parts separately:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx = \int_{-\infty}^{0} f(x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx
$$

#### Logical Unidirectionality and Cauchy Principal Value

##### 1. One-way Implication

If the generalized integral $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx $ converges, then if we let the symmetric upper and lower limits approach infinity simultaneously, its limit must also exist:

$$
\lim_{A \to +\infty} \int_{-A}^{A} f(x) \, dx \quad \text{exists}
$$

**Caution: The converse is not true!** The existence of this limit via symmetric approach does not imply that the original generalized integral converges.

##### 2. Definition of Cauchy Principal Value

To define this special integral state of “symmetric limit taking,” the concept of **Cauchy Principal Value** is introduced, denoted as $\text{P.V.}$ (Principal Value):

$$
\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x) \, dx = \lim_{A \to +\infty} \int_{-A}^{A} f(x) \, dx
$$

####  Classic Counterexample and Distinction

"Cauchy principal value exists", $\not\Rightarrow $ "generalized integral converges"

**Function under consideration**:

$$
f(x) = \frac{x}{1+x^2}
$$

##### 1. Strict Definition Perspective

We split it according to definition, prioritizing the generalized integral on the right semi-axis:

$$
\int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx = \lim_{A \to +\infty} \left. \frac{1}{2} \ln(1+x^2) \right|_{0}^{A} = \lim_{A \to +\infty} \frac{1}{2} \ln(1+A^2) = +\infty \quad (\text{diverges})
$$

Similarly, the integral on the left semi-axis $\int_{-\infty}^{0} \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx = -\infty $ also diverges.

**Conclusion**: According to the definition, the original generalized integral $\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx $ **diverges**.

##### 2. Cauchy Principal Value Perspective

Since the integrand function $ f(x) = \frac{x}{1+x^2}$ is an odd function (satisfying $ f(-x) = -f(x)$), we perform the definite integral over the symmetric interval $[-A, A]$:

$$
\int_{-A}^{A} \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx = 0 \quad (\text{the integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is always } 0)
$$

Then take the limit:

$$
\text{P.V.} \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx = \lim_{A \to +\infty} \int_{-A}^{A} \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx = \lim_{A \to +\infty} 0 = 0
$$

**Conclusion**: The Cauchy principal value of this integral exists and equals $ 0 $.

> **Core Summary**:
> 
> The convergence of an improper integral is an extremely strict requirement, demanding that the negative and positive infinities "behave independently and converge" on their own; whereas the Cauchy principal value $\text{P.V.}$ allows the areas at both ends to "cancel each other out" during a synchronously and equally (symmetrically) expanding process. Therefore, the two cannot be confused.

# Improper Integral

### I. Strict Definition of Improper Integrals and Singular Points

When the integration interval is a finite interval $[a, b]$, but the integrand function is unbounded at the endpoints of the interval, the concept of an improper integral must be introduced. Below, we take the left endpoint $ a $ as an example of a singular point:

#### 1. Prerequisites and Definition

Let the function $ f(x)$ be defined on the half-open interval $(a, b]$. If $ f(x)$ is **unbounded** in the right neighborhood of the point $ a $, then the point $ a $ is called a **singular point (Singular Point / Singular end)** of $ f(x)$.

#### 2. Convergence and Divergence

If $ f(x)$ is integrable on any finite subinterval $[a+\delta, b]$ (where $ 0 < \delta < b-a $), and the limit:

$$
\lim_{\delta \to 0^+} \int_{a+\delta}^{b} f(x) \, dx
$$

exists, then the improper integral $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx $ is said to **converge**.

If the above limit does not exist, then the improper integral is said to **diverge**.

### II. Transformation between Improper Integrals and Improper Limits (Reciprocal Substitution)

**Improper integrals and improper limits can transition into each other in essence.**

Let $ a $ be the singular point, and perform a reciprocal substitution on the integral $\int_{a+\delta}^{b} f(x) \, dx $:

Let $ t = \frac{1}{x-a}$, then $ x = \frac{1}{t} + a $, and thus $ dx = -\frac{1}{t^2} \, dt $.

Transforming the limits of integration:

- When $ x = b $, $ t = \frac{1}{b-a}$ - When $ x = a+\delta $, $ t = \frac{1}{\delta}$
    

Substituting into the integral expression:

$$
\int_{a+\delta}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{\frac{1}{\delta}}^{\frac{1}{b-a}} f\left(\frac{1}{t}+a\right) \left(-\frac{1}{t^2}\right) dt = \int_{\frac{1}{b-a}}^{\frac{1}{\delta}} f\left(\frac{1}{t}+a\right) \frac{1}{t^2} \, dt
$$

When $\delta \to 0^+$, the upper limit is $\frac{1}{\delta} \to +\infty $. Thus, the improper integral successfully transforms into an **improper limit improper integral**:

$$
\int_{\frac{1}{b-a}}^{+\infty} f\left(\frac{1}{t}+a\right) \frac{1}{t^2} \, dt
$$

### $ q $-Improper Integral Convergence

The most important benchmark criterion for improper integrals is given on the far right of the blackboard: analyzing the convergence of the integral $\int_{0}^{1} \frac{1}{x^q} \, dx \quad (q > 0)$ (where $ x=0 $ is the singular point).

First, find the definite integral in the neighborhood:

$$
\int_{\delta}^{1} \frac{1}{x^q} \, dx = \left. \frac{1}{1-q} x^{1-q} \right|_{\delta}^{1} = \frac{1}{1-q} (1 - \delta^{1-q})
$$

When $\delta \to 0^+$, classify the limit cases:

1. **When $ q \in (0, 1)$**: 
    
    Since $ 1-q > 0 $, when $\delta \to 0^+$, $\delta^{1-q} \to 0 $.
    
    The limit exists, and its value is $\frac{1}{1-q}$. Therefore, the improper integral **converges**.
    
2. **When $ q > 1 $**: 
    
    Since $ 1-q < 0 $, it can be written as $\frac{1}{\delta^{q-1}}$. When $\delta \to 0^+$, $\delta^{1-q} \to +\infty $.
    
    The limit is infinity, so the improper integral **diverges**.
    
3. **When $ q = 1 $ (discussed separately)**:

$$
\int_{\delta}^{1} \frac{1}{x} \, dx = \left. \ln(x) \right|_{\delta}^{1} = \ln(1) - \ln(\delta) = -\ln(\delta)
$$

When $\delta \to 0^+$, $-\ln(\delta) \to +\infty $. Therefore, the improper integral **diverges**.
    

#### 💡 Core Summary and Comparison (Pitfall Guide)

Comparing with the conclusion of the improper limit integral $ p $, we can find a very elegant 

- **Improper Limit Integral $\int_{1}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{x^p} \, dx $**: The space is too large, and the power must be **sufficiently large ($ p>1 $)** to press the function low enough to converge.
    
- **Improper Integral $\int_{0}^{1} \frac{1}{x^q} \, dx $**: The height is too high, and the power must be **sufficiently small ($ q<1 $)** to control the explosion rate at the singular point to converge.

### Other Cases of Singular Points and Strict Definitions

#### 1. Right endpoint is a singular point

Similarly, if the function $ f(x)$ is defined on the half-open interval $[a, b)$, and the right endpoint $ b $ is a singular point of $ f(x)$. Then the improper integral can be defined by the left limit:

$$
\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \lim_{\delta \to 0^+} \int_{a}^{b-\delta} f(x) \, dx
$$

#### 2. Singular point inside the interval

If the singular point $ c $ is located **inside** the integration interval $(a, b)$ (i.e., $ c \in (a, b)$ is a singular point of $ f(x)$), its convergence definition requires **independent convergence** on both sides:

$$
\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx \text{ converges} \Longleftrightarrow \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx \text{ converges} \quad \text{and} \quad \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \, dx \text{ converges}
$$

According to the strict notation of limits, both sides need to use **independent** perturbation amounts ($\delta $ and $\varepsilon $) to approach $ c $ separately:

$$
\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \lim_{\delta \to 0^+} \int_{a}^{c-\delta} f(x) \, dx + \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+} \int_{c+\varepsilon}^{b} f(x) \, dx
$$

> **Graphical Intuition**: At an interior singularity, $ c $ the function curve extends to both positive and negative infinity on both sides (similar to $\frac{1}{x}$ at $ 0 $ the vicinity of). A rigorous definition requires removing $[c-\delta, c+\varepsilon]$ this segment, and then $\delta, \varepsilon $ independently approaching $ 0 $ The areas on both sides have limits.

### Cauchy Principal Value of Improper Integrals

If we force the rates of approach from both sides to be perfectly symmetric (i.e., let $\delta = \varepsilon $), the corresponding limit is called the **Cauchy Principal Value of the Improper Integral**, denoted as $\text{P.V.}$:

$$
\text{P.V.} \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \lim_{\delta \to 0^+} \left( \int_{a}^{c-\delta} f(x) \, dx + \int_{c+\delta}^{b} f(x) \, dx \right)
$$

### Analysis of Classic Examples

Consider $\int_{0}^{1} \frac{1}{x} \, dx $.

#### 1. Strict Definition Perspective

Split the integral into two parts using $ 0 $ as the boundary:

- Right side: $\int_{0}^{1} \frac{1}{x} \, dx $ diverges (by the $ q $-Improper Integral Theorem, $ q=1 $ diverges);
    
- Left side: $\int_{-1}^{0} \frac{1}{x} \, dx $ also diverges.
    

Since the two parts cannot converge independently, by definition:

$$
\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{x} \, dx \quad \mathbf{\text{diverges}}
$$

#### 2. Cauchy Principal Value Perspective

Use symmetric boundaries $\delta $ approaching the singularity $ 0 $ simultaneously:

$$
\lim_{\delta \to 0^+} \left( \int_{-1}^{-\delta} \frac{1}{x} \, dx + \int_{\delta}^{1} \frac{1}{x} \, dx \right)
$$

Since $\frac{1}{x}$ is an odd function, over the symmetrically removed interval $[-1, -s] \cup [s, 1]$, the positive and negative areas completely cancel out, and the integral value is always $ 0 $:

$$
\lim_{\delta \to 0^+} \left( \ln|-1| - \ln|-\delta| + \ln|1| - \ln|\delta| \right) = \lim_{\delta \to 0^+} (0 - \ln \delta + 0 + \ln \delta) = \lim_{\delta \to 0^+} 0 = 0
$$

From this, we get:

$$
\text{P.V.} \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{x} \, dx = 0
$$
