The geometric series we are familiar with can be seen as a Taylor expansion:

$$
\frac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \dots + x^n + o(x^n)
$$

This looks like a series of functions, so **under what conditions can this equation be written in the form of an infinite series?**

$$
\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n \quad (?)
$$

- **Observation Point**: This transformation only has mathematical meaning when $ x \to 0 $ (or more accurately, within the radius of convergence $|x| < 1 $).

# Function Term Series

Definition of a Function Term Series:

- **Definition Form**: 

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n(x), \quad x \in X
$$

where $ u_n(x)$ is a sequence of functions defined on the set $ X $.
    
- **Partial Sum Function**: 

$$
S_n(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} u_k(x)
$$

This is the sum of the first $ n $ terms, and it is itself a function of $ x $.
    
- **Sum Function**: 
    
    When $ n \to \infty $, if the sequence of partial sum functions $\{S_n(x)\}$ converges to a limit, this limit is called the **Sum Function**, denoted as:

$$
S_n(x) \longrightarrow S(x)
$$

## Convergence of the Limit of a Function Sequence

We consider the limit and convergence of the function sequence $\{f_n\}$, and try to apply our ideas from the series of numbers to it.

### 1. Prerequisites

- **Common Domain**: Assume that every function in the sequence $\{f_n(x)\}$ is defined on the same set $ X $.
    
- **Pointwise Existence**: For **every** fixed independent variable $ x $ in the domain $ X $, the sequence $\{f_n(x)\}$ (which has collapsed to a constant sequence at this point) has a limit.
    

### 2. Definition of the Limit Function

- If the above limit exists, we can define a new function $ f(x)$, whose value at the point $ x $ is the limit of the sequence: 

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = f(x)
$$

- At this point, $ f(x)$ is called the **Limit Function** of the function sequence $\{f_n\}$.
    

### 3. Notation and Expression

- This relationship is written as:

$$
f_n \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} f \quad (\text{on } X \text{ on})
$$

- This means that the function sequence $\{f_n\}$ **converges pointwise** to $ f $ on the set $ X $.
    

### Pointwise Convergence

Pointwise convergence is about a local victory. Picture the functions as a pack of runners heading for a finish line. Pointwise convergence requires **each runner eventually to reach their own finish line**. The runners can get there at different speeds.

### uniform convergence

Let the function sequence $\{f_n\}$ in the set $ X $ converge to the limit function $ f $.

If for **any** given $\varepsilon > 0 $, there exists a natural number that depends only on $\varepsilon $ , such that when $ N_{\varepsilon}$, for **all** $ n \geq N_{\varepsilon}$ , it holds that:$ x \in X $Then it is called a function sequence

$$
|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon
$$

} $\{f_n\}$ On $ X $ **uniformly converges** to $ f $.

**Notation**:

$$
f_n \rightrightarrows f, \quad x \in X
$$

(Note: Here we use a **double arrow** to distinguish it from the single arrow of pointwise convergence).

---

### **Notation**: 

The essential difference between the two lies in **$ N $ dependence on $ x $ :

- **Pointwise convergence**: For different points $ x $, the rate of convergence can be different. To achieve the same accuracy $\varepsilon $, some points may require $ n=100 $, some points may require $ n=10000 $. This means $ N $ is $\varepsilon $ and $ x $ a function of:$ N(\varepsilon, x)$.
    
- **Uniform convergence**: There exists a "universal" $ N $such that if $ n $ is large enough, the entire function curve will enter the limit function $ f(x)$ in $\varepsilon $its neighborhood. At this point, $ N $ it only depends on the required accuracy:$ N(\varepsilon)$.

### Example: Using the Mean Inequality to determine uniform convergence

#### 1. Problem Setup

Let the sequence of functions $ f_n(x) = x^n(1-x)^n $with domain $ x \in [0, 1]$.

#### 1. Problem Setting

- **Pointwise limit**: Obviously, for $[0, 1]$ arbitrary $ x $when $ n \to \infty $ is$ f_n(x) \to 0 $.
That is, the limit function $ f(x) \equiv 0 $.
    
- **Finding a uniform upper bound**: We need to estimate $|f_n(x) - f(x)| = |x(1-x)|^n $ the maximum value of . According to the Arithmetic-Geometric Mean Inequality (AM-GM Inequality):

$$
x(1-x) \leq \left( \frac{x + (1-x)}{2} \right)^2 = \frac{1}{4}
$$

Therefore:

$$
|f_n(x) - 0| \leq \left( \frac{1}{4} \right)^n
$$

- **Conclusion**: Since the upper bound $\left( \frac{1}{4} \right)^n $ is independent of $ x $ and when $ n \to \infty $ approaches $ 0 $so:

$$
f_n \rightrightarrows 0 \quad (\text{on } [0, 1] \text{ uniform convergence})
$$

These two examples reveal a standard paradigm for determining uniform convergence—the **$ M $ Test Idea**:

1. First, find the pointwise limit $ f(x)$.
    
2. calculate the modulus of the deviation $|f_n(x) - f(x)|$.
    
3. **Key Step**: Find a sequence that does not depend on $ x $ the sequence $ M_n $, such that the deviation is always less than or equal to $ M_n $.
    
4. If $\lim_{n \to \infty} M_n = 0 $, then it converges uniformly.
    

###  The Criterion Theorem for Uniform Convergence

The most commonly used **necessary and sufficient condition** for determining uniform convergence (usually called $ M $ the criterion method):

- The Criterion Theorem for Uniform Convergence
    
    Let $ f_n \to f $(	ext{pointwise convergence}). If there exists a sequence $\{a_n\}$, satisfying:
    
    1. $\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = 0 $;
        
    2. for all $ x \in X $ and everything $ n \geq 1 $, it holds that $|f_n(x) - f(x)| \leq a_n $;
        
- **Conclusion**: Then the sequence of functions $\{f_n\}$ at $ X $ converges uniformly to $ f $ ($ f_n \rightrightarrows f $).
    

> **Intuitive Understanding**: If we can find a "cover" $ a_n $that can cover the error at all points, and this cover itself shrinks to $ 0 $zero, then the convergence is uniform.

### A method to prove that a series **does not converge uniformly**:

#### 1. Problem Setup

$$
f_n(x) = \frac{nx}{1 + n^2x^2}, \quad x \in [0, 1]
$$

#### **Criterion**:

- **Pointwise Limit**:
    
    - If $ x=0 $,$ f_n(0)=0 $.
        
    - If $ x \in (0, 1]$, $ n^2 $ the order of the denominator is higher than the numerator $ n $, $ n \to \infty $ is$ f_n(x) \to 0 $.
        
    - Conclusion: The limit function $ f(x) \equiv 0 $.
        
- **Construct a sequence (negation criterion)**:
    
To prove non-uniform convergence, we need to find a point $ x_n $ where the function value does not approach $ 0 $.
    
Let $ x_n = \frac{1}{n}$ (this sequence can be easily found by observation):

$$
|f_n(x_n) - f(x_n)| = |f_n(\frac{1}{n}) - 0| = \frac{n \cdot \frac{1}{n}}{1 + n^2 \cdot (\frac{1}{n})^2} = \frac{1}{1+1} = \frac{1}{2}
$$

- **Conclusion**: Since the error always remains in $ 1/2 $, it cannot be covered by an arbitrarily small $\varepsilon $, so it **does not converge uniformly** on $[0, 1]$.

### Example: Uniform convergence on a bounded closed interval

#### 1. Problem Setup

$$
f_n(x) = \frac{n + x^2}{nx}, \quad x \in (0, M]
$$

#### **Criterion**:

- **Pointwise Limit**: The expression is split into $ f_n(x) = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{x}{n}$. When $ n \to \infty $, $\frac{x}{n} \to 0 $, so $ f_n(x) \to \frac{1}{x}$.
    
- **Finding a Uniform Bound**:

$$
|f_n(x) - \frac{1}{x}| = |\frac{x}{n}|
$$

On the interval $(0, M]$, the maximum deviation occurs at $ x=M $:

$$
|\frac{x}{n}| \leq \frac{M}{n} = a_n
$$

- **Conclusion**: Let $ a_n = \frac{M}{n}$, when $ n \to \infty $, $ a_n \to 0 $. Therefore, it **converges uniformly** on $(0, M]$.

### Cauchy Criterion for Uniform Convergence

#### 1. Statement of the Theorem

Let the sequence of functions $\{f_n\}$ be defined on the set $ X $. The **necessary and sufficient condition** for $\{f_n\}$ to **converge uniformly** on $ X $ (i.e., there exists a function $ f $ such that $ f_n \rightrightarrows f $) is:

For **any** given $\varepsilon > 0 $, there **exists** a natural number $ N_{\varepsilon}$ that depends only on $\varepsilon $, such that for **all** $ n, m \geq N_{\varepsilon}$ and **all** $ x \in X $, we have:

$$
|f_n(x) - f_m(x)| < \varepsilon
$$

---

#### 2. Deconstruction of the Core Logic

- **Internal Consistency**: The essence of the Cauchy criterion is to require that the terms of a sequence $ n $ are very close to each other when they are sufficiently large.
    
- **Escaping Dependence on the Limit Function**: 
    
    - In previous criteria, we usually need to first find $ f(x) = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x)$, and then analyze $|f_n(x) - f(x)|$.
        
    - **The Cauchy Criterion** only requires comparing two terms of the sequence, $ f_n $ and $ f_m $. This means that even if the limit function is extremely complex or difficult to express explicitly, we can still discuss its uniform convergence.
        
- **Manifestation of 'Uniform'**: 
    
Unlike the Cauchy criterion for sequences, the $ N_{\varepsilon}$ here must be general for the entire domain $ X $. No matter which $ x $ you choose in $ X $, as long as the index exceeds $ N_{\varepsilon}$, the distance between the two terms must be less than $\varepsilon $.

What is the use of uniform convergence anyway?

### Core Theorem: Uniform Convergence and Continuity

#### 1. Statement of the Theorem

Let the sequence of functions $\{f_n\}$ be defined on the set $ X $:

- **Condition 1**: Each term $ f_n(x)$ is a **continuous function** on $ X $ (denoted as $ f_n \in C(X)$);
    
- **Condition 2**: $\{f_n\}$ **converges uniformly** on $ X $ to the limit function $ f $ (denoted as $ f_n \rightrightarrows f $);
    
- **Conclusion**: The limit function $ f(x)$ is also a **continuous function** on $ X $ (i.e., $ f \in C(X)$).

---

#### 2. Proof Idea: The $\varepsilon/3 $ Technique

To prove that $ f(x)$ is continuous at $ x_0 $, we need to control the size of $|f(x) - f(x_0)|$.

By inserting terms, we can decompose it into three parts:

$$
|f(x) - f(x_0)| \leq |f(x) - f_n(x)| + |f_n(x) - f_n(x_0)| + |f_n(x_0) - f(x_0)|
$$

1. **First Term $|f(x) - f_n(x)|$**: Guaranteed by **uniform convergence**. As long as $ n $ is large enough, this term is less than $\varepsilon/3 $ for all $ x $.
    
2. **Second Term $|f_n(x) - f_n(x_0)|$**: Guaranteed by the **continuity of $ f_n $**. After fixing $ n $, this term is less than $\varepsilon/3 $ when $ x $ approaches $ x_0 $.
    
3. **Third Term $|f_n(x_0) - f(x_0)|$**: Also guaranteed by **uniform convergence** (convergence at the specific point $ x_0 $).
    

---

Reviewing the example of $ f_n(x) = x^n $ on $[0, 1]$:

- Each term $ x^n $ is continuous.
    
- But the limit function $ f(x)$ is discontinuous at $ x=1 $.
    
- **Reason**: It is precisely because it **does not satisfy uniform convergence** on $[0, 1]$. This counterexample perfectly demonstrates the necessity of this theorem.

### Uniform Convergence Preserves Integrability

#### 1. Statement of the Theorem

Let the sequence of functions $\{f_n\}$ be defined on the bounded closed interval $[a, b]$:

- **Condition 1**: Each term $ f_n(x)$ is a **continuous function** on $[a, b]$ (i.e., $ f_n \in C[a, b]$);
    
- **Condition 2**: $\{f_n\}$ **converges uniformly** on $[a, b]$ to the limit function $ f $ (denoted as $ f_n \rightrightarrows f $);
    
- **Conclusion**: The order of the limit and the integral sign can be interchanged, i.e.:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_a^b f_n(x) dx = \int_a^b f(x) dx
$$

> **Intuitive Analogy**: Since uniform convergence guarantees that the entire function curve approaches the limit function 

---

#### 2. Organization of the Proof Process

Completing the proof by estimating the error:

- **Error Expression**: We need to prove $\left| \int_a^b f_n(x) dx - \int_a^b f(x) dx \right| \to 0 $.
    
- **Amplification using Integral Properties**:

$$
\left| \int_a^b (f_n(x) - f(x)) dx \right| \leq \int_a^b |f_n(x) - f(x)| dx
$$

- **Substitution using Uniform Convergence**:
    
According to the definition of uniform convergence, for any $\varepsilon > 0 $, there exists $ N_{\varepsilon}$ such that when $ n \geq N_{\varepsilon}$, for all $ x \in [a, b]$, we have $|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon $.
    
- **Final Estimation**: 

$$
\int_a^b |f_n(x) - f(x)| dx \leq \int_a^b \varepsilon \cdot dx = \varepsilon (b - a)
$$

As $\varepsilon $ approaches $ 0 $, the above error term clearly approaches $ 0 $.

If the convergence is not uniform, it is not necessarily true that:

#### Differentiation of Function Sequences and Uniform Convergence

Let $\{f_n\}$ be a sequence of functions defined on the interval $ X = [a, b]$ , if it satisfies the following conditions:

- **Pointwise Convergence**: There exists $ x_0 \in X $, such that $\{f_n(x_0)\}$ converges.
    
- **Continuously Differentiable**:$ f_n \in C^1(X)$,that is, each $ f_n $ on $ X $ has a first continuous derivative.
    
- **Uniform convergence of the derivative**: $ g $there exists a function $\{f'_n\}$ on $ X $ uniformly converges to $ g $on

$$
f'_n \rightrightarrows g, \quad x \in X
$$

**Then the following conclusion can be drawn:**

1. The sequence of functions $\{f_n\}$ on $ X $ uniformly converges to a function $ f $(that is, $ f_n \rightrightarrows f $).
    
2. Limit Function $ f $ on $ X $ differentiable, and its derivative equals the limit of the derivative function, that is:

$$
f' = g \quad \text{or written as} \quad \left( \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n \right)' = \lim_{n \to \infty} f'_n
$$

---

#### 2. Proof Sketch

2. Proof Sketch (Proof Sketch)

Using the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** for estimation:

$$
f_n(x) = f_n(a) + \int_a^x f'_n(t) \, dt
$$

When $ n \to \infty $ the corresponding terms tend to:

$$
f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x g(t) \, dt
$$

To prove uniform convergence, consider the absolute value of the difference:

$$
|f_n(x) - f_m(x)| \leq |f_n(a) - f_m(a)| + \int_a^x |f'_n(t) - f'_m(t)| \, dt
$$

Since $ f_n(a)$ converges and $ f'_n $ converges uniformly, by the **Cauchy Criterion for Convergence**, the right side of the above can be made arbitrarily small, thus proving $ f_n $ of uniform convergence. Then, differentiating the second equation, using the derivative of the variable limit integral, we get $ f' = g $ .

### Dirichlet's Test (Uniform Version)

Consider $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} a_n(x)b_n(x)$ on $ X $:

② There exists $ M > 0 $ such that $\left| \sum_{k=1}^{n} b_k(x) \right| \le M, \quad \forall n, \forall x \in X $ (i.e., the partial sums are **uniformly bounded**);

### Abel's Test (Uniform Version)

Consider $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} a_n(x)b_n(x)$ on $ X $:

If:

① For any $ x \in X $, $\{a_n(x)\}$ is **monotonic** with respect to $ n $, and there exists $ L > 0 $ such that $|a_n(x)| \le L, \quad \forall n, \forall x $ (i.e., the sequence is **uniformly bounded**);

② $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty} b_n(x)$ **converges uniformly**;

$$
\implies \sum a_n(x)b_n(x) \text{ uniform convergence}
$$

---

- **Dirichlet's logic:** $ b_n(x)$'s ability is relatively weak; its partial sums can only achieve being bounded (uniformly bounded), and cannot converge on their own. At this point, $ a_n(x)$ needs to show extremely strong suppressing power (monotonically converging to 0) to forcibly pull the entire series into the embrace of convergence.
    
- **Abel's logic:** $ b_n(x)$ itself is already excellent enough; it can achieve **uniform convergence** on its own. At this point, the requirement for $ a_n(x)$ is relaxed; it doesn't need to converge to 0, it just needs to behave itself nearby, not to disrupt the stability.
