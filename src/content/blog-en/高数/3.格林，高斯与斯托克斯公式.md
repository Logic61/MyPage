### Gradient Theorem
Let $\phi(\vec{r})$ be a scalar field defined on a spatial region ($ C^1 $ continuously differentiable),$ L $ let $ A $ be a smooth curve in space from point $ B $ to point, then:

$$
\int_{L} \nabla \phi \cdot d\vec{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A)
$$

Here, $\nabla \phi \cdot d\vec{r}$ is the **total differential** of the scalar field.$ d\phi $.

Proof:
Let the parameter equation of the space curve $ L $ be $\vec{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t))$, where $ t \in [t_A, t_B]$.

correspondingly, the starting point $ A = \vec{r}(t_A)$, the ending point $ B = \vec{r}(t_B)$.

#### 1. Expand the line integral on the left side

By the definition of the line integral, we write $ d\vec{r}$ as $\frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} dt $:

$$
\int_{L} \nabla \phi \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{t_A}^{t_B} \left( \nabla \phi(\vec{r}(t)) \cdot \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} \right) dt
$$

#### 2. Inverse application of the Chain Rule

Observe the dot product term inside the parentheses. According to the **Chain Rule** for the differentiation of composite functions of multiple variables:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} \phi(\vec{r}(t)) = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} \frac{dz}{dt}
$$

This is exactly $\nabla \phi \cdot \frac{d\vec{r}}{dt}$.

Therefore, the integral can be simplified to:

$$
\int_{t_A}^{t_B} \frac{d}{dt} \phi(\vec{r}(t)) \, dt
$$

#### 3. Returning to the one-variable Newton-Leibniz formula

Now, this is the integral of a derivative of a standard single-variable function. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$
\int_{t_A}^{t_B} \frac{d}{dt} \phi(\vec{r}(t)) \, dt = \phi(\vec{r}(t_B)) - \phi(\vec{r}(t_A))
$$

substituting the endpoint $ B $ and the starting point $ A $ coordinates, thus proving:

$$
\int_{L} \nabla \phi \cdot d\vec{r} = \phi(B) - \phi(A)
$$

No matter how complicated the inside is, as long as it satisfies such a condition, we only need to worry about the boundary values, which provides great convenience.

What should we do? Should we calculate it in three parts? It's very troublesome. In fact, it can be simplified by using what is called the Stokes' formula to transform it into a surface integral. We will first give the Stokes' formula to give you an idea, and we will elaborate later.
#### Solution Steps

##### 1. Curl Calculation (Curl)

Let the vector field $\mathbf{F} = (P, Q, R) = (z^2 - y^2, x^2 - z^2, y^2 - x^2)$. According to Stokes' theorem, $\oint_L \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_\Sigma (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$, calculate the curl:

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ z^2-y^2 & x^2-z^2 & y^2-x^2 \end{vmatrix}
$$

- **$ x $ components**:$\frac{\partial R}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z} = 2y - (-2z) = 2(y+z)$
    
- **$ y $ components**:$\frac{\partial P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x} = 2z - 0 = 2z $(Note that here $ R $ does not include $ x $, and $\mathbf{j}$ the term needs to be negative): it should be $ 2z - (-2x) = 2(z+x)$
    
- **$ z $ components**:$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x - (-2y) = 2(x+y)$
    

Obtained:$\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = (2(y+z), 2(z+x), 2(x+y))$.

##### 2. Selecting the Surface of Integration $\Sigma $ Take $\Sigma $ over the sphere $ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1 $ the part in the first octant. Its outward unit normal vector is $\mathbf{n} = (x, y, z)$.

Calculate $(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n}$:

$$
(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{n} = 2(y+z)x + 2(z+x)y + 2(x+y)z = 4(xy + yz + zx)
$$

##### 3. Simplification using symmetry

Since the surface $\Sigma $ is completely symmetric with respect to $ x, y, z $ the integral can be simplified to:

$$
I = \iint_\Sigma 4(xy + yz + zx) dS = 12 \iint_\Sigma xy \, dS
$$

##### 4. Coordinate Projection and Integration

On the sphere, using the parametric equation $ x = \sin\phi \cos\theta, y = \sin\phi \sin\theta, dS = \sin\phi \, d\phi d\theta $.

First octant range:$\theta \in [0, \pi/2], \phi \in [0, \pi/2]$.

$$
\iint_\Sigma xy \, dS = \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\theta \cos\theta \, d\theta \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^3\phi \, d\phi
$$

- **$\theta $ Part **:$\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\theta \cos\theta \, d\theta = \left[ \frac{1}{2}\sin^2\theta \right]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{1}{2}$
    
- **$\phi $ Part **:$\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^3\phi \, d\phi = \frac{2}{3}$(Using $\sin^3\phi = \sin\phi(1-\cos^2\phi)$ or Walli's Formula)
    

##### 5. Final Result

$$
I = 12 \times \left( \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} \right) = 12 \times \frac{1}{3} = \mathbf{4}
$$

It can be seen that although there is still some amount of calculation, we do not need to calculate three lines, which is quite comforting, at least in my opinion. ### Green's Formula

$$
\oint_{\partial D^+}Pdx+Qdy=\iint_D(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y})dxdy
$$

Next, we give the proof:
#### Stage One: Proof for Special Regions (Simply connected, convex regions)

We first prove one half of the formula on a **standard region**, for example one bounded in the $ y $ direction by the two graphs $ y = \phi_1(x)$ and $ y = \phi_2(x)$.

##### 1. Target Equation

Prove the component about $ P $ the component:

$$
\oint_{\partial D^+} P \, dx = \iint_D -P_y \, dx \, dy
$$

##### 2. Derivation Process

Using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we perform iterated integration on the double integral:

$$
\begin{aligned} \iint_D \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \, dx \, dy &= \int_a^b \left( \int_{\phi_1(x)}^{\phi_2(x)} \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \, dy \right) dx \\ &= \int_a^b [P(x, \phi_2(x)) - P(x, \phi_1(x))] \, dx \end{aligned}
$$

the line integral over the corresponding boundary curve $\oint_{\partial D^+} P \, dx $ Divided into four parts:

- **Bottom edge and top edge**: Along $ y=\phi_1(x)$ the integral of $\int_a^b P(x, \phi_1(x)) \, dx $; along $ y=\phi_2(x)$(reverse)is $-\int_a^b P(x, \phi_2(x)) \, dx $.
    
- **Side**:Since on the side $ dx = 0 $, the integral is 0.
    

Combining gives:

$$
\oint_{\partial D^+} P \, dx = -\iint_D P_y \, dx \, dy
$$

Similarly, it can be proven $\oint_{\partial D^+} Q \, dy = \iint_D Q_x \, dx \, dy $.

#### Second Stage: Decomposition of Complex Regions

If the region $ D $ has a complex shape (non-convex or non-standard), we cut it into several small standard regions using **auxiliary lines** $ D_1, D_2, \dots, D_n $.

- **Addition of Volume Integrals**:$\iint_D = \sum \iint_{D_i}$.
    
- **Cancellation of Line Integrals**:When you add the boundary integrals of all the small regions $\oint_{\partial D_i^+}$, the internal auxiliary lines are traversed twice, and in opposite directions, so the **internal integrals all cancel out**, leaving only the outermost boundary $\partial D^+$.

### Proof of Stokes' Theorem

Let $ P, Q, R \in C^1(S)$, and we want to prove:

$$
\oint_{L^+} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = \iint_{S^+} (\nabla \times \vec{F}) \cdot \vec{n}^+ \, dS
$$

#### 1. Proof of target decomposition

It is sufficient to prove the component form:

$$
\oint_{L^+} P \, dx = \iint_{S^+} (P_z \, dz \wedge dx - P_y \, dx \wedge dy)
$$

Here, $\wedge $ is the so-called **exterior derivative**, which will be briefly mentioned in later notes; for now, you can simply understand it as representing the direction $ dzdx $.

#### 2. Parametric expression

Introduce the parametric equation $(u, v) \to \vec{r}(u, v)$, where:

$$
\begin{cases} x = x(u, v) \\ y = y(u, v) \\ z = z(u, v) \end{cases}
$$

The corresponding normal vector differential $\vec{du} \wedge \vec{dv} \approx \vec{n}^+ \, dS $.

#### 3. Derivation process

Using the chain rule and Green's theorem:

$$
\begin{aligned} \oint_{L^+} P \, dx &= \oint_{L^{'+}} P(x_u \, du + x_v \, dv) \\ &= \oint_{L^{'+}} (P x_u) \, du + (P x_v) \, dv \\ &= \iint_{D'} \left[ (P x_v)_u - (P x_u)_v \right] \, du \, dv \\ &= \iint_{D'} (P_u x_v - P_v x_u) \, du \, dv \\ &= \iint_{D'} \left[ (P_x x_u + P_y y_u + P_z z_u) x_v - (P_x x_v + P_y y_v + P_z z_v) x_u \right] \, du \, dv \\ &= \iint_{D'} \left[ -P_y (x_u y_v - x_v y_u) + P_z (z_u x_v - z_v x_u) \right] \, du \, dv \\ &= \iint_{D'} (P_z B + (-P_y) C) \, du \, dv \end{aligned}
$$

Where:

- $ C = \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = x_u y_v - x_v y_u $-$ B = \frac{\partial(z, x)}{\partial(u, v)} = z_u x_v - z_v x_u $

By comparison, we know that

$$
\iint_{S^+} (P_z \, dz \wedge dx - P_y \, dx \wedge dy)
$$

is consistent. Similarly, it can be proven that the other components also satisfy this form, thus proving Stokes' Theorem.

### No curl, no divergence

Having no curl and no divergence has some nice properties, but for an arbitrary function, it often cannot directly satisfy no curl and no divergence. How do we apply the properties we obtained? In fact, we can perform a decomposition.

---

### Proof of Hodge Decomposition Theorem (Three-dimensional region $\Omega $)

#### 1. Setting the decomposition target

Let $\vec{F} \in C^1(\Omega)$, and we seek $\vec{F}_1, \vec{F}_2 $ such that:

1. **Decomposability**: $\vec{F} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 = \nabla u + \nabla \times \vec{G}$
    
2. **Field properties**: $\nabla \times \vec{F}_1 = 0 $ (no curl); $\nabla \cdot \vec{F}_2 = 0 $ and on the boundary $\vec{F}_2 \cdot \vec{n} = 0 $ (no source and tangential).
    
3. **Orthogonality**: $\iiint_{\Omega} \vec{F}_1 \cdot \vec{F}_2 \, dV = 0 $
    

#### 2. Construction process (Introducing Neumann boundary)

To determine $\vec{F}_1 = \nabla u $, we use the no-source property of $\vec{F}_2 $:

$$
\nabla \cdot \vec{F}_2 = \nabla \cdot (\vec{F} - \nabla u) = 0 \implies \nabla^2 u = \nabla \cdot \vec{F}
$$

This is the **Poisson equation** in $\Omega $. To ensure the uniqueness of the decomposition and eliminate boundary interference, we introduce the **Neumann boundary condition**:

$$
\left. \frac{\partial u}{\partial n} \right|_{\partial \Omega} = \nabla u \cdot \vec{n} = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{n}
$$

- **Existence**: According to the theory of partial differential equations, the solution $ u $ to the above boundary value problem exists and is unique up to an additive constant.
    
- **Corollary**: After determining $ u $, $\vec{F}_1 = \nabla u $ is uniquely determined, and thus $\vec{F}_2 = \vec{F} - \vec{F}_1 $ automatically satisfies $\nabla \cdot \vec{F}_2 = 0 $.
    

#### 3. Proof of Orthogonality (Core Calculation)

Verify that the $\vec{F}_1 $ inner product with $\vec{F}_2 $ in $ L^2 $ is 0:

$$
\begin{aligned} \iiint_{\Omega} \vec{F}_1 \cdot \vec{F}_2 \, dV &= \iiint_{\Omega} \nabla u \cdot \vec{F}_2 \, dV \\ &= \iiint_{\Omega} \left[ \nabla \cdot (u \vec{F}_2) - u (\nabla \cdot \vec{F}_2) \right] \, dV \\ &= \iint_{\partial \Omega} u (\vec{F}_2 \cdot \vec{n}) \, dS - \iiint_{\Omega} u (\nabla \cdot \vec{F}_2) \, dV \end{aligned}
$$

According to the construction conditions:

- From the Poisson equation, we have $\nabla \cdot \vec{F}_2 = 0 $, so the volume integral term is **0**.
    
- From the Neumann boundary condition, we have $\vec{F}_2 \cdot \vec{n} = (\vec{F} - \nabla u) \cdot \vec{n} = 0 $, so the surface integral term is **0**.
    

**Conclusion**: $\iiint_{\Omega} \vec{F}_1 \cdot \vec{F}_2 \, dV = 0 $, which proves the orthogonality of the decomposition.
