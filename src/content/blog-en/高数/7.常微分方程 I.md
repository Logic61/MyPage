## From Field Theory to Differential Equations
In field theory, we discussed so-called divergence-free and curl-free: 

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla u)=0,\ \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times G)=0
$$

and that a function can be nicely decomposed into some divergence and some curl:

$$
F=\nabla u+\nabla \times G
$$

### Maxwell's Equations
The differential form of the set of equations, like the thunderous $ Maxwell $ set, is actually written in the language of $\nabla $. This was also mentioned in field theory.

$$
\begin{cases}
\nabla \cdot E = \rho \\
\nabla \cdot B = 0 \\
\nabla \times E = -B_t \\
\nabla \times B = J + E_t
\end{cases}
$$

As mentioned above, we can try to decompose $ E $:

$$
E = \nabla (-U) + \nabla \times\int B\, dt
$$

Physically, it is probably the electrostatic field and the eddy current field, generated respectively by the gradient of the potential and the changing magnetic field.
Can we eliminate $ B $ to get an equation containing only $ E $?
#### Derivation of Wave Equation

##### 1. Basic Equations

The derivation starts from Maxwell's equations in a vacuum (or with no source term $ J=0, \rho=0 $):

- **Faraday's Law of Induction:** $\nabla \times E = -B_t $ - **Ampère-Maxwell Law:** $\nabla \times B = E_t $ (Note: The coefficients are simplified here, and it is assumed that there are no sources $ J=0 $)
    

##### 2. Mathematical Derivation Steps

Take the **Curl** of both sides of Faraday's law:

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla \times E) = -(\nabla \times B)_t
$$

**Left Side (using vector operator identities):**

$$
\nabla \times (\nabla \times E) = \nabla(\nabla \cdot E) - \Delta E
$$

> **Key Point:** In a source-free region (vacuum), due to $\nabla \cdot E = 0 $ (obtained from Gauss's Law), the left side simplifies to **$-\Delta E $**.

**Right Side (substituting Ampère's Law):**

$$
-(\nabla \times B)_t = -(E_t)_t = -E_{tt}
$$

##### 3. Final Conclusion: Wave Equation

Combining the left and right sides and canceling the negative sign, we get the law governing the evolution of electromagnetic waves in space:

$$
E_{tt} = \Delta E
$$

Expanding gives:

$$
E_{tt} = E_{xx} + E_{yy} + E_{zz}
$$

> **Physical Meaning:** This is a typical **Wave Equation**. It shows that the electric field (and magnetic field) can propagate in space in the form of a wave through the change of its own field, without needing a medium.

It can be seen that such an equation is an equation satisfied by the derivatives of $ E $, which is the differential equation in physical intuition. How to describe it mathematically? Let's start from a simple case—the ordinary differential equation, which is a differential equation without partial derivatives.

# Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)

## Basic Definition

Let $ x $ be an independent variable, $ y $ be the dependent variable, and $ y $ be its derivatives of various orders $ y', y'', \dots, y^{(n)}$.

**Definition ($ n $ Order ODE):**

$$
F(x, y, y', \dots, y^{(n)}) = 0 \quad (\star)
$$

An equation that satisfies this equation is called an **$ n $ Order Ordinary Differential Equation**.

**Definition (Solution):**

If the function $ y(x)$ satisfies the equation $(\star)$ on the interval $[a, b]$, then $ y(x)$ is called a **solution** to this equation on the interval $[a, b]$.

---

## 2. Example

### First-order model: Exponential decay

$$
m'(t) = -a m(t)
$$

- **Form of the Solution:** $ m(t) = C e^{-at} \quad (\forall C \in \mathbb{R})$ - **Characteristic:** The solution exists and is not unique (depending on the constant $ C $).
    

### Second-order model: Simple harmonic motion

$$
x''(t) = -k x(t)
$$

- **A solution that might be noticed:** $ x(t) = \sin(t)$ or $ x(t) = \cos(t)$ - **Noticing the linearity of the equation, we find a more general solution:** $ x(t) = C_1 \cos(t) + C_2 \sin(t) \quad (\forall C_1, C_2 \in \mathbb{R})$
    

This more general solution is called the general solution.

## Definition of General Solution

For a set of solutions $ y(x; C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n)$ containing $ n $ linearly independent arbitrary constants $ C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n $, if it satisfies the following two conditions:

1. **Independence:** The constants $ C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n $ are **linearly independent** on the interval $ x \in [a, b]$.
    
2. **Validity:** For every set of fixed $ C_1, \dots, C_n $, the function $ y(x, C_1, \dots, C_n)$ satisfies the equation $(\star)$ on $[a, b]$.
    

Then $ y(x; C_1, \dots, C_n)$ is called a **general solution** to the equation $(\star)$.
The function obtained after fixing the constants is also called a particular solution, for example, $\sin(t)$ in the simple harmonic motion example is obtained by setting the constants to $ 0,1 $.

---

## Constant Independence (Wronskian Determinant)

To determine whether a set of solutions can form a general solution, we need to introduce a test for linear independence:

**Definition (Constant Independence):**

For a set of functions $\psi(x, C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n)$, if $ C_1, \dots, C_n $ is linearly independent on $ x \in [a, b]$, the necessary and sufficient condition usually involves the **Wronskian determinant** being non-zero:

$$
\left| \frac{\partial(\psi, \psi', \psi'', \dots, \psi^{(n-1)})}{\partial(C_1, C_2, C_3, \dots, C_n)} \right| \neq 0, \quad x \in [a, b]
$$

> **Logical Closure:**
> 
> - **First-order equation** has one arbitrary constant $ C $.
>     
> - **Second-order equation** has two linearly independent arbitrary constants $ C_1, C_2 $.
>     
> - **n-th order equation** requires $ n $ linearly independent solutions to construct the general solution.
>     

We can verify whether the solution to simple harmonic motion satisfies the constant independence.

### Verification: Independence of Constants in the General Solution

**1. Setting the Function Form**

The family of solution functions for the second-order equation is given by:

$$
x(t, C_1, C_2) = C_1 \cos(t) + C_2 \sin(t), \quad \forall C_1, C_2 \in \mathbb{R}
$$

Taking the first derivative with respect to time $ t $:

$$
x'(t, C_1, C_2) = -C_1 \sin(t) + C_2 \cos(t)
$$

**2. Constructing the Determinant (A variation of the Wronskian idea)**

To verify the independence of $ C_1, C_2 $, calculate the determinant of the partial derivatives of the function vector with respect to the constant vector:

$$
\left| \frac{D(x, x')}{D(C_1, C_2)} \right| = \begin{vmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial C_1} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial C_2} \\ \frac{\partial x'}{\partial C_1} & \frac{\partial x'}{\partial C_2} \end{vmatrix}
$$

**3. Substitution and Calculation**

Substitute the partial derivative results into the matrix:

$$
= \begin{vmatrix} \cos(t) & \sin(t) \\ -\sin(t) & \cos(t) \end{vmatrix}
$$

Using the determinant calculation rule $ ad - bc $:

$$
= \cos^2(t) - (-\sin^2(t))
$$

$$
= \cos^2(t) + \sin^2(t)
$$

$$
= 1 \neq 0
$$

---

### Conclusion

Since the value of this determinant is **1** (never zero), according to the criterion, the constants $ C_1 $ and $ C_2 $ are **linearly independent** in the solution space.

This proves that $ x(t) = C_1 \cos(t) + C_2 \sin(t)$ is indeed the **general solution** to this second-order differential equation.

For the solution of a general equation, we can also view it as a function, and thus there are so-called implicit functions, and correspondingly, there are so-called general integrals.
## General Integral

### 1. Definition

For $ n $ ordinary differential equations $ F(x, y, y', \dots, y^{(n)}) = 0 \quad (\star)$, if its general solution is given by an implicit function equation:

$$
\Phi(x, y; C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n) = 0
$$

If this equation, under the premise of constant independence, can be reduced back to the original equation $(\star)$ through algebraic operations such as differentiation with respect to $ x $, then this implicit equation is called the **general integral** of the original equation.

> **Core Difference:**
> 
> - **General Solution**: Usually refers to the explicit function form $ y = f(x, C_1, \dots, C_n)$.
>     
> - **General Integral**: Refers to the implicit equation form, which cannot always be easily solved for the explicit $ y $.
>     

In fact, it means eliminating the influence of the derivative of $ y $, which means solving the differential.

---

### 2. Case Demonstration: Circle Family and Differential Equation

The blackboard demonstrates the conversion logic between the general integral and the differential equation using a geometric example:

- **Differential Equation:** $ x + y \cdot y' = 0 $ - **General Integral Guess:** $ x^2 + y^2 = C $ (This is a family of circles centered at the origin)
    

**Verification Process:**

Differentiating the implicit equation $ x^2 + y^2 = C $ with respect to $ x $:

1. $\frac{d}{dx}(x^2) + \frac{d}{dx}(y^2) = \frac{d}{dx}(C)$ 2.$ 2x + 2y \cdot y' = 0 $
    
3. Simplifying to: $ x + y \cdot y' = 0 $
    

**Conclusion:**

The implicit equation $ x^2 + y^2 = C $ is the general integral of the differential equation $ x + y \cdot y' = 0 $. If written as an explicit general solution, it is:

$$
y = \pm \sqrt{C - x^2}
$$

---

### 3. Geometric Intuition

In a coordinate system, this general integral represents a family of concentric circles. The differential equation $ y' = -\frac{x}{y}$ actually describes that the tangent slope at any point on the circle must be perpendicular to the line connecting that point to the origin (the radius).

Could there be solutions that fall outside the general solution? Or, is it possible that traversing the constants in the general solution cannot reach a certain solution?

## Definition: Singular Solution

If the function $ y(x)$ satisfies the original equation $(\star)$, but it is **not contained in the general solution** (i.e., it cannot be obtained by assigning any constant $ C $ from the general solution), then it is called a **singular solution** of the equation.

### Typical Case Analysis: $ y^2 + (y')^2 = 1 $

#### 1. General Solution and Verification

Given the differential equation:

$$
y(x)^2 + (y'(x))^2 = 1
$$

Its **general solution** form is:

$$
y(x) = \sin(x-c), \quad c \in \mathbb{R}
$$

**Constant Independence Verification:**

Judging whether it provides an effective degree of freedom by taking the partial derivative with respect to the constant $ c $:

$$
\partial_c y = -\cos(x-c) \not\equiv 0
$$

> **Core Logic:** Although this partial derivative is zero at certain specific points, it is **not identically zero** over the interval, so the constant $ c $ is independent.

---

#### 2. Singular Solutions

Observing the structure of the equation, two special constant solutions can be found that are not included in the above general solution family:

- **$ y(x) \equiv 1 $**
    
- **$ y(x) \equiv -1 $**
    

**Verification:** If $ y = \pm 1 $, then $ y' = 0 $. Substituting into the original equation: $(\pm 1)^2 + 0^2 = 1 $, the equation holds.

Since these two solutions cannot be obtained by assigning a value to $ c $ from the general solution (the function $\sin $ cannot be identically equal to 1), they are defined as the **singular solutions** of this equation.

---

#### Envelope and Singular Solution

##### Core Definition

For a **First Order Ordinary Differential Equation (1st Order ODE)**:

If a function $ y(x)$ at any point on its curve:

- **At every point** is tangent to **some solution** in the general solution family of the equation;
    
- And $ y(x)$ itself also satisfies the differential equation.
    

Then $ y(x)$ is a **singular solution** of the equation.

---

##### Why does "tangent" imply "loss of uniqueness"?

From a geometric perspective, the envelope curve has a peculiar property of "coincidence":

1. **Coordinates are the same:** At the point of tangency, the envelope curve and the general solution curve have the same $ y $ value.
    
2. **Equal Slopes:** Due to tangency, their derivatives $ y'$ are also completely identical.
    

**Inference:**

For the same initial point $(x_0, y_0)$, two different integral curves now appear (the envelope itself and the general solution curve passing through that point), and they have the same slope at that point. This indicates that the **Existence and Uniqueness Theorem** for differential equations is violated along this trajectory.

---

##### Discriminant Logic

- **General Solution Family:** $\Phi(x, y, C) = 0 $ - **Envelope Equation:** It is usually obtained by solving the following system of equations and eliminating the parameter $ C $:

$$
\begin{cases} \Phi(x, y, C) = 0 \\ \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial C} = 0 \end{cases}
$$

- **Verification:** If the envelope equation obtained is substituted into the original ODE and holds true, and it cannot be obtained by assigning values to $ C $, then it is an singular solution.

In fact, there are infinitely many singular solutions. For the example above, we can move a bit along $ y=-1 $, then move along the trigonometric function to $ y=1 $, and then move randomly... ## Initial Value Problem (Cauchy Problem)

For a $ n $ order ordinary differential equation, how to lock a unique trajectory through initial conditions.

### Definition

For $ n $ order ODE:$ F(x, y, y', \dots, y^{(n)}) = 0 $, find a particular solution $ y(x)$ such that it satisfies the following $ n $ initial conditions:

$$
\begin{cases} y(0) = y_0 \\ y'(0) = y_1 \\ \dots \\ y^{(n-1)}(0) = y_{n-1} \end{cases}
$$

Where $ y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{n-1}$ is the **given initial value**.

### Core Research

For the Cauchy problem, the three dimensions most concerned by mathematicians and physicists are:

1. **Existence:** Can such a solution actually be found?
    
2. **Uniqueness:** Is there only one solution? (This corresponds to whether the 'envelope/singular solution' discussed earlier will violate uniqueness).
    
3. **Long-term Behavior:** As the independent variable $ x $ (usually time $ t $) increases, does the solution tend to stabilize, diverge, or enter a chaotic state?

How to look at existence?
### Independence of General Solution Constants and Inverse Mapping

#### 1. Equation Form of Initial Conditions

For a general solution $ y(x, C_1, \dots, C_n)$ containing $ n $ arbitrary constants $ C_1, \dots, C_n $, given the initial value $(y_0, y_1, \dots, y_{n-1})$, we can establish the following system of equations:

$$
\begin{cases} y(0, C_1, \dots, C_n) = y_0 \\ y'(0, C_1, \dots, C_n) = y_1 \\ \dots \\ y^{(n-1)}(0, C_1, \dots, C_n) = y_{n-1} \end{cases}
$$

#### 2. Core Determination: Jacobi Determinant

To ensure that the above initial values **uniquely** lock down a set of constants $(C_1, \dots, C_n)$, it must be guaranteed that the mapping is locally invertible. The condition for this is that the Jacobi determinant is non-zero:

$$
\left| \frac{D(y, y', \dots, y^{(n-1)})}{D(C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n)} \right| \neq 0
$$

- **Mathematical Meaning:** This condition is equivalent to the **Independence** of the constants.
    
- **Geometric Intuition:** It guarantees that the mapping from the 'constant space' to the 'initial value space' is a **local diffeomorphism**. That is, for every set of reasonable initial values, we can uniquely find the corresponding constant solution by working backward.
    

#### 3. Perspective of the Inverse Mapping Theorem

A more abstract mapping description:

- Let the mapping $ g(\vec{C}) = \vec{u}$, where $\vec{C}$ is the constant vector, and $\vec{u}$ is the initial value vector.
    
- If the Jacobi determinant is non-zero, then according to the **Inverse Function Theorem**, an inverse mapping exists:

$$
\vec{C} = g^{-1}(\vec{u})
$$

- This means: **The reason the general solution can determine a particular solution through initial values is essentially because the independence of the constants guarantees the invertibility of the mapping.**

#### Limitations of the General Solution

The general solution is just the general pattern we expect. Besides the singular solutions mentioned above, we can consider:

$$
F(x,y,y')=0\text{and}G(x,y,y')=0
$$

If we multiply two equations, we get a new differential equation. These two equations can be unrelated, but their general solutions are both general solutions of the new equation. Two general solutions?!

# Specific Calculation

## Separable Variables

### 1. Basic Form

For a first-order ODE, if its form is as follows:

$$
y'(x) = f(x) \cdot g(y)
$$

### 2. Solution Steps

- **Finding Constant Solutions (Equilibrium Solutions):**
    
    Observe if $ g(y)$ has any zeros. That is, if there exists a constant $ c_0 $ such that $ g(c_0) = 0 $, then **$ y(x) = c_0 $ is a solution to the original equation**.
    
- **Separation of Variables:**
    
    If $ g(y) \neq 0 $ on some interval, the equation can be rewritten as:

$$
\frac{y'}{g(y)} = f(x)
$$

### 3. Principle Analysis

Let $ F'(x) = f(x)$, $ G'(y) = \frac{1}{g(y)}$ (assuming $ g(y) \neq 0 $).

Differentiating/integrating both sides of the separated equation with respect to $ x $ yields the implicit solution:

$$
G(y(x)) = F(x) + C
$$

If $ G $ has an inverse function, the explicit solution is:

$$
y(x) = G^{-1}(F(x) + C)
$$

**Verification:**

Using the chain rule for differentiation:

$$
y'(x) = (G^{-1})'(F(x) + C) \cdot F'(x) = \frac{1}{G'(G^{-1}(F(x)+C))} \cdot f(x) = g(y) \cdot f(x)
$$

---

## Example Demonstration

**Equation:** $ y' = -xy $ Here $ f(x) = -x $, $ g(y) = y $.

1. **Checking Zeros:**
    
    Let $ g(y) = y = 0 $, which gives **$ y \equiv 0 $ is a particular solution to the equation**.
    
2. **Separation of Variables (Assuming $ y \neq 0 $):**

$$
\frac{y'}{y} = -x
$$

3. **Integrating Both Sides:**

$$
\int \frac{1}{y} dy = \int -x dx
$$

$$
\ln|y| = -\frac{1}{2}x^2 + C
$$

To solve for $ y $, take the exponential of both sides of the equation:

$$
|y| = e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^2 + C} = e^C \cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^2}
$$

Let $\tilde{C} = e^C $ (since $ e^C > 0 $, then $\tilde{C} > 0 $), so:

$$
|y| = \tilde{C} e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^2}
$$

Remove the absolute value sign on the left, and introduce a sign (positive or negative) on the right:

$$
y = \pm \tilde{C} e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^2}
$$

At this point, the coefficient $\pm \tilde{C}$ can take any non-zero real number.

Notice the constant solution **$ y \equiv 0 $** that we found in step ①.

- If we allow the coefficient in the above equation to be **0**, then this equation can cover $ y \equiv 0 $.
    
- Redefine a new arbitrary constant $\hat{C}$, such that it includes positive numbers, negative numbers, and zero ($\hat{C} \in \mathbb{R}$).
    

Finally, the **General Solution** of the equation is written as:

$$
\mathbf{y(x) = \hat{C} e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^2}}
$$

##  Variable Substitution
### One
**Basic Model:**

For a differential equation of the form $ y' = f(ax + by + c)$:

1. **Let:** $ z = ax + by + c $ 2. **Differentiate:** $ z' = a + b \cdot y' = a + b \cdot f(z)$ 3. **Transform:** Obtain a separable variable equation in terms of $ z $: $ z' = a + b f(z)$
    

---

#### Typical Example Problem

**Equation:** $ y' = \sin(x + y + 1)$

**1. Variable Substitution**

Let $ z = x + y + 1 $, then we have $ z' = 1 + y'$.

Substituting into the original equation gives:

$$
z' = 1 + \sin(z)
$$

**2. Finding Equilibrium Solutions**

Consider the case of $ 1 + \sin(z) = 0 $:

When $ z = 2k\pi + \frac{3}{2}\pi $ ($ k \in \mathbb{Z}$), then $ z' = 0 $.

Substituting back to the original variables, we get a set of **linear solutions**:

**$ y(x) = 2k\pi + \frac{3}{2}\pi - x - 1 $**

**3. Calculating the General Solution (Separation of Variables Method)**

When $ 1 + \sin(z) \neq 0 $, integrate:

$$
\int \frac{dz}{1 + \sin(z)} = \int 1 dx
$$

- **Left Side Integration Derivation:**
    
    Using trigonometric identities, the integral is:

$$
-\frac{\cos(z)}{1 + \sin(z)} = \tan\left(\frac{z}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)
$$

_ (Note: Simplified using the half-angle formula)_
    
- **Result:**

$$
\tan\left(\frac{x + y + 1}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = x + C
$$

Further simplification yields:
    
    **$ y = 2 \arctan(x + C) + \frac{\pi}{2} - x - 1 $**
    

Notice that $ y $ can be added to $ 2k\pi $ without affecting the validity of the equation, so the actual general solution is:

$$
y = 2 \arctan(x + C) + \frac{\pi}{2} - x - 1+2k\pi
$$

---

#### Regarding the Solution

The distribution of the solution family for this equation:

- **Feature:** The solution curve is divided by a series of parallel lines with a slope of $-1 $ (i.e., the equilibrium solutions $ y = -x - 1 + 2k\pi + \frac{3}{2}\pi $).
    
- **Trend:** Between these "isolation bands," the general solution curve exhibits a periodic fluctuation. When $ x \to \infty $, the solution will approach these specific lines infinitely.

### Two, Homogeneous

**1. Identifying the Model**

When the differential equation can be expressed as a function of the ratio of the independent variable to the dependent variable:

$$
y' = f(x, y) = h\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)
$$

**2. Introducing an Intermediate Variable**

Let:

$$
u = \frac{y}{x} \implies y = x \cdot u
$$

**3. Differentiation Transformation**

Differentiate $ y = xu $ with respect to $ x $ (applying the product rule):

$$
y' = (xu)' = x \cdot u' + u
$$

**4. Establishing a New Equation**

Substitute the above result into the original equation $ y' = h(u)$:

$$
xu' + u = h(u)
$$

After simplification:

$$
u' = \frac{h(u) - u}{x}
$$

---

#### Logical Deconstruction

- **Essence:** The core of this transformation lies in "ratio-forming," which collapses a bivariate function $ f(x, y)$ into a univariate function $ h(u)$, thereby transforming the equation into a **separable variable** form:

$$
\frac{du}{h(u) - u} = \frac{dx}{x}
$$

- **Applicable Scenarios:** If every term in the equation has the same degree in $ x $ and $ y $ (homogeneous), then it must be convertible to the form $ h(y/x)$.
    
- **Potential Risk:** During subsequent integration, special attention must be paid to the case where the denominator $ h(u) - u = 0 $ is zero, which usually corresponds to a ray in the solution space (i.e., the equilibrium solution).
    

---

### Three, Fractional Linear Combination

#### I. Core Model

The equation form is:

$$
y' = f\left( \frac{a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1}{a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2} \right)
$$

The method of handling this equation depends on the geometric relationship between the two lines represented by the numerator and the denominator, i.e., whether the determinant $ D = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 \end{vmatrix}$ is zero.

---

#### II. Case (a): Intersecting Lines ($ D \neq 0 $)

When the two lines intersect, the constant terms $ c_1 $ and $ c_2 $ can be eliminated by **translating the coordinate origin** to the intersection point.

1. **Finding the Intersection Point:** Solve the linear system $\begin{cases} a_1 x + b_1 y + c_1 = 0 \\ a_2 x + b_2 y + c_2 = 0 \end{cases}$, obtaining the unique solution $(x_0, y_0)$.
    
2. **Coordinate Translation:** Let $ u = x - x_0, v = y - y_0 $.
    
3. **Equation Transformation:** Substituting into the original equation, the constant term disappears, and the equation becomes:

$$
v'(u) = f\left( \frac{a_1 u + b_1 v}{a_2 u + b_2 v} \right) = f\left( \frac{a_1 + b_1(v/u)}{a_2 + b_2(v/u)} \right)
$$

At this point, the equation is transformed into a standard **homogeneous equation**, which can be further solved using $ w = v/u $.
    

---

#### III. Case (b): Parallel Lines $ D = 0 $

When two lines are parallel, it means the coefficients are proportional: $(a_1, b_1) = \lambda(a_2, b_2)$.

1. **Simplifying the Ratio:** At this point, the linear parts of the numerator and the denominator are related.

$$
y' = f\left( \frac{(a x + b y) + c_1}{\lambda(a x + b y) + c_2} \right) = g(ax + by)
$$

2. **Variable Substitution:** We return to the simplest form by setting **$ z = ax + by $**.
    
3. **Result:** The equation is transformed into a separable variable form for calculation.
    

---

#### Summary and Observation

This demonstrates the idea of “**reduction**” in mathematical processing:

- If there are **intersection points**, we eliminate the constant term through “translation,” reducing it to a **homogeneous equation**.
    
- If there are **no intersection points** (parallel), we reduce it to a **one-variable function equation** through “overall substitution.”
    

Thus far, we have fully collected the three main techniques for variable substitution for first-order differential equations:

1. **$ y' = f(ax+by+c)$** (Overall Substitution)
    
2. **$ y' = h(y/x)$** (Homogenization Substitution)
    
3. **$ y' = f(\frac{L_1}{L_2})$** (Coordinate Translation/Parallel Substitution)

## First-Order Linear ODE (Linear in $ y, y'$)

**1. Basic Definition and Classification**

The standard form of the equation is:

- **Non-homogeneous equation:** $ y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$— denoted as $(*)$ - **Homogeneous equation:** $ y' + P(x)y = 0 $— denoted as $(**)$
    

**2. Theory of Solution Structure**

The most fascinating aspect of linear equations is the superposition of their solutions:

- **Linear Property of Homogeneous Solutions:** If $ y_1, y_2 $ are $(**)$ two solutions of $ C_1y_1 + C_2y_2 $ then their linear combination $ C\varphi(x)$ is also a solution. The general solution has the form, and **has no singular solutions**. This is easy to verify by substitution.
    
- **General Solution Structure Formula:**
    
    If $\hat{y}$ is a **particular solution** to the non-homogeneous equation $(*)$, and $\varphi $ is a **non-zero solution** to the corresponding homogeneous equation $(**)$, then the **general solution** of $(*)$ can be expressed as:

$$
y = \hat{y} + C\varphi
$$

> **Intuitive Understanding:** This is completely consistent with the logic in linear algebra that “the general solution of a non-homogeneous linear system = particular solution + general solution of the homogeneous system.”
    

---

###  Derivation of the solution for the homogeneous equation $(**)$

The process of solving the homogeneous equation using the **integrating factor method**:

1. **Constructing the Primitive Function:** Let $ F(x)$ satisfy $ F'(x) = P(x)$, which means $ F(x) = \int P(x)dx $.
    
2. **Introducing the Integrating Factor:** Multiply both sides of the equation $(**)$ by $ e^{F(x)}$ (which is clearly $ e^{F(x)} \neq 0 $):

$$
y'e^F + yF'e^F = 0
$$

3. **Using the Product Rule in Reverse:**

$$
(ye^F)' = 0
$$

4. **Integrating to get the conclusion:**

$$
ye^F = C \implies y(x) = Ce^{-F(x)}
$$

Thus, the general solution of the homogeneous equation is: **$ y(x) = C e^{-\int P(x)dx}$**

This actually aligns with intuition, just as when we guess the solution to $ y=e^{-x}$ using $ y'+y=0 $. We utilized the invariance of the derivative of the exponential function to construct the differential. Furthermore, since every step is an equivalent transformation, there are no singular solutions.

### Solving the non-homogeneous equation—Variation of Parameters

**1. Core Idea**

For the general solution $ y = Ce^{-F(x)}$ of the homogeneous equation $(**)$, we boldly assume that the general solution of the non-homogeneous equation $(*)$ has the same form, but replace the constant $ C $ with an unknown function $ C(x)$:

$$
y = C(x)e^{-F(x)}
$$

**2. Solving Process**

We continue to use the **integrating factor $ e^{F(x)}$**:

- **Original Equation:** $ y' + P(x)y = Q(x)$—$(*)$- **Multiplying by the Integrating Factor:** Multiply both sides by $ e^{F(x)}$, where $ F'(x) = P(x)$:

$$
y'e^F + yP(x)e^F = Q(x)e^F
$$

- **Combining the Left Side:** According to the reverse process of the product rule for derivatives:

$$
(ye^F)' = Q(x)e^F
$$

- **Integrating Both Sides:**

$$
ye^{F(x)} = \int_{x_0}^x Q(\tilde{x})e^{F(\tilde{x})}d\tilde{x} + C
$$

---

### II. General Formula for First-Order Linear ODE

Through the above derivations, we obtain the final **general solution formula**:

$$
y(x) = \left( \int_{x_0}^x Q(\tilde{x})e^{F(\tilde{x})} d\tilde{x} + C \right) e^{-F(x)}
$$

**Structural Decomposition:**

1. **Particular Solution Part:** $ e^{-F(x)} \int Q(x)e^{F(x)}dx $ 2. **Homogeneous Solution Part:** $ Ce^{-F(x)}$

This is also step-by-step equivalent, so there are no singular solutions; this is a good property of linear equations.

### Example: RL Circuit

**1. Circuit Components**

- **Source EMF:** $ E $- **Inductor:** $ L $- **Resistor:** $ R $
    

**2. Establishing the Equation**

According to Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL), the voltage balance equation for the circuit is:

$$
L \cdot I'(t) + R \cdot I(t) = E
$$

Setting the initial condition: $ I(0) = 0 $ (i.e., the circuit is connected at time $ t=0 $, with initial current of zero).

---

#### Mathematical Transformation and Solving

**1. Standardization**

To apply the formula for first-order linear ODE, divide both sides of the equation by $ L $:

$$
I' + \frac{R}{L}I = \frac{E}{L}
$$

Let the constant be $ P = \frac{R}{L}$, $ Q = \frac{E}{L}$, and the equation simplifies to the standard form:

$$
I' + PI = Q
$$

**2. Solving using the Integrating Factor Method**

- **Integrating Factor:** $ e^{Pt}$ - **Transformation:** $(I \cdot e^{Pt})' = Q e^{Pt}$ - **Integration:** $ I \cdot e^{Pt} = \frac{Q}{P} e^{Pt} + C $ - **General Solution Formula:** $ I(t) = \frac{Q}{P} + C e^{-Pt}$
    

---

#### Substituting physical quantities and initial conditions

**1. Substitute Physical Parameters**

Due to $\frac{Q}{P} = \frac{E/L}{R/L} = \frac{E}{R}$, the general solution is written as:

$$
I(t) = \frac{E}{R} + C e^{-\frac{R}{L}t}
$$

**2. Determine Arbitrary Constants**

Substitute the initial condition $ I(0) = 0 $:

$$
0 = \frac{E}{R} + C \implies C = -\frac{E}{R}
$$

**3. Final Particular Solution**

$$
I(t) = \frac{E}{R} \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{R}{L}t} \right)
$$

---

#### Physical significance evaluation

- **Transient Response:** The exponential term $ e^{-\frac{R}{L}t}$ describes the process of the current growing from zero. The presence of the inductance $ L $ generates a "back electromotive force," which impedes the sudden change in current.
    
- **Steady-State Current:** When $ t \to \infty $, the exponential term approaches $ 0 $, and the current approaches a stable value:

$$
I_{\text{steady}} = \frac{E}{R}
$$

At this point, the inductance acts like a short circuit, following the basic Ohm's law.

## Integrating Factor
### I. Introduction of the Problem: Total Differential Equation

**1. Basic Form**

Writing the differential equation in symmetric form:

$$
P(x, y)dx + Q(x, y)dy = 0
$$

**2. Test Condition (Exactness Test)**

If there exists a function $ u(x, y)$ such that $ du = P dx + Q dy = 0 $, then the equation is called a **Total Differential Equation** (or Exact Equation). Its general solution is directly:

$$
u(x, y) = C
$$

Based on the properties of total differentials, this requires satisfying:

$$
Q_x = P_y
$$

 (i.e., $\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}$)

---

### II. Non-Total Differential Equations and Integrating Factor

If $ N_x \neq M_y $, the equation is not a total differential equation. At this point, we seek a non-zero function $\mu(x, y)$ such that multiplying by it makes the equation "exact":

$$
\mu M dx + \mu N dy = 0 \implies (\mu N)_x = (\mu M)_y
$$

The $\mu $ here is called the **Integrating Factor**.

---

### III. Derivation of the Integrating Factor (Example using a One-Variable Factor)

Finding a general $\mu(x, y)$ usually involves partial differential equations, which is very difficult. A shortcut for finding a **one-variable integrating factor** (only dependent on $ x $ or $ y $):

**1. Expand the Test Expression:**

According to the product rule for differentiation, $(\mu N)_x = (\mu M)_y $ expands to:

$$
\mu N_x + \mu_x N = \mu M_y + \mu_y M
$$

After simplification:

$$
\mu(N_x - M_y) = -\mu_x N + \mu_y M
$$

**2. Assume $\mu = \mu(x)$:**

Then $\mu_y = 0 $, and the equation simplifies to:

$$
\mu(N_x - M_y) = -\mu_x N
$$

After transformation, we get a separable variable equation in terms of $\mu $:

$$
\frac{\mu_x}{\mu} = -\frac{N_x - M_y}{N}
$$

**3. Solution Conclusion:**

If the right-hand side term $\frac{M_y - N_x}{N}$ **is only a function of $ x $**, let it be $ F(x)$, then the integrating factor is:

$$
\mu(x) = e^{\int F(x) dx}
$$

---

### Supplementary Case: Testing for $\mu = \mu(y)$

If the integrating factor is only a function of $ y $, the derivation process is symmetric to $ x $:

1. **Test Condition:** Examine the expression $\frac{N_x - M_y}{M}$.
    
2. **Conclusion:** If this expression **only depends on $ y $**, let it be $ G(y)$, then:

$$
\frac{\mu_y}{\mu} = \frac{N_x - M_y}{M} = G(y)
$$

3. **Integrating Factor Formula:**

$$
\mu(y) = e^{\int G(y) dy}
$$

---

### II. Comprehensive Example Practice

**Equation:** $(2xy^2 - y)dx + (x + 3y^3)dy = 0 $ where $ M = 2xy^2 - y $, $ N = x + 3y^3 $.

**1. Exactness Check**

- $ M_y = 4xy - 1 $-$ N_x = 1 $ is clearly $ M_y \neq N_x $, so the equation is **not** a total differential equation.
    

**2. Finding the Integrating Factor (Trial and Error Process)**

- **Try $\mu(x)$:**
    
    Calculate $\frac{N_x - M_y}{N} = \frac{1 - (4xy - 1)}{x + 3y^3} = \frac{2 - 4xy}{x + 3y^3}$.
    
    The result contains $ y $, indicating that there is no integrating factor depending only on $ x $.
    
- **Try $\mu(y)$:**
    
    Calculate $\frac{N_x - M_y}{M} = \frac{1 - (4xy - 1)}{2xy^2 - y} = \frac{2(1 - 2xy)}{y(2xy - 1)} = -\frac{2}{y}$.
    
    The result **only depends on $ y $**! This satisfies the condition, so let $ G(y) = -\frac{2}{y}$.
    

**3. Calculate the Integrating Factor**

$$
\mu(y) = e^{\int -\frac{2}{y} dy} = e^{-2\ln|y|} = \frac{1}{y^2}
$$

**4. Transformation and Solving**

Multiply both sides of the equation by $\frac{1}{y^2}$, obtaining the new equation:

$$
\frac{2xy^2 - y}{y^2}dx + \frac{x + 3y^3}{y^2}dy = 0
$$

Simplified to:

$$
\left( 2x - \frac{1}{y} \right)dx + \left( \frac{x}{y^2} + 3y \right)dy = 0
$$

Verification shows that at this point, $ P_y = \frac{1}{y^2}$, $ Q_x = \frac{1}{y^2}$, has become a total differential equation.

**5. Final General Solution**

Integrate $ P $ with respect to $ x $, and integrate $ Q $ with respect to $ y $, and combine (paying attention to duplicates):

$$
u(x, y) = x^2 - \frac{x}{y} + \frac{3}{2}y^2 = C
$$

Additionally, notice that when $ y \equiv 0 $, $ M=0 $ is simultaneously $ dy=0 $, so the equation also holds at this point.

### Special Integrating Factor for Homogeneous Equations

**Core Theorem:**

For a homogeneous differential equation $ M(x, y)dx + N(x, y)dy = 0 $ (i.e., $ M, N $ is a homogeneous function of the same degree), if $ xM + yN \neq 0 $, then one integrating factor for this equation is:

$$
\mu(x, y) = \frac{1}{xM + yN}
$$

Proof:

#### 1. Core Tool: Euler's Homogeneous Function Theorem

Based on the properties of homogeneous functions, if $ f(x, y)$ is of degree $ n $ homogeneous, then it satisfies:

$$
x \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + y \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = n f
$$

Specifically for our function:

- (1) $ x M_x + y M_y = n M $- (2)$ x N_x + y N_y = n N $
    

---

#### 2. Determining the Goal

We need to prove that the equation after multiplying by $\mu $, which is $\frac{M}{xM+yN}dx + \frac{N}{xM+yN}dy = 0 $, satisfies the condition for total differential:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{M}{xM + yN} \right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \frac{N}{xM + yN} \right)
$$

---

#### 3. Step-by-step Partial Differentiation

**Left side (Partial derivative with respect to $ y $):**

Applying the quotient rule $\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$, let the denominator be $ D = xM + yN $:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \frac{M}{D} \right) = \frac{M_y (xM + yN) - M \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(xM + yN)}{D^2}
$$

Expanding the partial derivative term of the denominator $\frac{\partial D}{\partial y} = x M_y + N + y N_y $:

$$
\text{numerator} = M_y(xM + yN) - M(x M_y + N + y N_y)
$$

$$
= x M M_y + y M_y N - x M M_y - MN - y M N_y
$$

Canceling $ x M M_y $, we get:

$$
\text{numerator}_L = y M_y N - MN - y M N_y \quad \dots (\alpha)
$$

**Right side (Partial derivative with respect to $ x $):**

Similarly applying the quotient rule:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \frac{N}{D} \right) = \frac{N_x (xM + yN) - N \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(xM + yN)}{D^2}
$$

Expanding the partial derivative term of the denominator $\frac{\partial D}{\partial x} = M + x M_x + y N_x $:

$$
\text{numerator} = N_x(xM + yN) - N(M + x M_x + y N_x)
$$

$$
= x M N_x + y N N_x - MN - x N M_x - y N N_x
$$

Canceling $ y N N_x $:

$$
\text{numerator}_R = x M N_x - MN - x N M_x \quad \dots (\beta)
$$

---

#### 4. Using Euler's Theorem for Equivalent Transformation

Now we want to prove $(\alpha) = (\beta)$. Writing both expressions together and observing:

$$
y M_y N - MN - y M N_y \overset{?}{=} x M N_x - MN - x N M_x
$$

Canceling $-MN $ from both sides, we get:

$$
N(y M_y + x M_x) \overset{?}{=} M(y N_y + x N_x)
$$

At this point, substitute into **Euler's Homogeneous Function Theorem**:

- Left side $= N \cdot (n M) = n MN $ - Right side $= M \cdot (n N) = n MN $
    

**Equal on both sides!**

### II. Typical Example Problem Practice

**Equation:** $(x - y)dx + (x + y)dy = 0 $ where $ M = x - y, N = x + y $are both first-order homogeneous functions.

**1. Exactness Check**

$ N_x = 1, M_y = -1 $Because $ N_x \neq M_y $the equation is not an exact differential equation.

**2. Solution Perspective One: Homogenization Substitution (Review)**

The equation can be written as:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x - y}{x + y} = -\frac{1 - y/x}{1 + y/x}
$$

This is a typical $ h(y/x)$ form, which can be solved using $ u = y/x $.

**3. Solution Perspective Two: Constructing a Special Integrating Factor**

Using the formula on the right side of the blackboard:

- **Denominator Calculation:** $ xM + yN = x(x - y) + y(x + y) = x^2 - xy + xy + y^2 = x^2 + y^2 $
    
- **Locking Factor:** **$\mu = \frac{1}{x^2 + y^2}$**
    

**4. Transformation and Integration**

Multiply $\mu $ back into the original equation:

$$
\frac{x - y}{x^2 + y^2}dx + \frac{x + y}{x^2 + y^2}dy = 0
$$

Splitting the terms:

$$
\left( \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}dx + \frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}dy \right) + \left( \frac{x dy - y dx}{x^2 + y^2} \right) = 0
$$

**5. Final General Solution**

- The first part is $\frac{1}{2}d(\ln(x^2 + y^2))$ - The second part is $ d(\arctan\frac{y}{x})$
    
    Combining gives:

$$
u(x, y) = \frac{1}{2}\ln(x^2 + y^2) + \arctan\frac{y}{x} = C
$$
