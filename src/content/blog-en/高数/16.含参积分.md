### The Continuity Theorem for Parametric Integrals

#### 1. Core Problem and Motivation

In calculus, we often encounter functions of the form $ h(y) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx $. A very natural question is: **If the integrand $ f(x, y)$ is continuous, is the resulting integral function $ h(y)$ also continuous?**

If $ h(y)$ is continuous, it means that for any $ y_0 $, there exists $\lim_{y \to y_0} h(y) = h(y_0)$. Substituting the definition of $ h(y)$, this equation is actually:

$$
\lim_{y \to y_0} \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} \left( \lim_{y \to y_0} f(x, y) \right) \, dx
$$

This is the **problem of interchanging the limit operator ($\lim $) and the integral operator ($\int $)**. To ensure the validity of this exchange, we need a rigorous mathematical proof.

#### 2. Theorem Statement

> **Theorem**: Let the function $ f(x, y)$ be **Continuous** on the closed rectangular region $ R = [a, b] \times [c, d]$. Then the new function defined by the integral
> 
>

$$
h(y) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx
$$

> 
> is also continuous on the interval $[c, d]$, i.e., $ h(y) \in C[c, d]$.

#### 3. Proof Idea and Rigorous Derivation

**Motivation**: To prove that $ h(y)$ is continuous at $ y_0 $, we need to control that the difference between the two integral values, $|h(y) - h(y_0)|$, can be arbitrarily small when $ y $ approaches $ y_0 $. Since the integration interval is fixed, if we can make the difference of the integrand $|f(x, y) - f(x, y_0)|$ shrink overall, we can control the error through integration.

The obstacle encountered here is,$ x $ is in $[a, b]$ the variation. Fortunately, a continuous function of two variables on a compact set (a closed rectangle) has **uniform continuity**, which means that the control of the error can be done "independent of" $ x $ the specific value taken".

**Rigorous Derivation Steps**:

1. **Introducing Uniform Continuity**: Because $ f(x, y)$ it is continuous on the closed rectangle $[a, b] \times [c, d]$ and by Cantor's Theorem, it must be **uniformly continuous** over the entire region.
    
2. **Quantifying the Error**: By the definition of uniform continuity, for any given $\varepsilon > 0 $, there must exist one that only depends on $\varepsilon $ Related $\delta > 0 $that when two points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ the distance between them is less than $\delta $ then the difference of their function values is less than $\varepsilon $.
    
    that is: when $|(x_1, y_1) - (x_2, y_2)| < \delta $ there is $|f(x_1, y_1) - f(x_2, y_2)| < \varepsilon $.
    
3. **Specializing to parameters $ y $**We keep $ x $ the coordinates the same, only let $ y $ Approximation $ y_0 $For any $ y \in [c, d]$if $|y - y_0| < \delta $then for the interval $[a, b]$ all$ x $there exists:

$$
|f(x, y) - f(x, y_0)| < \varepsilon
$$

4. **The difference in the magnified integral value**: Using the linearity of the integral and the absolute value inequality, we estimate the target difference:

$$
|h(y) - h(y_0)| = \left| \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx - \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y_0) \, dx \right|
$$

$$
\le \int_{a}^{b} |f(x, y) - f(x, y_0)| \, dx
$$

5. **Amplifying the Difference of the Integrals**: Using the linearity of the integral and the absolute value inequality, we estimate the target difference:

$$
\le \int_{a}^{b} \varepsilon \, dx = \varepsilon (b - a)
$$

Since $ b - a $ is a constant, when $\varepsilon \to 0 $ then,$\varepsilon(b-a) \to 0 $.
    

This completes the proof, showing that under the condition of overall continuity, the interchange of the limit and the integral is safe:

$$
\lim_{y \to y_0} \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} \left( \lim_{y \to y_0} f(x, y) \right) \, dx
$$

### A Classic Counterexample: When Continuity Breaks Down

#### 1. Motivation

The rigor of mathematics is not only reflected in "when it can be interchanged," but also in "why it cannot be interchanged if the conditions are not met." The theorem above requires $ f(x, y)$ continuity on a closed region that **includes the limit point**. If the parameter $ y $ limit point (for example, $ y \to 0 $) causes the function to lose continuity locally, then can the limit and integral signs still be easily interchanged?

The counterexample below shows what can go wrong if we interchange the operators without checking those conditions.

#### 2. Construction of the Counterexample

Consider a parameter-dependent integral on $ x \in [0, 1]$, with $ y > 0 $ approaching $ 0 $:

$$
h(y) = \int_{0}^{1} \frac{x}{y^2} e^{-\frac{x^2}{y^2}} \, dx
$$

Our goal is to examine when $ y \to 0 $ the behavior of this function.

#### 3. Comparing the Two Orders of Operations

We want to compare two completely different orders of operations:

- **Path A (Limit first, then integrate)**: Assume we can move the limit inside the integral.
    
- **Path B (Integrate first, then take the limit)**: Strictly following the mathematical definition, first calculate $ h(y)$ the analytical expression, and then look at its limit.
    

##### We want to compare two completely different orders of operations:

**Steps**: First, without looking at the integral, we examine the integrand function $ f(x, y) = \frac{x}{y^2} e^{-\frac{x^2}{y^2}}$ when $ y \to 0 $ the point.

- **When $ x > 0 $ **: Let $ t = \frac{x^2}{y^2}$.
When $ y \to 0 $ then,$ t \to +\infty $.
    
    the original expression can be seen as $\frac{1}{x} \cdot t e^{-t}$.
According to L'Hôpital's rule, the rate of exponential growth is much faster than linear growth, therefore $\lim_{t \to +\infty} t e^{-t} = 0 $.
    
- **When $ x = 0 $ at the time of **:** the numerator is directly $ 0 $, so the function value is always $ 0 $, and the limit is naturally $ 0 $.
    

**Conclusion**:
At every point in $ x \in [0,1]$ the limit of the function is $ 0 $:

$$
\lim_{y \to 0} f(x, y) = 0
$$

If we blindly swap the signs, we will get:

$$
\text{Incorrect result} = \int_{0}^{1} \left( \lim_{y \to 0} f(x, y) \right) \, dx = \int_{0}^{1} 0 \, dx = \mathbf{0}
$$

##### Path B: First calculate the integral (the true $ h(y)$)

**Steps**: We will first calculate this definite integral diligently. Notice that the structure of the integrand is very convenient, making it extremely suitable for using the **Substitution Rule**.

Observation: $ d\left(-\frac{x^2}{y^2}\right) = -\frac{2x}{y^2} \, dx $, which means $\frac{x}{y^2} \, dx = -\frac{1}{2} d\left(-\frac{x^2}{y^2}\right)$.

We directly perform the indefinite integration by completing the square:

$$
\int \frac{x}{y^2} e^{-\frac{x^2}{y^2}} \, dx = -\frac{1}{2} \int e^{-\frac{x^2}{y^2}} \, d\left(-\frac{x^2}{y^2}\right) = -\frac{1}{2} e^{-\frac{x^2}{y^2}}
$$

Now, substitute the limits of integration $[0, 1]$:

$$
h(y) = \left[ -\frac{1}{2} e^{-\frac{x^2}{y^2}} \right]_{x=0}^{x=1} = \left( -\frac{1}{2} e^{-\frac{1}{y^2}} \right) - \left( -\frac{1}{2} e^{0} \right)
$$

$$
h(y) = \frac{1}{2} \left( 1 - e^{-\frac{1}{y^2}} \right)
$$

Now, we consider the limit of the integral $ h(y)$ of $ y \to 0 $ the:

When $ y \to 0 $, $\frac{1}{y^2} \to +\infty $, therefore $-\frac{1}{y^2} \to -\infty $, leading to $ e^{-\frac{1}{y^2}} \to 0 $.

$$
\lim_{y \to 0} h(y) = \frac{1}{2} (1 - 0) = \mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}
$$

#### 4. Conclusion and Critical Summary

We compare the results of the two approaches:

- **Limit first, then integrate (Path A)**: $ 0 $
    
- **Integrate first, then take the limit (Path B, True Value)**: $\frac{1}{2}$
    

**Key Conclusion: $ 0 \neq \frac{1}{2}$, the limit sign and the integral sign cannot be interchanged here.**

**Why is this? (Essential Analysis)**

If we graph the function $ f(x, y)$ as $ y $ decreases, we observe a phenomenon: Although the function value eventually collapses to $ 0 $ at every fixed $ x > 0 $; near the right side of $ x=0 $, the function bulges into a huge "peak". As $ y $ approaches $ 0 $, this peak becomes narrower and higher.

Despite the peak becoming narrower, the **area** enclosed beneath it stubbornly approaches $\frac{1}{2}$. This phenomenon, where the overall integral does not approach $ 0 $ due to drastic local fluctuations, is called **Non-uniform Convergence** in higher mathematics. It precisely proves that: **Without the guarantee of continuity (or uniform convergence) over an entire closed region, the interchange of symbols in calculus will fail.**

### Order Interchange (Iterated Integral)

For a continuous function $ f(x, y)$ defined on the rectangular region $ R = [a, b] \times [c, d]$, is the result of integrating with respect to $ x $ first and then with respect to $ y $ equal to the result of integrating with respect to $ y $ first and then with respect to $ x $?

#### 1. Concept Introduction and Definition

Let $ f(x, y) \in C([a, b] \times [c, d])$, $ f $ which is continuous on this closed rectangle. We define a single-variable function $ h(y)$that eliminates $ x $ by applying the integral $ x $ the variable change:

$$
h(y) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx
$$

#### 2. Core Theorem (Fubini's Theorem)

If $ f(x, y)$ is continuous, then $ h(y)$ is also continuous on $[c, d]$, and satisfies:

$$
\int_{c}^{d} h(y) \, dy = \int_{c}^{d} \left( \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx \right) dy = \int_{a}^{b} \left( \int_{c}^{d} f(x, y) \, dy \right) dx
$$

#### 3. Construction Motivation and Intuitive Understanding

Draw the two-dimensional plane into a fine grid and calculate the Riemann sum $\sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^m f(x_i, y_j) \Delta x_i \Delta y_j $. This matrix-like grid reveals the essence of integral interchange.

When facing the volume of a curved pillar in three-dimensional space, integration is actually an art of "slicing and summing".

- **First $ x $ then $ y $** means that we first slice the rectangular region into thin "slats" parallel to the $ x $ axis, calculate the cross-sectional area under each slat $ h(y)$, and then accumulate these cross-sectional areas along the direction of the $ y $ axis.
    
- **First $ y $ then $ x $** means rotating the slicing direction by 90 degrees, first calculating the cross-sectional area parallel to the $ y $ axis, and then accumulating along the $ x $ axis.
    

Because the grid is finite, the sum of the small columns is obviously equal whether we sum the rows first or the columns first. When the grid is infinitely subdivided approaching the differential, this discrete exchange naturally flows into the exchange of continuous integral signs. **Confidence Rating: High**. Because continuity guarantees the uniqueness of the limit of the Riemann sum.

### Interchange of Differential and Integral Signs

A practical tool for integrals with parameters—how to differentiate an integral structure.

#### Core Theorem (Leibniz Integral Rule)

Let $ f(x, y) \in C([a, b] \times [c, d])$, and its partial derivative with respect to the parameter $ y $, $ f_y(x, y)$, is also continuous in the region.

If we define $ h(y) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx $, then $ h(y)$ is differentiable, and its derivative can directly pass through the integral sign:

$$
h'(y) = \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx \right) = \int_{a}^{b} \frac{\partial}{\partial y} f(x, y) \, dx
$$

#### Construction Motivation: Why do this?

In traditional calculus, the derivative (the rate of change in the micro-scale) and the integral (the macro-scale accumulation) are inverse operators. However, when the derivative acts on the "parameter variable" $ y $, and the integral acts on the "integration variable" $ x $, these two operators, due to acting on different dimensions, establish a kind of parallelism between them.

We want to know how the total quantity represented by the integral changes when the entire environment (parameter variable $ y $) undergoes a small perturbation. If we can "throw" the differentiation sign inside the integral sign, we can first observe the rate of change of every small local part with respect to $ y $, and then accumulate these rates of change.

The proof is as follows:
**By using the variable limit integral as a bridge, we cleverly avoided using the definition directly to find the limit** $\Delta y \to 0 $ The tedious process of approximation**.

#### Proof Steps and Logical Chain

**Step 1: Reconstructing the function using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**

Since $ f_y(x, y)$ is continuous, by the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we can express the original function $ f(x, y)$ as the integral of its partial derivative over the interval $[c, y]$ plus an initial value. That is:

$$
f(x, y) = \int_{c}^{y} f_y(x, s) \, ds + f(x, c)
$$

_Note: Here, we introduce an auxiliary variable $ s $ as the dummy variable of integration, and $ y $ becomes the upper limit. For easier understanding, this can be analogized to $ F(x) = \int_a^x F'(t)dt + F(a)$ in single-variable calculus._

**Step 2: Substituting the reconstructed expression into $ h(y)$**

We substitute the integral expression of $ f(x, y)$ into the original function $ h(y) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx $:

$$
h(y) = \int_{a}^{b} \left( \int_{c}^{y} f_y(x, s) \, ds + f(x, c) \right) dx
$$

**Step 3: Linearly Decomposing the Integral Term**

Using the linearity property of integrals, we split the addition inside into two independent integrals:

$$
h(y) = \int_{a}^{b} \left( \int_{c}^{y} f_y(x, s) \, ds \right) dx + \int_{a}^{b} f(x, c) \, dx
$$

**Step 4: Applying the "Order Interchange Theorem" from Part I**

Carefully observe the first term $\int_{a}^{b} \left( \int_{c}^{y} f_y(x, s) \, ds \right) dx $. Due to the continuity of $ f_y $, it perfectly satisfies the exchange law that we proved in the first part. We interchange the order of integration for $ x $ and $ s $:

$$
h(y) = \int_{c}^{y} \left( \int_{a}^{b} f_y(x, s) \, dx \right) ds + \int_{a}^{b} f(x, c) \, dx
$$

**Step Five: Differentiating with respect to both sides of $ y $**

Now, we differentiate with respect to the variable $ y $ on both sides of the equation:

$$
\frac{d}{dy} h(y) = \frac{d}{dy} \left[ \int_{c}^{y} \left( \int_{a}^{b} f_y(x, s) \, dx \right) ds \right] + \frac{d}{dy} \left[ \int_{a}^{b} f(x, c) \, dx \right]
$$

Let's analyze the derivative of the right side term by term:

1. For the second term $\int_{a}^{b} f(x, c) \, dx $, the bounds $ a, b $ and the input $ c $ are constant. The integral is therefore constant in $ y $, so its derivative is zero: $\frac{d}{dy}[\text{constant}] = 0 $.
    
2. For the first term, it perfectly matches the **differentiation formula for a variable limit integral** (which is the hint written at the bottom right of the blackboard: $\left(\int_a^x f(t)dt\right)' = f(x)$). When the outer $\frac{d}{dy}$ encounters the integral sign $\int_{c}^{y}$ with an upper limit of $ y $, the two cancel each other out, and the $ s $ inside the integrand is automatically replaced by $ y $.
    

Thus, we finally arrive elegantly:

$$
h'(y) = \int_{a}^{b} f_y(x, y) \, dx
$$

That is:

$$
\frac{d}{dy} \left( \int_{a}^{b} f(x, y) \, dx \right) = \int_{a}^{b} \frac{\partial}{\partial y} f(x, y) \, dx
$$

**Done.**

### Example 1: Cleverly constructing the order exchange of a double integral

#### 1. Problem restatement

Calculate the definite integral:

$$
\int_{0}^{1} \frac{x^2 - x}{\ln x} \, dx
$$

#### 2. Motivation and construction idea

The difficulty in directly solving this integral lies in the $\ln x $ in the denominator. When differentiating, we know that differentiating the power function $ x^y $ with respect to $ y $ yields $\ln x $ (i.e., $\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^y) = x^y \ln x $). Conversely, if we can see $\frac{x^2 - x}{\ln x}$ as the result of integrating some function with respect to $ y $, we can eliminate $\ln x $.

Therefore, our **motivation** is: to use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to 'reduce' the combination of the numerator and denominator into an integral with respect to a new variable $ y $, thereby extending the single-variable integral to a double integral, and then simplifying it by exchanging the order of integration.

#### 3. Complete derivation process

**Step One: Creating from nothing, converting a single-variable function into a variable limit integral**

Observe the numerator $ x^2 - x $, which can be seen as the difference of $ x^y $ from $ y=1 $ to $ y=2 $. Using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$
\frac{x^2 - x}{\ln x} = \frac{1}{\ln x} \left[ x^y \right]_{y=1}^{y=2} = \frac{1}{\ln x} \int_{1}^{2} \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^y) \, dy = \frac{1}{\ln x} \int_{1}^{2} x^y \ln x \, dy
$$

Since $\ln x $ is independent of the integration variable $ y $, we can eliminate the $\ln x $ in the denominator:

$$
\frac{x^2 - x}{\ln x} = \int_{1}^{2} x^y \, dy
$$

**Step Two: Substituting into the original integral to construct a double integral**

Placing the above result back into the original integral gives an iterated integral:

$$
\int_{0}^{1} \frac{x^2 - x}{\ln x} \, dx = \int_{0}^{1} \left( \int_{1}^{2} x^y \, dy \right) dx
$$

**Step Three: Exchanging the order of integration (Core step)**

Since the integrand $ f(x,y) = x^y $ is continuous over the region $[0, 1] \times [1, 2]$, it satisfies the integral exchange law. We change the order of integration from first $ y $ then $ x $ to **first $ x $ then $ y $**:

$$
= \int_{1}^{2} \left( \int_{0}^{1} x^y \, dx \right) dy
$$

**Step Four: Integrating with respect to $ x $ first**

When integrating with respect to $ x $ at this point, $ y $ is treated as a constant. This is a simple power function integral:

$$
\int_{0}^{1} x^y \, dx = \left[ \frac{1}{y+1} x^{y+1} \right]_{x=0}^{x=1} = \frac{1}{y+1}
$$

**Step Five: Finally integrating with respect to $ y $**

Substitute the result back into the outer integral:

$$
= \int_{1}^{2} \frac{1}{y+1} \, dy = \left[ \ln(y+1) \right]_{1}^{2} = \ln 3 - \ln 2 = \ln \frac{3}{2}
$$

**Final Answer**: $\ln \frac{3}{2}$

### Example 2: Solving a parameter integral by differentiating inside the integral sign (Variation of Poisson's Integral)

#### 1. Problem restatement

Let the parameter-dependent integral be:

$$
I(r) = \int_{0}^{\pi} \ln(1 - 2r\cos x + r^2) \, dx
$$

Find the value of the integral $ I(1)$ when $ r=1 $.

#### 2. Motivation and construction idea

It is extremely difficult to integrate this expression directly with respect to $ x $. Since the expression contains an explicit parameter $ r $, if we differentiate with respect to the parameter $ r $, using **Leibniz's rule** to pass the derivative operator inside the integral sign, the derivative of the logarithm will become a rational function, which might greatly simplify the integrand.

Therefore, our **motivation** is: to first find the derivative of the function $ I'(r)$, and by observing the behavior of the derivative, to infer the structure of the original function $ I(r)$.

#### 3. Complete derivation process

**Step One: Differentiating with respect to the parameter $ r $ inside the integral sign**

Assume $(x, r) \in [0, \pi] \times [0, c]$ (where $ c < 1 $). By Leibniz's formula:

$$
I'(r) = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \ln(1 - 2r\cos x + r^2) \, dx = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{2r - 2\cos x}{1 - 2r\cos x + r^2 } \, dx
$$

**Step Two: Calculating the derivative integral (Finding the value of $ I'(r)$)**

##### 1. Splitting the integral term and simplifying the motivation

To make the form of the integrand easier to handle, we first complete the numerator to a structure related to the denominator:

$$
2r - 2\cos x = \frac{1}{r}(2r^2 - 2r\cos x) = \frac{1}{r}(1 - 2r\cos x + r^2 + r^2 - 1)
$$

After this splitting, the original integral can be split into two parts:

$$
I'(r) = \frac{1}{r} \int_{0}^{\pi} 1 \, dx + \frac{r^2 - 1}{r} \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{1}{1 - 2r\cos x + r^2} \, dx
$$

$$
I'(r) = \frac{\pi}{r} + \frac{r^2 - 1}{r} \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{1}{1 - 2r\cos x + r^2} \, dx
$$

The current core task is to solve the typical trigonometric rational integral on the right:

$$
J = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{1}{1 - 2r\cos x + r^2} \, dx
$$

##### 2. Solving $ J $ using the universal formula

**Step One: Introducing a substitution**

Let $ t = \tan \frac{x}{2}$.

When $ x $ changes from $ 0 $ to $\pi $, $ t $ changes from $ 0 $ to $+\infty $.

From trigonometric identities, we know:

$$
\cos x = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}, \quad dx = \frac{2}{1 + t^2} \, dt
$$

**Step Two: Substituting into the integral expression**

Placing the above substitution into the integral $ J $:

$$
J = \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{1}{1 - 2r\left(\frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}\right) + r^2} \cdot \frac{2}{1 + t^2} \, dt
$$

Multiplying the numerator and denominator by $(1 + t^2)$ to eliminate the complex fraction:

$$
J = \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{2}{(1 + r^2)(1 + t^2) - 2r(1 - t^2)} \, dt
$$

Combining the denominator according to the power of $ t $:

$$
\text{Incorrect Guess} = (1 + r^2 + 2r)t^2 + (1 + r^2 - 2r) = (1 + r)^2 t^2 + (1 - r)^2
$$

So the integral becomes:

$$
J = \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{2}{(1 + r^2 + 2r)t^2 + (1 - r)^2} \, dt = \int_{0}^{+\infty} \frac{2}{(1 + r)^2 t^2 + (1 - r)^2} \, dt
$$

**Step Three: Extracting the coefficient and using the standard integral formula**

This is a standard integral form for arctangent ($\arctan $). To apply $\int \frac{1}{a^2 u^2 + b^2}du = \frac{1}{ab}\arctan(\frac{au}{b})$, we find the corresponding coefficients:

Here, $ a = 1 + r $, $ b = 1 - r $ (because $ r \in [0, 1)$, so $ 1 - r > 0 $).

$$
\begin{aligned} J &= 2 \cdot \left[ \frac{1}{(1 + r)(1 - r)} \arctan\left( \frac{1 + r}{1 - r} t \right) \right]_{0}^{+\infty} \\ &= \frac{2}{1 - r^2} \left( \lim_{t \to +\infty} \arctan\left( \frac{1 + r}{1 - r} t \right) - \arctan(0) \right) \end{aligned}
$$

When $ t \to +\infty $, due to $\frac{1 + r}{1 - r} > 0 $, the inside of the parentheses tends to $+\infty $, therefore $\arctan(+\infty) = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

$$
J = \frac{2}{1 - r^2} \cdot \left( \frac{\pi}{2} - 0 \right) = \frac{\pi}{1 - r^2}
$$

##### 3. Substitute back into the original expression, witnessing the elimination of the miracle

Now we substitute the calculated $ J = \frac{\pi}{1 - r^2}$ back into the initial $ I'(r)$ expression:

$$
\begin{aligned} I'(r) &= \frac{\pi}{r} + \frac{r^2 - 1}{r} \cdot J \\ &= \frac{\pi}{r} + \frac{r^2 - 1}{r} \cdot \left( \frac{\pi}{1 - r^2} \right) \end{aligned}
$$

Notice the coefficient of the second term, $\frac{r^2 - 1}{1 - r^2} = -1 $, therefore:

$$
I'(r) = \frac{\pi}{r} - \frac{\pi}{r} = 0
$$

**Conclusion**: After rigorous algebraic manipulation, we eliminated all trigonometric functions, finally proving that the derivative $ I'(r)$ is indeed **identically equal to 0** on the interval $ r \in (0, 1)$.

$$
I'(r) \equiv 0, \quad r \in [0, 1)
$$

_ (Motivational explanation: A derivative that is identically zero means the function $ I(r)$ is a **constant function** in this interval, not changing with the variation of $ r $.)_

**Step Three: Determining the Constant Value (Using the Special Value Method)**

Since $ I(r) \equiv I(0)$ holds for all $ r \in [0, 1)$, we only need to substitute $ r=0 $ into the original integral to lock down this constant:

$$
I(0) = \int_{0}^{\pi} \ln(1 - 0 + 0) \, dx = \int_{0}^{\pi} \ln(1) \, dx = \int_{0}^{\pi} 0 \, dx = 0
$$

From this, we get:

$$
I(r) \equiv 0, \quad \forall r \in [0, 1)
$$

However, this cannot be directly extended to $ I(1)$, because this corresponds to a singularity, which requires studying improper integrals with parameters.

# Generalized Integrals with Parameters: Uniform Convergence and Test Criteria

## I. Cognitive Bridge: The Parallel Universe of Integrals and Series

The key to understanding generalized integrals with parameters lies in the function term series, by applying the **method of translation analogy**.

| **Conceptual Dimension** | **Function Term Series** | **Generalized Integral with Parameters** |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Local Truncation** | Partial Sum: $ S_n(y) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k(y)$ | Variable Upper Limit Integral: $ S_A(y) = \int_{a}^{A} f(x, y) \, dx $ |
| **Limit Approximation** | Letting the number of terms go to infinity: $\lim_{n \to \infty} S_n(y) = S(y)$ | Letting the upper limit go to infinity: $\lim_{A \to +\infty} S_A(y) = S(y)$ |

**Construction Motivation**:

Just as the partial sums $ S_n(y)$ can converge pointwise or uniformly as $ n \to \infty $, the truncated integrals $ S_A(y)$ can approach $ S(y)$ at rates that depend on the parameter as $ A \to +\infty $. To justify exchanging integration and differentiation on the infinite interval, we need a precise definition of uniform convergence.

## II. The Definition in $\varepsilon-A $ Terms

### 1. Definition

Suppose the improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, y) \, dx $ converges pointwise to $ S(y)$ for $ y \in Y $.

For any given $\varepsilon > 0 $, there exists a constant that only depends on $\varepsilon $ , and not on $ y $ a constant $ A_0 \ge a $, such that when the upper limit of integration $ A > A_0 $ is$ y \in Y $ for all

$$
\left| \int_{a}^{A} f(x, y) \, dx - S(y) \right| \le \varepsilon \quad (\text{or written as } \left| \int_{A}^{+\infty} f(x, y) \, dx \right| \le \varepsilon)
$$

: then the generalized integral is said to **converge uniformly** on the interval $ Y $ (often denoted by ) $\rightrightarrows $ (symbolic representation), denoted as:

$$
\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, y) \, dx \rightrightarrows S(y) \quad (A \to +\infty, y \in Y)
$$

### is.

- **Pointwise Convergence (Weak Control)**: For each different $ y $the truncation point required to achieve the desired approximation accuracy $ A $ is erratic. If one $ y $ requires $ A=100 $and another $ y $ requires $ A=10^8 $we cannot perform a unified control over the entire region macroscopically.
    
- **Uniform Convergence (Strong Control)**: Found a "universal lower bound for the entire field" $ A_0 $." As long as the truncation position exceeds $ A_0 $no matter the variable $ y $ In the set $ Y $ how to change, the error will be tightly suppressed within $\varepsilon $ the bounds.
    

## III. Weierstrass Criterion (W-test / M-test)

Because directly using $\varepsilon-A $ the definition to prove uniform convergence requires first finding the complex $ S(y)$which is usually very difficult. We need a method that does not require knowing the limit result, but can determine it directly by looking at the integrand function.

### 1. Theorem Content (Weierstrass M-test for Integrals)

Let $ f(x, y)$ defined on $[a, +\infty) \times Y $ Above. If there exists a non-negative function **independent of the parameter $ y $ and**, satisfying: $\varphi(x)$**Strong Suppression (Controllability)**:

1. such that, $\forall x \in [a, +\infty), \forall y \in Y $always has:

$$
|f(x, y)| \le \varphi(x)
$$

2. **Foundation Convergence (Integrability)**: The one-dimensional improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} \varphi(x) \, dx $ **converges**.
    

Then the parameterized variable improper integral $\int_{a}^{+\infty} f(x, y) \, dx $ ... $ Y $ is **absolutely and uniformly convergent**.

### 2. Derivation and Proof

III. Weierstrass Test (W-Test / M-Test)

Uniform convergence of improper integrals is equivalent to: $\varepsilon > 0 $for any $ A_0 $, there exists $ B > A > A_0 $ , that for any two cut-off points

$$
\left| \int_{A}^{B} f(x, y) \, dx \right| \le \varepsilon \quad (\forall y \in Y)
$$

1. Theorem Content (Weierstrass M-test for Integrals)

we examine the absolute value of this "tail integral", using the triangle inequality for integrals to bring the absolute value sign inside the integral:

$$
\left| \int_{A}^{B} f(x, y) \, dx \right| \le \int_{A}^{B} |f(x, y)| \, dx
$$

**Step 3: Substitute the strong control function** $\varphi(x)$**

Since we know $|f(x, y)| \le \varphi(x)$, we can further amplify the entire integral:

$$
\int_{A}^{B} |f(x, y)| \, dx \le \int_{A}^{B} \varphi(x) \, dx
$$

**Step 4: Borrow strength to complete the proof**

Because $\int_{a}^{+\infty} \varphi(x) \, dx $ is a standard one-variable integral that is already known to converge. According to the Cauchy criterion for definite integrals, when $ A, B $ is sufficiently large (greater than some $ A_0 $) its tail integral must be arbitrarily small:

$$
\int_{A}^{B} \varphi(x) \, dx \le \varepsilon
$$

chaining together the entire inequality chain, we get:

$$
\left| \int_{A}^{B} f(x, y) \, dx \right| \le \int_{A}^{B} \varphi(x) \, dx \le \varepsilon \quad (\forall y \in Y)
$$

This perfectly matches the Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence. **Proof complete.**

### Example: $\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{\sin(ax)}{x} \mathrm{d}x $ To see where uniform convergence fails, we examine the most classic oscillating integral, where the parameter is $ a > 0 $ and $ a \in (0, +\infty)$.

Although for any fixed $ a>0 $, by substitution $ u = ax $, the integral can converge to the definite value of the Dirichlet integral $\frac{\pi}{2}$, but when $ a $ approaching $ 0 $ the convergence rate undergoes a devastating delay. We use the negation of the Cauchy criterion to look at this collapse. Consider the remainder term:

$$
\left|\int_A^{+\infty} \frac{\sin(ax)}{x} \mathrm{d}x\right| = \left|\int_{aA}^{+\infty} \frac{\sin u}{u} \mathrm{d}u\right|
$$

The crisis here is that no matter how far we push the truncation point $ A $, as long as the parameter $ a $ is sufficiently small, the lower limit $ aA $ can shrink back near the origin.

If we $ A $ maliciously let the parameter $ a $ interact while wandering, causing $ a = \frac{1}{A}$( $ A $, the $ a $ are all in the domain $(0, +\infty)$ of definition), then the above formula degenerates to:

$$
\left|\int_1^{+\infty} \frac{\sin u}{u} \mathrm{d}u\right| = c > 0
$$

This means the remainder can never be uniformly suppressed to an arbitrarily small $\varepsilon $. Therefore, the integral **does not converge uniformly** on $(0, +\infty)$.

### Cropping and refining the parameter interval: Taking $\int_0^{+\infty} t e^{-t^2 x^2} \mathrm{d}x $ as an example

The physical boundary of the set $ Y $ containing the parameter directly determines the existence of uniform convergence. We demonstrate the behavior of the control flow in different environments by performing a layered analysis of the parameter space $ t $.

#### 1. Closed interval away from the origin $ t \in [c, d] \quad (c > 0)$: In this interval, which is strictly isolated from the origin, the growth of the original function is blocked at both ends. When $ x $ is large, the decay rate of the exponential part is determined by the minimum $ t $ (i.e., $ c $), and the coefficient is determined by the maximum $ t $ (i.e., $ d $):

$$
t e^{-t^2 x^2} \le d e^{-c^2 x^2} = \varphi(x)
$$

Since $\int_0^{+\infty} d e^{-c^2 x^2} \mathrm{d}x $ is a convergent Gaussian integral, by the M-test, we can instantly determine that the integral **converges uniformly** in this interval.

#### 2. Semi-infinite interval $ t \in [c, +\infty) \quad (c > 0)$: When the parameter is allowed to stretch to $+\infty $, the M-test amplification strategy fails because the coefficient has $ t $ (as there is no constant $ d $ ceiling). At this point, we must return to the direct estimation of the remainder. By substitution $ s = tx $ ($\mathrm{d}s = t\mathrm{d}x $):

$$
\left|\int_A^{+\infty} t e^{-t^2 x^2} \mathrm{d}x\right| = \left|\int_{tA}^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s\right|
$$

Notice that when $ t \ge c $, the lower limit of the integral is forcibly pushed far away: $ tA \ge cA $. Therefore:

$$
\left|\int_{tA}^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s\right| \le \left|\int_{cA}^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s\right|
$$

The integral on the right is completely independent of $ t $, and it must inevitably approach $ 0 $ as $ A \to +\infty $ increases. Therefore, by setting $ A \ge \frac{N_\varepsilon}{c}$, we can guarantee that the remainder is less than $\varepsilon $. The integral **converges uniformly** on $[c, +\infty)$.

#### 3. The invisible abyss containing the origin $ t \in [0, +\infty)$: If the interval is extended to touch the origin, the balance breaks again. Intuitively, when $ t \to 0 $, the entire integrand seems to collapse to $ 0 $, but as long as $ t \neq 0 $, its integral value at infinity is always:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} t e^{-t^2 x^2} \mathrm{d}x = \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}
$$

However, at the isolated point $ t=0 $, the integral value plummets to $ 0 $. This discontinuity in the limit integral value has already signaled the failure of uniform convergence.

If we strictly examine it using the Cauchy criterion: In the remainder $\int_A^{+\infty} t e^{-t^2 x^2} \mathrm{d}x = \int_{tA}^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s $, if $ t $ shrinks in tandem with the increase of $ A $, taking $ t = \frac{1}{A} \in [0, +\infty)$, the remainder is constantly the constant $\int_1^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s > 0 $. The global control chain is broken, and thus it **does not converge uniformly** on the entire interval $[0, +\infty)$.

When the Weierstrass M-test is powerless due to the strong oscillation (cancellation of positive and negative parts) of the integrand, we need more refined tools to handle generalized parameter-dependent integrals of the product form like $\int_a^{+\infty} f(x,y)g(x,y) \mathrm{d}x $.

The idea is to extend the classical Dirichlet and Abel tests for integrals in one variable so that the convergence estimates hold **uniformly in the parameter**.

## IV. Oscillation Control Tools for Uniform Convergence

To ensure that the integral converges uniformly over the parameter set $ Y $, we need to impose asymmetric constraints on the behavior of the two factors $ f(x,y)$ and $ g(x,y)$. The common prerequisite for these two theorems is: **For any fixed $ y \in Y $, $ f(x,y)$ must be monotonic with respect to $ x $**.

### 1. Dirichlet Test (Uniform Version)

Used when one part of the integral has good oscillatory cancellation, and the other part tends to zero uniformly.

If the following two conditions are met:

1. $ f(x,y)$ is monotonic with respect to $ x $, and $ f(x,y) \to 0 $ is **uniformly valid** for $ y \in Y $ when $ x \to +\infty $. That is:

$$
\lim_{x \to +\infty} \sup_{y \in Y} |f(x,y)| = 0
$$

2. The local integral of $ g(x,y)$ is uniformly bounded, meaning its antiderivative is **uniformly bounded**:

$$
\exists M > 0, \quad \text{such that } \left| \int_a^A g(x,y) \mathrm{d}x \right| \le M, \quad \forall A \ge a, \forall y \in Y
$$

Then the generalized integral $\int_a^{+\infty} f(x,y)g(x,y) \mathrm{d}x $ **converges uniformly** on $ Y $.

### 2. Abel Test (Uniform Version)

Used when one part itself converges uniformly, and the other part remains stable (does not run wild) uniformly.

If the following two conditions are met:

1. $ f(x,y)$ is monotonic with respect to $ x $, and is **uniformly bounded** over the entire parameter domain:

$$
\exists M > 0, \quad \text{such that } |f(x,y)| \le M, \quad \forall x \in [a, +\infty), \forall y \in Y
$$

2. The generalized integral $\int_a^{+\infty} g(x,y) \mathrm{d}x $ **converges uniformly** on $ Y $.
    

Then the generalized integral $\int_a^{+\infty} f(x,y)g(x,y) \mathrm{d}x $ **converges uniformly** on $ Y $.

### Classic Case Analysis:$\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-tx} \frac{\sin x}{x} \mathrm{d}x \quad (t \in [0, +\infty))$ This integral perfectly demonstrates how to use the theorem above to achieve fine analytical control at the boundary. We need to prove that it is uniformly convergent on the closed semi-axis containing zero. $ t \in [0, +\infty)$ The above is uniformly convergent.

Since $\frac{\sin x}{x} \to 1 $ when $ x \to 0 $, the origin is not a singularity. We only need to focus on the behavior at $ x \to +\infty $. We decompose it into two parts:

$$
f(x,t) = e^{-tx}, \quad g(x,t) = \frac{\sin x}{x}
$$

#### Perspective One: Trying to use the Dirichlet Test directly? (Encountering frustration)

- If we set $ f(x,t) = e^{-tx}$, although it is monotonically decreasing with respect to $ x $, when $ t \to 0 $, $ e^{-tx} \to 1 $. This means that when $ t $ drifts near $ 0 $, its rate of approach to $ 0 $ at infinity can be arbitrarily slow. In other words, $ f(x,t) \to 0 $ is **not uniform** with respect to $ t \in [0, +\infty)$.
    
- Therefore, applying the Dirichlet Test directly to the entire integral on $[0, +\infty)$ is declared a failure.
    

#### Perspective Two: Re-decomposing the factors and the clever intervention of the Abel Test

Since directly controlling the decay rate is not feasible, we can reassign the roles of the factors, utilizing the logic of Abel's test: "uniformly bounded + uniformly convergent".

1. **Examining the first part $ f(x,t) = e^{-tx}$**:
    
    For any $ t \ge 0 $ and $ x \ge 0 $, the exponential function clearly satisfies:

$$
|e^{-tx}| \le 1
$$

This shows that $ f(x,t)$ is monotonic with respect to $ x $ and **uniformly bounded** on $ t \in [0, +\infty)$.
    
2. **Examining the second part $ g(x,t) = \frac{\sin x}{x}$**:
    
    This part does not even contain the parameter $ t $. From the knowledge of single-variable integration, the Dirichlet integral $\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} \mathrm{d}x $ is convergent. Since the parameter is not involved at all in this part, it degenerates into a mundane **uniform convergence**.
    

> **Conclusion**:According to Abel's test, the combination of the uniformly bounded factor $ e^{-tx}$ and the uniformly convergent integral $\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} \mathrm{d}x $ perfectly locks down the **uniform convergence** of the original integral $\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-tx} \frac{\sin x}{x} \mathrm{d}x $ on $ t \in [0, +\infty)$.

#### Application: Unlocking the Tool of Differentiation and Evaluation

Uniform convergence is by no means mere theoretical talk; it is our "pass" for performing analytical operations (such as differentiation or taking limits) under the integral sign.

Let $ S(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-tx} \frac{\sin x}{x} \mathrm{d}x $. Since the integral converges uniformly on $ t \in [0, +\infty)$, and after formally differentiating with respect to $ t $ it satisfies the stronger condition of uniform convergence (on $ t \in [t_0, +\infty), t_0 > 0 $), we can legally pass the derivative operator inside the integral sign:

$$
S'(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( e^{-tx} \frac{\sin x}{x} \right) \mathrm{d}x = \int_0^{+\infty} (-x) e^{-tx} \frac{\sin x}{x} \mathrm{d}x = -\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-tx} \sin x \mathrm{d}x
$$

This is a basic integration by parts or complex exponential integral, resulting in:

$$
S'(t) = -\frac{1}{1 + t^2} \quad (t > 0)
$$

Integrating both sides with respect to $ t $, we get:

$$
S(t) = -\arctan t + C
$$

To determine the constant $ C $, due to the good control of the integral at $ t \to +\infty $, it is easy to see that $\lim_{t \to +\infty} S(t) = 0 $. And $\lim_{t \to +\infty} (-\arctan t) = -\frac{\pi}{2}$, thus solving for $ C = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

Finally, we obtain:

$$
S(t) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan t
$$

Because we proved earlier that the integral **converges uniformly** on the closed interval containing $ t=0 $, by the continuity theorem for uniformly convergent integrals, we can confidently take the limit of $ t \to 0^+$:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} \mathrm{d}x = S(0) = \frac{\pi}{2}
$$

The bridge built by uniform convergence solves the problem of the oscillating integral, which was originally difficult to calculate directly.

After mastering the theorem for interchanging the order of integration in generalized integrals with parameters, we can elegantly solve a class of hard, classic integrals that are almost impossible to calculate directly, but which can be instantly reduced in dimension by introducing a parameter "dimension".

Below, we will use the tool of **interchanging the order of integration under the integral sign** to completely deconstruct and calculate this high-difficulty integral.

### Classic Example: Calculating a Rational Generalized Integral Using Interchange of Integration Order

#### Core Task

Find the value of the generalized integral:

$$
I = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{e^{-ax^2} - e^{-bx^2}}{x^2} \mathrm{d}x \quad (a, b > 0)
$$

#### Motivation and Strategic Conception

Facing this integral, directly searching for the antiderivative is a dead end because $ x^2 $ in the denominator obstructs us from using the properties of the Gaussian integral.

However, notice that the structure of the numerator $ e^{-ax^2} - e^{-bx^2}$ is very special; it perfectly matches the reverse expression of the fundamental theorem of calculus. If we introduce a continuously changing dynamic parameter $ t $, and reduce these two endpoint values to an integral of a derivative with respect to $ t $:

$$
\left. -e^{-tx^2} \right|_a^b = e^{-ax^2} - e^{-bx^2}
$$

Then, by differentiation, we can eliminate $ x^2 $ in the denominator! This is a perfect opportunity to create conditions for changing the order of integration.

#### Rigorous Derivation and Calculation Steps

##### Step 1: Introducing a parameter and rewriting as a iterated integral

Using the fundamental theorem of calculus, rewrite the numerator as an integral with respect to the parameter $ t $:

$$
e^{-ax^2} - e^{-bx^2} = \int_a^b \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( -e^{-tx^2} \right) \mathrm{d}t = \int_a^b x^2 e^{-tx^2} \mathrm{d}t
$$

Substitute this expression back into the original integral $ I $:

$$
I = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1}{x^2} \left( \int_a^b x^2 e^{-tx^2} \mathrm{d}t \right) \mathrm{d}x
$$

After eliminating $ x^2 $ in the denominator, we obtain an extremely clean iterated integral structure:

$$
I = \int_0^{+\infty} \left( \int_a^b e^{-tx^2} \mathrm{d}t \right) \mathrm{d}x
$$

##### Step 2: Interchanging the order of integration

Since the inner generalized integral $\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-tx^2} \mathrm{d}x $ is uniformly convergent with respect to the parameter $ t $ when $ t \in [a, b]$ and $ a > 0 $, (which can be easily proven using the Weierstrass M-test with $ e^{-ax^2}$ as the control function).

Meeting all the safety boundary conditions of the integrability theorem, we boldly **interchange the order of integration of $ x $ and $ t $**:

$$
I = \int_a^b \left( \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-tx^2} \mathrm{d}x \right) \mathrm{d}t
$$

##### Step 3: Solving the inner Gaussian integral

Now, the inner integral becomes a standard Gaussian integral form. To solve it precisely, we perform a variable substitution on the inner integral.

Let $ s = \sqrt{t}x $, then $\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\mathrm{d}s $, and the integration limits remain unchanged at $[0, +\infty)$:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-tx^2} \mathrm{d}x = \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} \mathrm{d}s = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s
$$

Knowing the classic Gaussian integral value $\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-s^2} \mathrm{d}s = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}$, the result of the inner integral is:

$$
\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}
$$

##### Step 4: Outer parameter integration and final lock

Substitute the result of the inner calculation into the outer integral, performing a regular single-variable integration with respect to the parameter $ t $ on $[a, b]$:

$$
I = \int_a^b \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \frac{1}{\sqrt{t}} \mathrm{d}t = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \int_a^b t^{-\frac{1}{2}} \mathrm{d}t
$$

Calculating using the antiderivative formula for power functions:

$$
I = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \left[ 2\sqrt{t} \right]_a^b = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2} \cdot 2(\sqrt{b} - \sqrt{a}) = \sqrt{\pi}(\sqrt{b} - \sqrt{a})
$$

By introducing a one-dimensional virtual parameter space $ t $, the algebraic dilemma originally stuck in the denominator $ x^2 $ transforms into the product of a mundane Gaussian integral and a power function integral after interchanging the order of integration.

The final answer is extremely elegant:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \frac{e^{-ax^2} - e^{-bx^2}}{x^2} \mathrm{d}x = \sqrt{\pi}(\sqrt{b} - \sqrt{a})
$$

After exploring the issue of integrability, we return to the most powerful analytical tool—**differentiation under the integral sign (differentiability theorem)**.

Next, we will completely demonstrate how to peel away and finally lock down a parameterized generalized integral using differentiation under the integral sign, trigonometric substitution, and rationalization substitution through a highly technical example.

### Core Task

Solving the parameterized generalized integral:

$$
S(t) = \int_0^1 \frac{\arctan(tx)}{x \sqrt{1-x^2}} \mathrm{d}x \quad (t \in [0, +\infty))
$$

#### Rigorous Derivation and Calculation Steps

##### Step 1: Passing the derivative operator (Differentiation under the integral sign)

Notice that when $ x \to 0 $, using the Taylor expansion $\arctan(tx) \sim tx $, the integrand approaches $\frac{t}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, so $ x=0 $ is not a singularity; whereas $ x=1 $ is an obvious singularity where the integrand tends to infinity.

To eliminate the complex $\arctan $ structure, we differentiate with respect to the parameter $ t $. By the theorem of differentiability, assuming the resulting integral converges uniformly (the subsequent steps will automatically verify its good properties on $ t \in [0, +\infty)$), we pass the differential operator inside the integral:

$$
S'(t) = \int_0^1 \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left[ \frac{\arctan(tx)}{x \sqrt{1-x^2}} \right] \mathrm{d}x = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{x \sqrt{1-x^2}} \cdot \frac{x}{1+(tx)^2} \mathrm{d}x
$$

By eliminating the $ x $ from the numerator and denominator, we obtain a purely algebraic rational function integral:

$$
S'(t) = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}(1+t^2 x^2)} \mathrm{d}x
$$

##### Step Two: Classic Trigonometric Substitution

Facing the $\sqrt{1-x^2}$ in the denominator, the most natural motivation is to use trigonometric functions to simplify it.

Let $ x = \sin\theta $, then $\mathrm{d}x = \cos\theta \mathrm{d}\theta $. When $ x $ changes from $ 0 $ to $ 1 $, the integration interval for $\theta $ correspondingly becomes $\left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$:

$$
S'(t) = \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{1}{\cos\theta (1+t^2 \sin^2\theta)} \cos\theta \mathrm{d}\theta = \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{1}{1+t^2 \sin^2\theta} \mathrm{d}\theta
$$

##### Step Three: Variation of the Universal Substitution (Rationalization)

To solve the trigonometric rational integral with respect to $\theta $, we need to transform it into a traditional algebraic rational fraction.

We introduce the substitution $ u = \tan\theta $. Under this transformation, we have the classic geometric relation:

$$
\sin^2\theta = \frac{u^2}{1+u^2}, \quad \theta = \arctan u \implies \mathrm{d}\theta = \frac{1}{1+u^2} \mathrm{d}u
$$

When $\theta $ moves from $ 0 $ towards $\frac{\pi}{2}$, $ u $ soars from $ 0 $ to $+\infty $. Substituting these relations into the integral expression:

$$
S'(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1}{1 + t^2 \left(\frac{u^2}{1+u^2}\right)} \cdot \frac{1}{1+u^2} \mathrm{d}u
$$

Find a common denominator and simplify the denominator:

$$
S'(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1+u^2}{(1+u^2) + t^2 u^2} \cdot \frac{1}{1+u^2} \mathrm{d}u = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1}{1 + (1+t^2)u^2} \mathrm{d}u
$$

##### Step Four: Calculating the Derivative Integral Value

The above form is a standard antiderivative structure of the $\arctan $. For clear expression, we rewrite the denominator in the standard sum of squares form:

$$
S'(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1}{1 + \left(\sqrt{1+t^2}u\right)^2} \mathrm{d}u = \left. \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}} \arctan\left(\sqrt{1+t^2}u\right) \right|_0^{+\infty}
$$

Substitute the limits:

$$
S'(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}} \left( \frac{\pi}{2} - 0 \right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}
$$

(Note: Since $ S'(t)$ is continuous and bounded on $ t \in [0, +\infty)$, its corresponding parameter-dependent generalized integral clearly converges uniformly, which inversely proves the complete legitimacy of the differentiation operation in the first step.)

##### Step Five: Recovering the Original Integral

Now, we have the derivative of $ S(t)$, and we only need to perform a simple single-variable integration with respect to $ t $:

$$
S(t) = \int \frac{\pi}{2} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}} \mathrm{d}t = \frac{\pi}{2} \ln\left(t + \sqrt{1+t^2}\right) + C
$$

To determine the integration constant $ C $, we return to the starting point of the original integral. Obviously, when the parameter is $ t = 0 $, the numerator $\arctan(0) = 0 $, causing the entire integral value to collapse to zero:

$$
S(0) = 0
$$

Substitute $ t=0 $ into the expression we obtained with the constant:

$$
S(0) = \frac{\pi}{2} \ln(0 + \sqrt{1+0}) + C = \frac{\pi}{2} \ln(1) + C = C \implies C = 0
$$

By ingeniously using differentiation under the integral sign, we transform a complex inverse trigonometric generalized integral into a logarithmic form. The final answer is extremely elegant:

$$
\int_0^1 \frac{\arctan(tx)}{x \sqrt{1-x^2}} \mathrm{d}x = \frac{\pi}{2} \ln\left(t + \sqrt{1+t^2}\right)
$$

When dealing with limits and transformations of parameter-dependent integrals, the trickiest case is when **both the integration interval and the parameter interval stretch to infinity**. The integrability theorem we discussed previously is limited to the exchange where the parameter $ y $ is on a finite closed interval $[c,d]$. If the parameter domain also becomes an infinite interval, i.e., considering the exchange of iterated integrals under double infinite boundaries:

$$
\int_c^{+\infty} \left( \int_a^{+\infty} f(x,y) \mathrm{d}x \right) \mathrm{d}y \stackrel{?}{=} \int_a^{+\infty} \left( \int_c^{+\infty} f(x,y) \mathrm{d}y \right) \mathrm{d}x
$$

In this case, due to the mutual entanglement of the double limit process at infinity, mere 'uniform convergence' is insufficient to support the unconditional exchange of operators. To prevent non-uniform collapse at infinity, we need stronger analytical tools.

## Core Theorem: Interchange of Integration Order for Double Infinite Intervals

Let $ f(x,y)$ be continuous on the first quadrant region $ x \in [a, +\infty), y \in [c, +\infty)$. To safely interchange the order of these two infinite integrals, mathematical analysis provides two parallel logical paths: **local uniform convergence plus absolute integrability control**, or **global absolute double integrability (Fubini type)**.

### Path One: Inner Uniform Convergence + Outer Absolute Integrability Control

If the following conditions are met:

1. **Local Uniform Convergence**: For any fixed finite upper bound $ d > c $, the integral $\int_a^{+\infty} f(x,y) \mathrm{d}x $ **converges uniformly** on the parameter subinterval $ y \in [c, d]$; similarly, for any fixed $ b > a $, $\int_c^{+\infty} f(x,y) \mathrm{d}y $ **converges uniformly** on $ x \in [a, b]$.
    
2. **Absolute Integrability Constraint (Strong Control)**: At least one of the following two absolute iterated integrals **exists and converges**:

$$
\int_c^{+\infty} \left( \int_a^{+\infty} |f(x,y)| \mathrm{d}x \right) \mathrm{d}y \quad \text{or} \quad \int_a^{+\infty} \left( \int_c^{+\infty} |f(x,y)| \mathrm{d}y \right) \mathrm{d}x
$$

Then the order of integration for the double infinite region can be legally interchanged, i.e.:

$$
\int_c^{+\infty} \left( \int_a^{+\infty} f(x,y) \mathrm{d}x \right) \mathrm{d}y = \int_a^{+\infty} \left( \int_c^{+\infty} f(x,y) \mathrm{d}y \right) \mathrm{d}x
$$

### Motivational Perspective: Why emphasize 'existence of the absolute integral'? 

When both intervals are infinite, the function may exhibit violent positive and negative alternation at infinity. If we only require the original integral to converge (possibly conditionally), then after exchanging the order, the mechanism of cancellation of oscillations might decouple under different integration paths, leading to unequal results, or even one side converging while the other diverges.

Introducing the absolute integrability of $|f(x,y)|$ is essentially erecting a bounded 'energy ceiling' in space, fundamentally stifling any weak equilibrium state that relies on oscillations at infinity, providing solid confidence protection for the operator exchange.

## Classic Application: Re-examination of the Core Formula of the Gaussian Integral in Probability Theory

To see the power of the double infinite integral exchange, we can use it to explain the core cornerstone of probability theory and mathematical statistics—the normalization of the total energy of the Gaussian integral.

### Strategic Conception

We want to calculate the famous Laplace-Gaussian integral value:

$$
J = \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-x^2} \mathrm{d}x
$$

The conventional method is to use a polar coordinate transformation for the double integral. However, within the framework of parameter-dependent generalized integrals, we can introduce an invisible exponential parameter $ y $ and directly extract $ J^2 $ through the exchange of double infinite integrals by constructing a bivariate control flow.

## Rigorous Derivation and Analytical Steps

### Step One: Constructing a Control Function with Two-Dimensional Structure

Consider the function $ f(x,y) = x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}$, whose domain is $ x \in [0, +\infty), y \in [0, +\infty)$. The motivation for designing this function is extremely subtle: it retains the product structure of a quadratic form in the exponent, while retaining the Jacobi factor $ x $ externally, which facilitates the substitution.

We now calculate the iterated integral of it over the entire first quadrant.

### Step Two: Path $ A $— Integrating $ y $ first, then $ x $

First, treat $ x $ as a constant parameter and perform the generalized integration with respect to $ y $ over $[0, +\infty)$.

To eliminate the $ y^2 $ in the exponent, we make the variable substitution $ u = xy $, so $\mathrm{d}y = \frac{1}{x} \mathrm{d}u $:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)} \mathrm{d}y = x e^{-x^2} \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-(xy)^2} \mathrm{d}y = x e^{-x^2} \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-u^2} \frac{1}{x} \mathrm{d}u
$$

The external $ x $ perfectly cancels with the $ x $ in the denominator. Notice that the remaining inner integral is the standard Gaussian integral $\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-u^2} \mathrm{d}u = J $, therefore:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)} \mathrm{d}y = J e^{-x^2}
$$

Next, integrate this result with respect to the outer variable $ x $ from $ 0 $ to $+\infty $ integrate:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \left( \int_0^{+\infty} x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)} \mathrm{d}y \right) \mathrm{d}x = \int_0^{+\infty} J e^{-x^2} \mathrm{d}x = J \cdot \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-x^2} \mathrm{d}x = J^2
$$

### Step 3: Path $ B $— First $ x $ then $ y $ integrate

Now we change perspective and integrate with respect to $ x $ first.

Notice that the integrand $ x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}$ of the outer $ x $ is exactly a constant multiple of the derivative of the inner exponent with respect to $ x $ the variable. Therefore, the inner integral can be solved directly by completing the differential:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)} \mathrm{d}x = \left. -\frac{1}{2(1+y^2)} e^{-x^2(1+y^2)} \right|_{x=0}^{x=+\infty} = 0 - \left( -\frac{1}{2(1+y^2)} \right) = \frac{1}{2(1+y^2)}
$$

Next, we integrate this result with respect to the parameter $ y $ over $[0, +\infty)$:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} \left( \int_0^{+\infty} x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)} \mathrm{d}x \right) \mathrm{d}y = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1}{2(1+y^2)} \mathrm{d}y = \left. \frac{1}{2} \arctan y \right|_0^{+\infty} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

### Step 4: Swapping Legitimate Verification and Final Locking

Since the integrand $ f(x,y) = x e^{-x^2(1+y^2)}$ is non-negative in the first quadrant, its original integral is completely equivalent to its absolute value integral. The iterated integral value $\frac{\pi}{4}$ calculated along path $ B $ is a definite positive real number, which directly proclaims that the **absolute integrability constraint condition in the theorem is perfectly satisfied**.

Therefore, the operation of swapping the integral over the double infinite interval is completely legitimate. The endpoints of the calculations along both paths must be forcibly aligned:

$$
J^2 = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$

Since the Gaussian integral is positive, taking the square root on both sides yields:

$$
J = \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-x^2} \mathrm{d}x = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}
$$

## Summary

Through the theorem of swapping integrals over double infinite intervals, we are able to re-establish the classic value of the Gaussian integral within a completely self-consistent logic, without the need for explicit two-dimensional plane coordinate transformations (such as the Jacobian determinant of polar coordinates), relying only on the algebraic penetration of one-dimensional generalized integrals with parameters.

After a rigorous analysis and control of the limits, derivatives, and integrals of generalized integrals with parameters, we now study two special functions defined by these generalized integrals—the **Gamma function ($\Gamma $) and the Beta function ($ B $)**.

They are not only analytic continuations of the factorial function to the domain of real (and even complex) numbers, but they are also the ultimate link connecting various hardcore integrals.

# Special Functions
## 1. Gamma Function: Continuous Extension of the Factorial

### Core Definition

For the parameter $\alpha > 0 $, the Gamma function is defined by the following generalized integral with parameters over the double infinite interval:

$$
\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^{+\infty} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-x} \mathrm{d}x
$$

> **Brief Analysis of Convergence**: This integral has a singularity at $ x \to 0 $ (when $ 0 < \alpha < 1 $), and it is a generalized integral at $ x \to +\infty $. By the comparison test, it can be easily shown that the integral converges uniformly on any compact subinterval if and only if $\alpha > 0 $, thereby defining a continuously differentiable function.

### Basic Properties and Recurrence Relation

By integration by parts, we can directly establish the factorial ladder of the Gamma function:

$$
\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^{+\infty} x^{\alpha-1} \mathrm{d}(-e^{-x}) = \left. -x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x} \right|_0^{+\infty} + (\alpha-1)\int_0^{+\infty} x^{\alpha-2}e^{-x} \mathrm{d}x
$$

When $\alpha > 1 $, the first term collapses to $ 0 $, yielding the core recurrence formula:

$$
\Gamma(\alpha) = (\alpha-1)\Gamma(\alpha-1)
$$

### Classic Discrete Points and Special Values

- **Discrete Integer Points**: Starting from $\Gamma(1) = \int_0^{+\infty} e^{-x} \mathrm{d}x = 1 $, and combining it with the recurrence formula, it is clear that for any positive integer $ n $:

$$
\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!
$$

- **Important Special Value for Half-Integers**: By substituting the Gaussian integral, we can directly determine $\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)$. Let $ x = u^2 $, then $\mathrm{d}x = 2u\mathrm{d}u $:

$$
\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \int_0^{+\infty} x^{-\frac{1}{1}} e^{-x} \mathrm{d}x = \int_0^{+\infty} \frac{1}{u} e^{-u^2} (2u \mathrm{d}u) = 2\int_0^{+\infty} e^{-u^2} \mathrm{d}u = \sqrt{\pi}
$$

## 2. Beta Function: Power Index Combination over a Finite Interval

### Core Definition

For the parameter $ p > 0, q > 0 $, the Beta function (also known as the first kind of Euler integral) is defined by a generalized singular integral over a finite closed interval:

$$
B(p, q) = \int_0^1 x^{p-1}(1-x)^{q-1} \mathrm{d}x
$$

When $ p < 1 $, the origin is a singularity; when $ q < 1 $, $ x = 1 $ is designated as a singularity. The parameter constraint $ p > 0, q > 0 $ perfectly guarantees its convergence. By the substitution of symmetry $ t = 1-x $, it is easy to see:

$$
B(p, q) = B(q, p)
$$

## 3. The Ultimate Link: The Reciprocity Formula Between Beta and Gamma Functions

The Gamma function and the Beta function appear to be one over an infinite interval and the other over a finite interval, but their internal exponential and power structures possess a deep algebraic isomorphism. There exists a famous supreme core formula between them:

$$
B(p, q) = \frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}
$$

### Motivation and Rigorous Derivation

To see the origin of this formula, we need to multiply two one-dimensional Gamma functions, transform it into a double integral, and then extract the structure of the Beta function through a coordinate transformation.

Consider two independent Gamma integrals, introducing a variable substitution to facilitate the handling of polar coordinates in the plane.

Let $ x = u^2 $ ($\mathrm{d}x = 2u\mathrm{d}u $), $ y = v^2 $ ($\mathrm{d}y = 2v\mathrm{d}v $):

$$
\Gamma(p) = 2\int_0^{+\infty} u^{2p-1} e^{-u^2} \mathrm{d}u, \quad \Gamma(q) = 2\int_0^{+\infty} v^{2q-1} e^{-v^2} \mathrm{d}v
$$

Multiplying the two, we obtain an iterated integral over the first quadrant (which can then be converted into a double integral):

$$
\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q) = 4 \int_0^{+\infty} \int_0^{+\infty} u^{2p-1} v^{2q-1} e^{-(u^2+v^2)} \mathrm{d}u \mathrm{d}v
$$

Facing the structure $ e^{-(u^2+v^2)}$, the most natural motivation is to transform it into polar coordinates.

Let $ u = r\cos\theta, v = r\sin\theta $, the Jacobian determinant area element $\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v = r\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}\theta $. The geometric boundary corresponding to the first quadrant is $ r \in [0, +\infty), \theta \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$:

$$
\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q) = 4 \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \int_0^{+\infty} (r\cos\theta)^{2p-1} (r\sin\theta)^{2q-1} e^{-r^2} r \mathrm{d}r \mathrm{d}\theta
$$

Completely decoupling the radial variable $ r $ and the angular variable $\theta $:

$$
\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q) = 4 \left( \int_0^{+\infty} r^{2(p+q)-1} e^{-r^2} \mathrm{d}r \right) \left( \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} (\cos\theta)^{2p-1} (\sin\theta)^{2q-1} \mathrm{d}\theta \right)
$$

- **Examining the integral $ r $ on the left**: By re-substituting $ t = r^2 $ ($\mathrm{d}t = 2r\mathrm{d}r $), it immediately reverts to the standard definition of the Gamma function:

$$
2 \int_0^{+\infty} r^{2(p+q)-1} e^{-r^2} \mathrm{d}r = \int_0^{+\infty} t^{(p+q)-1} e^{-t} \mathrm{d}t = \Gamma(p+q)
$$

- **Examine the right side's $\theta $ integral**: To match it with the Beta function, we perform a trigonometric substitution in $ B(p, q)$ the original definition $ x = \sin^2\theta $ ($\mathrm{d}x = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta\mathrm{d}\theta $):

$$
B(p, q) = \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} (\sin^2\theta)^{p-1} (1-\sin^2\theta)^{q-1} \cdot 2\sin\theta\cos\theta \mathrm{d}\theta = 2\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} (\sin\theta)^{2p-1} (\cos\theta)^{2q-1} \mathrm{d}\theta
$$

By symmetry, this is exactly twice the angular integral decoupled above.
    

Piecing these two parts back into the original form completes the logical closed loop:

$$
\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q) = \Gamma(p+q) \cdot B(p, q) \implies B(p, q) = \frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}
$$

This formula perfectly algebraically hedges the strong non-integrability over a finite interval with the rate of decay at infinity, which is a stroke of genius in the entire theory of integral transformations.
