But note that the reverse is not true: even if a series converges after adding parentheses to its terms, the series itself may not converge. A very obvious example is $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n $It diverges, but by adding appropriate parentheses, it can be made to converge.

For example

#### Proof: Harmonic Series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}$ Diverges

**Example:** Consider the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}$where the general term is $ a_n = \frac{1}{n}$.

**Proof:** (Proof by contradiction)

Assume that this series **converges**, and let its sum be $ S $.

1. **Construct a new series $ b_n $:**
    
We group the terms of the original series in pairs, and let:

$$
b_n = a_{2n-1} + a_{2n} = \frac{1}{2n-1} + \frac{1}{2n}
$$

Since $\frac{1}{2n-1} > \frac{1}{2n}$, it is clear that:

$$
b_n = \frac{1}{2n-1} + \frac{1}{2n} > \frac{1}{2n} + \frac{1}{2n} = \frac{1}{n} = a_n
$$

which is $ b_n > a_n $.
    
2. **Define partial sums:**
    
    - Notation for series $\sum b_k $ The partial sum is $ B_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} b_k $.
        
    - Notation for series $\sum a_k $ The partial sum is $ A_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k $.
        
3. **Derive a contradiction:**
    
    By the associativity of series, if $\sum a_n $ converges to $ S $, then the series formed by adding parentheses to it at any point $\sum b_n $ must also converge to the same sum $ S $. This means:
    
    When $ n \to \infty $ hours,$ B_n \to S $ and $ A_n \to S $.
    
However, consider the difference between the two:

$$
B_n - A_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (b_k - a_k)
$$

Since every $ b_k - a_k > 0 $, the difference sequence is monotonically increasing, therefore:

$$
B_n - A_n \geq B_1 - A_1 = b_1 - a_1 = \left(\frac{1}{1} + \frac{1}{2}\right) - 1 = \frac{1}{2}
$$

If we let $ n \to \infty $, the left side will tend to $ S - S = 0 $, thus obtaining:

$$
0 \geq \frac{1}{2}
$$

This is clearly a **contradiction**.
    

**Conclusion:** The original hypothesis is false, the harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}$ diverges.

# Convergence Tests for Positive Term Series

If all the terms of the series are positive, then by using the Monotone Convergence Theorem, we only need to show $ S_n $ that there is an upper bound to know that the series converges.

Furthermore, there is a natural idea that if every term is large, then the sum should naturally be large.

## Comparison Test

Let two positive term series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ the general term satisfies $ u_n \leqslant v_n \quad (n=1, 2, \cdots)$, then:

1. If the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $converges, then the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ also converges;
    
2. If the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $If the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ also diverges.
    

---

### Proof

**For Conclusion (1):**

- Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ the sequence of partial sums be $\{S_n\}$ and $\{T_n\}$.
    
- From the given condition $ u_n \leqslant v_n $, we get: $ 0 \leqslant S_n \leqslant T_n \quad (n=1, 2, \cdots)$.
    
- Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ convergent. By the Monotone Convergence Theorem (Proposition 1), its sequence of partial sums $\{T_n\}$ must have an upper bound.
    
- That is, there exists a constant $ M $ such that $ T_n \leqslant M \quad (n=1, 2, \cdots)$.
    
- By the transitivity of inequalities, we get $ S_n \leqslant M \quad (n=1, 2, \cdots)$, which shows that $\{S_n\}$ also has an upper bound.
    
- Again citing the Monotone Convergence Theorem, we know that the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ converges.
    

**For Conclusion (2):**

- We use **proof by contradiction**.
    
- Assume that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ converges. Then, by Conclusion (1), we deduce that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ also converges.
    
- This contradicts the given condition that “$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ diverges.”
    
- Therefore, $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ must diverge. Done.
    

---

### Corollary (A more general form)

Notice that removing a finite number of terms from the beginning of a series does not affect its convergence, so we can get the following corollary:

If there exist constants $ N(\geqslant 1)$ and $ c(>0)$ such that when $ n \geqslant N $, we have:

$$
0 \leqslant u_n \leqslant c v_n
$$

Then:

- If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ converges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ also converges;
    
- If $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ diverges, then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ also diverges.

This is a bit like the Squeeze Theorem.

#### Convergence and Divergence of p-series

For example, we know that the harmonic series $ a_n=1/n $ diverges, so we can consider the summation of the so-called p-series $ 1/n^p $. Then, when $ 0<p<1 $, we know it also diverges.

Let $ p > 1 $. Below is a very mysterious method that does not use the integral test. The integral test is quite obvious, so I won't mention it. The integral test will be mentioned later.

##### 1. Establishing an inequality using the Mean Value Theorem for Derivatives

Consider the increment of the function $ f(x) = \frac{1}{x^{p-1}}$ on the interval $[n-1, n]$. According to the **Mean Value Theorem for Derivatives**:

$$
\frac{1}{(n-1)^{p-1}} - \frac{1}{n^{p-1}} = (p-1) \frac{1}{(n-\eta)^p}, \quad \eta \in (0, 1)
$$

Since $ n-\eta < n $, then $\frac{1}{(n-\eta)^p} > \frac{1}{n^p}$.

From this, we get the key inequality:

$$
\frac{1}{(n-1)^{p-1}} - \frac{1}{n^{p-1}} \geq (p-1) \frac{1}{n^p}
$$

After simplification:

$$
\frac{1}{n^p} \leq \frac{1}{p-1} \left( \frac{1}{(n-1)^{p-1}} - \frac{1}{n^{p-1}} \right)
$$

---

##### 2. Estimating the Partial Sum $ S_N $ Let the partial sum of the series be $ S_N = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{1}{n^p}$We separate the first term ($ n=1 $) and use the inequality above for the remaining terms:

$$
\begin{aligned} S_N &= 1 + \sum_{n=2}^{N} \frac{1}{n^p} \\ &\leq 1 + \frac{1}{p-1} \sum_{n=2}^{N} \left( \frac{1}{(n-1)^{p-1}} - \frac{1}{n^{p-1}} \right) \end{aligned}
$$

This is a **telescoping series**, and after summing, all intermediate terms cancel out:

$$
\begin{aligned} S_N &\leq 1 + \frac{1}{p-1} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{N^{p-1}} \right) \\ &< 1 + \frac{1}{p-1} \quad (\text{because } \frac{1}{N^{p-1}} > 0) \\ &= \frac{p-1+1}{p-1} = \frac{p}{p-1} \end{aligned}
$$

---

##### 3. Conclusion

From the above derivation, we know that the partial sum sequence $\{S_N\}$ is **monotonically increasing** and **bounded** (the upper bound is $\frac{p}{p-1}$).

According to the **Monotone Convergence Theorem**:

$$
\Rightarrow \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^p} \text{ converges}
$$

The technique is quite strong.

## The Limit Form of the Comparison Test

Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ be two positive term series, and assume that:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{u_n}{v_n} = h
$$

Where $ h $ is a finite number or $+\infty $. Then the following conclusion holds:

1. **When $ 0 \leqslant h < +\infty $**: If the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ converges, then the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ also converges;
    
2. **When $ 0 < h \leqslant +\infty $**: If the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ diverges, then the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ also diverges.
    

**Especially**: When $ 0 < h < +\infty $, the two infinite series **converge or diverge simultaneously**.
This is actually doing order estimation. Same order, same convergence/divergence.

---

### Proof

**Proof of Conclusion (1):**

- When $ 0 \leqslant h < +\infty $, by the definition of a limit, there exists a natural number $ N $ such that when $ n > N $:

$$
\frac{u_n}{v_n} < h + 1
$$

- That is, $ u_n < (h + 1)v_n $.
    
- By the Comparison Test (basic form), the convergence of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} v_n $ implies the convergence of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $.
    

**Proof of Conclusion (2):**

- When $ 0 < h \leqslant +\infty $, we consider the reciprocal of the ratio:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{v_n}{u_n} = \frac{1}{h}
$$

- We define that when $ h = +\infty $, $\frac{1}{h} = 0 $.
    
- Similarly, for sufficiently large $ n $, $ v_n < \left(\frac{1}{h} + 1\right)u_n $.
    
- This proves Conclusion (2). Done.

## Ratio Test (D'Alembert's Test)

In general, how do we use the geometric series to determine the convergence or divergence of a series? We use what is called the Ratio Test.

**Given Condition:**

Given a positive term series $\sum u_n $, where $ u_n > 0 $.

Let the limit of the ratio of consecutive terms be:

$$
\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n} = l
$$

**Conclusion:**

1. If $ l < 1 $, the series **converges**;
    
2. If $ l > 1 $ (or $ l = +\infty $), the series **diverges**;
    
3. If $ l = 1 $, the test is inconclusive; the series may converge or diverge, and requires separate determination.
    

---

### Proof Logic: Why the series converges when $ l < 1 $

**Proof Idea:** Use the Comparison Test to compare the original series with a convergent geometric series (geometric series).

1. **Finding the common ratio $ q $:**
    
Since $\lim_{n \to +\infty} \frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n} = l < 1 $, by the properties of limits, there must exist a constant $ q $ such that $ l < q < 1 $.
    
2. **Determining the range:**
    
There exists a sufficiently large positive integer $ N $ such that when $ n \geq N $, it always holds that $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n} < q $.
    
3. **Term-by-term amplification:**
    
    - $ u_{N+1} < q u_N $-$ u_{N+2} < q u_{N+1} < q^2 u_N $
        
    - It can be generalized to: $ u_n < q^{n-N} u_N = q^n \cdot \frac{u_N}{q^N}$
        
4. **Drawing the conclusion:**
    
Let the constant be $ C_N = \frac{u_N}{q^N}$, then for all $ n \geq N $, we have $ u_n < C_N q^n $.
    
Because $ 0 < q < 1 $, the geometric series $\sum q^n $ converges. By the **Comparison Test**, the series $\sum u_n $ must converge.
    

For the other side, we can deduce it by changing the sign.

---

### Example: Why does the test fail when $ l = 1 $?

Here are two examples of $ l=1 $ but with drastically different convergence behaviors:

- **Example 1 (Diverges):** The harmonic series $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n} = \frac{n}{n+1} \to 1 $, but this series **diverges**.
    
- **Example 2 (Converges):** The $ p $-series of $ p=2 $, $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n} = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^2 \to 1 $, but this series **converges**.

**Conclusion:**

2. If $ l > 1 $ (or $ l = +\infty $), the series **diverges**;

## Raabe's Test

Previously, we used the geometric series for a test, but the geometric series is not that powerful. Can the $ p $-series we discussed earlier also be used to determine convergence or divergence? The answer is yes.

**Given condition:** Given a positive term series $\sum u_n $, where $ u_n > 0 $. Let the limit of the transformed ratio of consecutive terms be:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} n \left( \frac{u_n}{u_{n+1}} - 1 \right) = R
$$

**Conclusion:**

1. **If $ R > 1 $**: The series **converges**;
    
2. **If $ R < 1 $**: The series **diverges**;
    
3. **If $ R = 1 $**: The test **fails**, and a more refined test (such as Gauss's Test) must be used.
    

---

### Proof Points and Logical Deduction

The core idea is to compare it with the known **p-series**.

#### 1. Properties of Auxiliary Series

Select the $ p $-series $ v_n = \frac{1}{n^p}$. Apply the same limit operation to it:

$$
n \left( \frac{v_n}{v_{n+1}} - 1 \right) = n \left( \frac{(n+1)^p}{n^p} - 1 \right) = n \left( (1 + \frac{1}{n})^p - 1 \right)
$$

Using the binomial expansion or Taylor expansion $(1+x)^p = 1 + px + O(x^2)$:

$$
\approx n \left( 1 + \frac{p}{n} + O(\frac{1}{n^2}) - 1 \right) \to p
$$

This shows that for the $ p $-series, this limit value is exactly equal to its power $ p $.

#### 2. Using the Comparison Test to prove the convergence of $ R > 1 $

- **Finding an intermediate value:** If $ R > 1 $, then there must exist a number $ p $ such that $ R > p > 1 $.
    
- **Establishing the inequality:** Since $\lim_{n \to \infty} n \left( \frac{u_n}{u_{n+1}} - 1 \right) = R > p $, there exists a sufficiently large $ N $ such that when $ n \geq N $:

$$
n \left( \frac{u_n}{u_{n+1}} - 1 \right) > n \left( \frac{(n+1)^p}{n^p} - 1 \right)
$$

- **Simplifying the inequality:**

$$
\frac{u_n}{u_{n+1}} > \frac{(n+1)^p}{n^p} \Rightarrow (n+1)^p u_{n+1} < n^p u_n
$$

- **Inductive amplification:** This shows that the sequence $\{n^p u_n\}$ is monotonically decreasing after $ n \geq N $. Therefore:

$$
(n+1)^p u_{n+1} < n^p u_n < \dots < N^p u_N = C_N
$$

Thus, we obtain $ u_{n+1} < \frac{C_N}{(n+1)^p}$.
    
- **Conclusion:** Since $ p > 1 $, by the **p-series convergence test** and the **Comparison Test**, the series $\sum u_n $ **converges**.

## Integral Test

In [[7. Summation #Integral Estimation]], we actually discussed summation by integral estimation.

### Theorem

**1. Theorem**

Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n $ be a positive term series ($ u_n \geq 0 $). If there exists a non-negative, monotonically decreasing function $ f(x)$ such that $ f(n) = u_n $, then:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} u_n \text{ converges} \iff \int_{1}^{+\infty} f(x) dx \text{ converges}
$$

**2. Core Equivalence Relationship**

It is not difficult to obtain the following chain through the principle of boundedness (the property that positive term series/non-negative functions are increasing):

- **Series Convergence** $\iff $ Partial Sum Sequence $\{S_n\}$ **Bounded Above**.
    
- **Integral Existence:** The definite integral $\int_{1}^{A} f(x) dx $ of the variable upper limit $\iff $ **is bounded above**.
    

---

### Proof Idea

By comparing the area of rectangles on a unit interval with the area under the curve, we can derive the following two key inequalities:

#### A. Proving "Series Convergence $\Rightarrow $ Integral Convergence"

Using the left rectangle method (or right shift comparison):

$$
\int_{1}^{n} f(x) dx \leq \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} u_k = S_{n-1}
$$

- **Logical Deduction:** If the series converges, then $\{S_{n-1}\}$ is bounded above, which leads to $\int_{1}^{n} f(x) dx $ also being bounded above. By the Monotone Bounded Theorem, when $ n \to \infty $, the integral $\int_{1}^{+\infty} f(x) dx $ must exist.
    

#### B. Proving "Integral Convergence $\Rightarrow $ Series Convergence"

Using the right rectangle method:

$$
S_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} u_k = u_1 + \sum_{k=2}^{n} u_k \leq u_1 + \int_{1}^{n} f(x) dx
$$

- **Logical Deduction**: If the improper integral converges, then its variable upper limit integral $\int_{1}^{n} f(x) dx $ has an upper bound, which in turn implies that $\{S_n\}$ also has an upper bound. Therefore, the positive term series $\sum u_n $ must converge.

Combining the two aspects leads to the original proposition being true.

### Application

The integral test can handle cases where the previously mentioned tests yield a limit of $ 1 $ but cannot handle them, for example,

$$
\sum \frac{1}{nlnn}
$$

Neither Raabe's test nor Cauchy's test can be applied, but by integrating and obtaining the original function $ lnlnn $, we know it diverges, and correspondingly, if we change it to $\frac{1}{n(lnn)^2}$, we know it converges.

Although powerful, it requires that the function can be integrated, so it has certain formal requirements and cannot solve everything at once.
