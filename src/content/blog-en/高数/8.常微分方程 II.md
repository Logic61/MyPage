## Existence and Uniqueness of Solutions
Initial Value Problem for First-Order Ordinary Differential Equations

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y), \quad y(x_0) = y_0
$$

**Given Conditions**:

1. The function $ f(x, y)$ in a closed rectangular region containing the point $(x_0, y_0)$

$$
R = \{ (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : |x - x_0| \leq a, |y - y_0| \leq b \}
$$

is continuous. Therefore, by the property of continuous functions on a closed interval, $ f $ is bounded on $ R $, meaning there exists a constant $ M > 0 $ such that for all $(x, y) \in R $, $|f(x, y)| \leq M $.

2. The function $ f(x, y)$ satisfies the **Lipschitz condition** with respect to the variable $ y $ on $ R $: there exists a constant $ L > 0 $ such that for any $(x, y_1) \in R $ and $(x, y_2) \in R $, it holds that

$$
|f(x, y_1) - f(x, y_2)| \leq L |y_1 - y_2|
$$

Under the above conditions, there exists a unique function $ y = \phi(x)$, defined on an interval $ I $ containing $ x_0 $, that satisfies the differential equation and the initial condition.

### Proof

Simply, we consider integrating both sides, obtaining:

$$
y=y_0+\int_{x_0}^x f(t,y)dt
$$

This transforms it into what is called an integral equation. How to solve it further? Using the idea of the Neumann method, we think that iteration might approximate the solution. First, let's guess a solution, for example, the constant function $\phi_0(x)\equiv y_0 $. This is the uniquely determined value. Although it does not satisfy the differential equation, we can correct the result using the equation. We consider the following iteration:

$$
\phi_{n+1}(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^{x} f(t, \phi_n(t)) \, dt, \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

Thus, if the sequence $\phi_n $ converges, then this limit also satisfies the integral equation, and consequently satisfies the differential equation. This can be done.

### Ensuring well-definedness
First, we know the constraint is $|x-x_0|\le a $, and $|\phi_n(x)-y_0|\le b $. Then, we find $ h $ by induction. Assume the constraint holds for $ n $ and before. The next step:

$$
|\phi_{n+1}(x)-y_0|=| \int_{x_0}^x f(t,\phi_n(x))dt | \le \int_{x_0}^x|f(t,\phi_n(x))|dt\le M|x-x_0|
$$

Then, in order for $\phi_{n+1}(x)-y_0\le b $, it is sufficient that $ M|x-x_0|\le b $. So, as long as we choose $ Mh\le b $, which is $ h\le b/M $. At the same time, $ h $ must also satisfy $ h\le a $. Combining these gives $ h=min(a,\frac{b}{M})$. Thus, defining it on $[x_0-h,x_0+h]$ is well-defined, and the image remains within the rectangular region.

### Estimating Consecutive Iterations

Starting from $ n=1 $:

$$
|\phi_1(x) - \phi_0(x)| = \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} f(t, y_0) \, dt \right| \leq M |x - x_0|
$$

For a general $ n \geq 1 $, using the iterative definition and the Lipschitz condition,

$$
\begin{aligned}
|\phi_{n+1}(x) - \phi_n(x)| &= \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} [f(t, \phi_n(t)) - f(t, \phi_{n-1}(t))] \, dt \right| \\
&\leq  \int_{x_0}^{x} |f(t, \phi_n(t)) - f(t, \phi_{n-1}(t))| \, dt 
\end{aligned}
$$

Using the Lipschitz condition:

$$
\leq L \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} |\phi_n(t) - \phi_{n-1}(t)| \, dt \right|
$$

**Derivation**: 
Let's try calculating the first few terms to find a pattern.

- Given: $|\phi_1(x) - \phi_0(x)| \leq M |x - x_0|$ - Calculate $|\phi_2(x) - \phi_1(x)|$:

$$
|\phi_2(x) - \phi_1(x)| \leq L \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} M |t - x_0| \, dt \right| = M L \frac{|x - x_0|^2}{2}
$$

- Calculate $|\phi_3(x) - \phi_2(x)|$:

$$
|\phi_3(x) - \phi_2(x)| \leq L \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} M L \frac{|t - x_0|^2}{2} \, dt \right| = M L^2 \frac{|x - x_0|^3}{3!}
$$

For all $ n \geq 1 $ and $ x \in I $, the following estimate holds:

$$
|\phi_n(x) - \phi_{n-1}(x)| \leq \frac{M L^{n-1}}{n!} |x - x_0|^n \leq \frac{M L^{n-1}}{n!} h^n
$$

### Proof of Existence
Consider the series

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \max_{x \in I} |\phi_n(x) - \phi_{n-1}(x)|
$$

According to the estimate, every term of this series is controlled by $\frac{M L^{n-1}}{n!} h^n $.

Consider the numerical series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{M L^{n-1}}{n!} h^n $. We can test its convergence using the ratio test:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\frac{M L^n}{(n+1)!} h^{n+1}}{\frac{M L^{n-1}}{n!} h^n} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{L h}{n+1} = 0
$$

Since the limit is 0 (which is less than 1), the numerical series converges.

By the **Weierstrass M-test**, since the absolute value of every term of the function series is controlled by the corresponding term of a convergent positive term series on the interval $ I $, the function series **converges uniformly** on $ I $.

**Conclusion**: 
This means the Picard iteration sequence $\{\phi_n(x)\}$ converges uniformly to a limit function on the interval $ I $, denoted as $\phi(x)$. Since each $\phi_n $ is continuous, and the convergence is uniform, the limit function $\phi(x)$ is also continuous.

Recall the iterative formula:

$$
\phi_{n+1}(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^{x} f(t, \phi_n(t)) \, dt
$$

Take the limit of both sides of the equation $ n \to \infty $.
The left side directly tends to $\phi(x)$.
For the right side, we can move the limit sign inside the integral, i.e.:

$$
\lim_{n \to \infty} \int_{x_0}^{x} f(t, \phi_n(t)) \, dt = \int_{x_0}^{x} \lim_{n \to \infty} f(t, \phi_n(t)) \, dt = \int_{x_0}^{x} f(t, \phi(t)) \, dt
$$

This shows that $\phi(x)$ satisfies the integral equation, and therefore is a solution to the initial value problem. Existence is proven.

### Proof of Uniqueness

**Derivation**: 
Assume there exists another function $\psi(x)$ that also satisfies the integral equation:

$$
\psi(x) = y_0 + \int_{x_0}^{x} f(t, \psi(t)) \, dt
$$

Consider the absolute value of the difference between the two solutions, $ u(x) = |\phi(x) - \psi(x)|$. Subtracting the integral equations satisfied by both and taking the absolute value:

$$
u(x) = \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} [f(t, \phi(t)) - f(t, \psi(t))] \, dt \right| \leq \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} |f(t, \phi(t)) - f(t, \psi(t))| \, dt \right|
$$

Applying the Lipschitz condition again:

$$
u(x) \leq L \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} |\phi(t) - \psi(t)| \, dt \right| = L \left| \int_{x_0}^{x} u(t) \, dt \right|
$$

Now I need to derive $ u(x) \equiv 0 $ from this integral inequality. Without loss of generality, consider the case of $ x \geq x_0 $. Define $ U(x) = \int_{x_0}^{x} u(t) \, dt $. Then $ U'(x) = u(x)$. The above inequality becomes:

$$
U'(x) \leq L \cdot U(x)
$$

Rearranging terms gives $ U'(x) - L U(x) \leq 0 $. Multiplying both sides by the integrating factor $ e^{-Lx}$:

$$
\frac{d}{dx} \left( e^{-Lx} U(x) \right) \leq 0
$$

This shows that the function $ e^{-Lx} U(x)$ is non-increasing on $ x \geq x_0 $. At $ x = x_0 $, $ U(x_0) = \int_{x_0}^{x_0} u(t) \, dt = 0 $. Therefore, for all $ x \geq x_0 $, $ e^{-Lx} U(x) \leq 0 $. But since $ e^{-Lx} > 0 $ and $ U(x) = \int_{x_0}^{x} u(t) \, dt \geq 0 $, we conclude $ U(x) \leq 0 $ and $ U(x) \geq 0 $, so $ U(x) = 0 $.

Since $ U(x) = 0 $, its derivative $ u(x) = U'(x)$ must also be 0 (since $ u $ is continuous). Thus, $ u(x) \equiv 0 $, which is $\phi(x) = \psi(x)$, holds for all $ x \geq x_0 $. For the case of $ x \leq x_0 $, the argument is similar.

**Conclusion**: Uniqueness is proven.

If we look from a higher perspective, we can use what is called the $ Banach $ fixed-point theorem. Simply understood, it means that a contraction mapping in a complete metric space has a unique fixed point. A contraction mapping means that the difference between the values of the mapping is less than the original difference, meaning the distance between the two points gets smaller after the mapping. What a complete metric space is, I don't know. But this way, the entire proof can be clearly organized.

First, transform it into an integral equation, choose a suitable small enough interval length so that the mapping corresponding to the integral equation is a contraction mapping (like choosing $ h $ above), and then the fixed-point theorem can be applied.

## Solution Structure of n-th Order Linear Ordinary Differential Equations

This part establishes the basic framework for the solution of linear equations.

- **n-th order linear homogeneous ODE**: It is pointed out that if there exist $ n $ linearly independent particular solutions $\psi_1, \dots, \psi_n $, then the general solution can be expressed as their linear combination $ y = C_1\psi_1 + \dots + C_n\psi_n $. Such equations have **no singular solutions**.
    
- **n-th order non-homogeneous linear ODE**: Its general solution structure is $ y = \psi^* + C_1\psi_1 + \dots + C_n\psi_n $, where $\psi^*$ is a particular solution to the non-homogeneous equation.
    
- **Core Tool Preview**: **Wronskian Determinant**, used to determine the linear dependence of a set of functions.
    

### Second-order linear ODE and the Existence and Uniqueness Theorem

We will specify the discussion to the second-order case.

- **Standard Form**: 
    
    - Non-homogeneous: $ y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = f(x)$
        
    - Homogeneous: $ y'' + p(x)y' + q(x)y = 0 $
        
- **Existence and Uniqueness Theorem**: 
    
    If the coefficients $ p(x), q(x)$ and $ f(x)$ are continuous on the interval $[a, b]$, then for the initial value problem ($ x_0 \in [a, b]$), the equation has exactly one unique solution on the interval.
    

### Definition and Determination of the Wronskian Determinant

We begin to derive the mathematical criterion for determining if solutions are linearly independent.

- **Definition**: For two solutions $\psi_1, \psi_2 $, the Wronskian determinant is defined as:

$$
W(x) = \begin{vmatrix} \psi_1 & \psi_2 \\ \psi_1' & \psi_2' \end{vmatrix}
$$

- **Theorem Core**:$\psi_1, \psi_2 $ On $(a, b)$ the necessary and sufficient condition for linear independence is $ W(x) \neq 0 $(for any arbitrary point in the interval) $ x $).
    

#### Logical Proof of Linear Dependence

The blackboard demonstrates the thought process for proving necessity and sufficiency.

- **Proof by Contradiction/Direct Derivation**: 
    
    - If $\psi_1, \psi_2 $ is linearly dependent, then there exist constants $ k_1, k_2 $, not all zero, such that $ k_1\psi_1 + k_2\psi_2 \equiv 0 $.
        
    - Differentiating yields a system of equations, and from the basics of linear algebra, its coefficient determinant $ W(x)$ must identically equal 0.
        
- **Extension**: Discusses the inevitable connection between $ W(x) \equiv 0 $ and the linear dependence of solutions.
    

#### Conclusion Convergence and Verification

Completing the final logical loop using the uniqueness of the initial value problem.

- **Uniqueness Application**: 
    
    - We construct a function $ Y(x) = C_1\psi_1(x) + C_2\psi_2(x)$.
        
    - If $ W(x_0) = 0 $, then we can find a set of constants $ C_1, C_2 $, not all zero, such that the initial value at a point $ x_0 $ is 0.
        
    - According to the uniqueness theorem, if the initial value is 0 and it satisfies the homogeneous equation, this solution must identically equal 0 throughout the entire interval ($ Y(x) \equiv 0 $).
        
- **Final Determination**: Thus, it is proven that in the framework of ODEs, if the initial value at a point $ W(x_0) = 0 $, the solution set must be linearly dependent; conversely, if it is linearly independent, then $ W(x)$ is never zero throughout the interval.

In the derivation above, we found that assuming the initial value at a point $ W(x)$ to be $ 0 $ leads to it being identically $ 0 $, which actually holds for all linear differential equations.

### Wronskian Determinant
#### 1. Core Background

Consider a second-order linear homogeneous differential equation:

$$
y'' + P(x)y' + Q(x)y = 0
$$

Assume $\varphi_1, \varphi_2 $ are two solutions to this equation.

#### 2. Deconstruction of the Derivation Logic

- **Definition of the Wronskian Determinant**
    
    First, define $ W(x)$ as the determinant of these two solutions:

$$
W(x) = \begin{vmatrix} \varphi_1 & \varphi_2 \\ \varphi_1' & \varphi_2' \end{vmatrix} = \varphi_1 \varphi_2' - \varphi_2 \varphi_1'
$$

- **Differentiating with respect to $ W(x)$**
    
    Using the product rule for differentiation (or the rule for differentiating a determinant):

$$
W'(x) = (\varphi_1 \varphi_2'' + \varphi_1' \varphi_2') - (\varphi_2 \varphi_1'' + \varphi_2' \varphi_1') = \varphi_1 \varphi_2'' - \varphi_2 \varphi_1''
$$

- **Substitution using the original equation**
    
    Since $\varphi_1, \varphi_2 $ satisfies the original equation, we can extract the second derivative term: $\varphi_i'' = -P(x)\varphi_i' - Q(x)\varphi_i $. After substitution, the terms containing $ Q(x)$ cancel out, leaving:

$$
W'(x) = \varphi_1 (-P \varphi_2' - Q \varphi_2) - \varphi_2 (-P \varphi_1' - Q \varphi_1) = -P(x) (\varphi_1 \varphi_2' - \varphi_2 \varphi_1')
$$

That is:

$$
W'(x) = -P(x)W(x)
$$

- **Solving the First-Order Linear Equation**
    
    This is a typical first-order separable differential equation, which simplifies to $ W'(x) + P(x)W(x) = 0 $.
    
    The general solution is obtained through the integrating factor method or separation of variables:

$$
W(x) = C e^{-\int P(x) dx}
$$

If the initial point is given at $ x_0 $, the expression is:

$$
W(x) = W(x_0) e^{-\int_{x_0}^x P(t) dt}
$$

---

#### 3. Key Conclusion and Intuition

The statement “**$ W $ does not change its sign**” is the core value of this theory in qualitative analysis.

- **Sign Determination:** Since the exponential function $ e^{f(x)}$ is always greater than zero, the sign of $ W(x)$ depends entirely on the initial value $ W(x_0)$.
    
- **Preservation of Linear Independence:** As long as the two solutions are linearly independent at some point $ x_0 $ (i.e., $ W(x_0) \neq 0 $), then they are linearly independent at any point in the continuous interval $ P(x)$. Conversely, if it is zero at one point, it is zero everywhere.

### General Solution Structure Theorem

- **Core Conclusion:** If $\varphi_1, \varphi_2 $ are linearly independent, then their linear combination $ y = C_1\varphi_1 + C_2\varphi_2 $ **contains all solutions**.
    
- This means that this set of solutions forms a **basis** for the solution space, and the general solution space of the equation is a two-dimensional vector space.
    

---

#### Logical Deduction

Why the above linear combination can cover all solutions:

1. **Constructing an Initial Value Problem:** Assume the equation $(\star)$ has an arbitrary solution $ u(x)$. At a point $ x_0 $, it satisfies the initial values $ y(x_0) = u(x_0)$ and $ y'(x_0) = u'(x_0)$.
    
2. **Determining Coefficients:** Consider the linear algebra equation system:

$$
\begin{cases} C_1\varphi_1(x_0) + C_2\varphi_2(x_0) = u(x_0) \\ C_1\varphi_1'(x_0) + C_2\varphi_2'(x_0) = u'(x_0) \end{cases}
$$

Because $\varphi_1, \varphi_2 $ are linearly independent, the determinant of this system of equations (that is, $ W(x_0)$) is not zero. According to Cramer's rule, **there must exist a unique set of solutions** $(C_1^*, C_2^*)$.
    
3. **Uniqueness closure:** * Let $ y(x) = C_1^*\varphi_1(x) + C_2^*\varphi_2(x)$, which is obviously also a solution to the original equation.
    
    - Since $ y(x)$ and $ u(x)$ have the exact same initial values at the point $ x_0 $, by the **uniqueness theorem** for differential equations, it must be that **$ u(x) \equiv y(x)$**.
