The previous integration regions were relatively straightforward, but sometimes we need to integrate along curves or over surfaces.

## Line Integrals
### 1. Definition of Line Integrals

For a space curve $ L $, if its parametric equations are:

$$
\begin{cases} x = x(t) \\ y = y(t) \\ z = z(t) \end{cases} \quad t \in [\alpha, \beta], \quad \beta > \alpha
$$

and satisfies $ x, y, z \in C^1([\alpha, \beta])$ (continuously differentiable of the first order), and the tangent vector $(x'(t), y'(t), z'(t)) \neq \vec{0}$.

Then the line integral of a function $ f(x, y, z)$ along the curve $ L $ is:

$$
\int_L f(x, y, z) \, ds = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} f(x(t), y(t), z(t)) \sqrt{[x'(t)]^2 + [y'(t)]^2 + [z'(t)]^2} \, dt
$$

> **Key Point:** The integration path is from point $ A $ to point $ B $, emphasizing "no backtracking".

---

### 2. Invariance under Parameter Transformation

The case of introducing a new parameter $\tau $. Let $ t = t(\tau)$, where $\tau \in [a, b]$.

#### Case ①: $ t'(\tau) > 0 $ (forward transformation)

At this time, $ t(a) = \alpha $, $ t(b) = \beta $. After the integral transformation:

$$
\int_a^b f(P(t(\tau))) \sqrt{[x'(t)]^2 + [y'(t)]^2 + [z'(t)]^2} \cdot t'(\tau) \, d\tau
$$

#### Case ②: $ t'(\tau) < 0 $ (backward transformation)

At this time, $ t(a) = \beta $, $ t(b) = \alpha $. Although the limits of integration are reversed, because the arc differential $ ds $ contains the absolute value term $|t'(\tau)|$, the final result remains consistent:

$$
\int_a^b f(P(t(\tau))) \sqrt{(x')^2 + (y')^2 + (z')^2} \cdot |t'(\tau)| \, d\tau
$$

This is a characteristic of the first type of line integral: it is direction-independent, and the result is the same whether moving forward or backward.

## Second Type Line Integral

### I. Physical Background: Work Done by a Variable Force

The most intuitive physical source for the second type of line integral is calculating the work $\vec{F}$ done by a variable force $ L $ along a curve $ W $.

- **Discretization Idea**: Dividing the curve into $ n $ small segments $\Delta \vec{r}_i $, assuming the force is approximately constant over each small segment.

$$
W \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{F}_i \cdot \Delta \vec{r}_i
$$

- **Limit Process**: When the divisions become infinitely fine, the exact work is obtained:

$$
W = \int_{L} \vec{F}(x, y, z) \cdot d\vec{r}
$$

---

### II. Core Properties

Similar to other integrals:

1. **Linearity**: 

$$
\int_{L} (a\vec{F} + b\vec{G}) \cdot d\vec{r} = a\int_{L} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} + b\int_{L} \vec{G} \cdot d\vec{r}
$$

2. **Directionality**: This is the biggest difference from the first type of integral.

$$
\int_{A \to B} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = -\int_{B \to A} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}
$$

**Conclusion**: The integral result depends on the direction of the path; reversing the direction yields the negative result.
    
3. **Additivity over Segments**: 
    
If the path $ C $ is composed of $ C_1 $ and $ C_2 $ connected head-to-tail, then:

$$
\int_{C} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{C_1} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} + \int_{C_2} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}
$$

---

### III. Definition and Expression Forms

The second type of line integral usually has three equivalent mathematical forms:

- **Vector Form**: $\int_{L} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r}$
    
- **Coordinate Form (Component Form)**:
    
Let $\vec{F} = (P, Q, R)$, the displacement element $ d\vec{r} = (dx, dy, dz)$, then:

$$
\int_{L} P \, dx + Q \, dy + R \, dz
$$

- **Conversion from First Type (Projection Form)**:

$$
\int_{L} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{L} (|\vec{F}| \cos \theta) \, ds
$$

Where $\theta $ is the angle between the force field and the tangent direction of the curve.
    

---

### IV. Calculation Method: Parameterization Method

The core step to convert the line integral into a definite integral is **"unifying the variable"**.

**Given Conditions**:

- The parametric equations of the curve $ L $: $ x=x(t), y=y(t), z=z(t)$
    
- Parameter range: $ t: \alpha \to \beta $ (Note: $\alpha $ corresponds to the starting point $ A $, and $\beta $ corresponds to the ending point $ B $)
    

**Calculation Steps**:

1. Calculate the differential term: $ dx = x'(t)dt, \quad dy = y'(t)dt, \quad dz = z'(t)dt $
    
2. Substitute into the integral formula:

$$
\int_{L} \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \left[ P(x(t), y(t), z(t))x'(t) + Q(\dots)y'(t) + R(\dots)z'(t) \right] dt
$$

---

When calculating the second type of integral, **you do not need** to square and sum under the square root like the first type, nor is it strictly required that the lower limit is less than the upper limit; the limits must strictly follow the path from **start to end**.

Finally, we point out the connection between the first type and the second type of line integrals. Although the definitions of the two types of line integrals are different, they can be converted into each other under certain conditions.

When the curve $ L $ is expressed by the parametric equations

$$
\begin{cases} x = x(t), \\ y = y(t), \quad \alpha \le t \le \beta \\ z = z(t), \end{cases}
$$

$(x'(t), y'(t), z'(t))$ is the tangent vector of the curve, and thus

$$
d\mathbf{r} = (dx, dy, dz) = (x'(t), y'(t), z'(t)) dt
$$

it is also the tangent vector, and its direction is consistent with the direction of the integration path. Also, the magnitude of $ d\mathbf{r}$ is exactly the arc differential, that is

$$
|d\mathbf{r}| = \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2 + (dz)^2} = ds
$$

Let the direction cosine of $ d\mathbf{r}$ be $\cos\alpha, \cos\beta, \cos\gamma $, then

$$
(\cos\alpha, \cos\beta, \cos\gamma) = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{|d\mathbf{r}|} = \left( \frac{dx}{ds}, \frac{dy}{ds}, \frac{dz}{ds} \right)
$$

From this, we get

$$
dx = \cos\alpha ds, \quad dy = \cos\beta ds, \quad dz = \cos\gamma ds
$$

---

Therefore

$$
\int_{\widehat{AB}} P dx + Q dy + R dz = \int_{\widehat{AB}} (P \cos\alpha + Q \cos\beta + R \cos\gamma) ds
$$

Where $\cos\alpha, \cos\beta, \cos\gamma $ is the direction cosine of the tangent line at any point on the curve $\widehat{AB}$ (and its direction is consistent with the direction of integration).

The above formula describes the relationship between the two types of line integrals. It should be noted that $\cos\alpha, \cos\beta, \cos\gamma $ in the formula is related to the direction of the curve. When the direction of the curve changes, $\cos\alpha, \cos\beta, \cos\gamma $ must change its sign.

For a plane curve, the above formula becomes the following form:

$$
\int_{\widehat{AB}} P dx + Q dy = \int_{\widehat{AB}} (P \cos\alpha + Q \cos\beta) ds
$$

Where $\cos\alpha, \cos\beta $ is the direction cosine of the tangent line at any point on the curve $\widehat{AB}$, in the same direction as $\widehat{AB}$.

---

From wandering along a curve to climbing a surface, we are essentially upgrading from "one-dimensional parameterization" to "two-dimensional mapping". The core contradiction is: **How to accurately project (or pull back) a curved area extended in three-dimensional space onto a flat two-dimensional parameter plane?**

We can break this process down into two stages: from the most intuitive "projection method" to the more general "parameter mapping method".

---

## First Type Surface Integral

For a surface explicitly defined by $ S: z = f(x, y)$, its projection onto the $ xy $ plane is $ D $.

$$
\iint_S F(x, y, z) \, dS
$$

This is the first type surface integral, where $ dS $ is the area element and does not carry a direction.
### 1. Geometric Compensation of the Area Element

Imagine an extremely small area element on the surface $ dS $. If you simply replace it with its projection onto the base plane $ d\sigma = dx dy $, you clearly underestimate its actual size because the surface has a "slope".

The core of the derivation lies in:

$$
dS = \sqrt{1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2} \, d\sigma
$$

The radical term here is essentially the concept of the **secant** (which is actually a cross product). If $\gamma $ is the angle between the surface normal vector and the positive direction of the $ z $ axis, then:

$$
dS = \frac{d\sigma}{|\cos \gamma|}
$$

This is a "projection compensation". When you look down from a height (project onto $ D $), you must multiply by an amplification factor to restore the true area of the slope. The specific formula is as follows:

### 2. Calculation Formula

$$
\iint_S F(x, y, z) \, dS = \iint_D F(x, y, f(x, y)) \sqrt{1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2} \, dx dy
$$

Why is it the term under the radical? It is actually still derived from the cross product, just like double integration.

---

## II. Parametric Surface: Microscopic Linearization

When the surface is no longer a simple $ z=f(x, y)$, but a more general parametric equation $\vec{r}(u, v) = (x(u, v), y(u, v), z(u, v))$, the projection method fails. At this point, we need to use the **cross product derivation**.

### 1. "Stretching" of the Local Tangent Plane

A small rectangle $ du \cdot dv $ on the $(u, v)$ plane, when mapped into space, becomes an approximate parallelogram.

- Its two edge vectors are the tangent vectors: $\vec{r}_u du $ and $\vec{r}_v dv $.
    
- This small area element $ dS $ is exactly equal to the magnitude of the cross product of these two vectors:

$$
dS = |\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v| \, du dv
$$

### 2. Geometricization of the Jacobian Determinant

Define $ A, B, C $:

$$
A = \frac{\partial(y, z)}{\partial(u, v)}, \quad B = \frac{\partial(z, x)}{\partial(u, v)}, \quad C = \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)}
$$

The final area element coefficient is $\sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + C^2}$. Structurally, this has a high degree of self-consistency with the $\sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}$ in line integrals. This looks a bit hard to calculate; we can use another method, which might be easier.

To avoid the complex cross-product determinant calculation, we introduce three scalar functions. Let the parametric surface be $\vec{r}(u, v)$, with partial derivative vectors (tangent vectors) being $\vec{r}_u $ and $\vec{r}_v $:

- **$ E = \vec{r}_u \cdot \vec{r}_u = x_u^2 + y_u^2 + z_u^2 $**: Measures the length stretching in the $ u $ direction.
    
- **$ F = \vec{r}_u \cdot \vec{r}_v = x_u x_v + y_u y_v + z_u z_v $**: Measures the angle between the $ u, v $ parameter lines (orthogonality).
    
- **$ G = \vec{r}_v \cdot \vec{r}_v = x_v^2 + y_v^2 + z_v^2 $**: Measures the length stretching in the $ v $ direction.

Then we use:

$$
|\vec{r}_u \times \vec{r}_v|^2 = |\vec{r}_u|^2 |\vec{r}_v|^2 (1 - \cos^2 \theta) = |\vec{r}_u|^2 |\vec{r}_v|^2 - (\vec{r}_u \cdot \vec{r}_v)^2
$$

This also means:

$$
A^2 + B^2 + C^2 = EG - F^2
$$

So we don't need to calculate the determinant cross product; we just need to calculate the dot product and the sum of squares, which is generally easier.

$$
S = \iint_{D'} \sqrt{EG - F^2} \, du dv
$$

### Example
Calculate the surface area of a sphere.
#### 1. Modeling: From Space to Projection Plane

First, we select the upper hemisphere as the object of study.

- **Surface Equation**: $ z = f(x, y) = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2 - y^2}$.
    
- **Projection Region $ D $**: The projection of the sphere onto the $ xy $ plane is a disk $ x^2 + y^2 \leq R^2 $.
    

---

#### 2. Calculating the "Correction Factor" for the Area Element

This is the most crucial step. To find $ dS = \sqrt{1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2} \, dA $, we need to calculate the partial derivatives:

- $ f_x = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{R^2 - x^2 - y^2}}$-$ f_y = \frac{-y}{\sqrt{R^2 - x^2 - y^2}}$
    

Substitute the radical term for simplification:

$$
1 + f_x^2 + f_y^2 = 1 + \frac{x^2}{R^2 - x^2 - y^2} + \frac{y^2}{R^2 - x^2 - y^2} = \frac{R^2}{R^2 - x^2 - y^2}
$$

Therefore, the area element is:

$$
dS = \frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 - x^2 - y^2}} \, dx dy
$$

---

#### 3. Integration Transformation: The Elegance of Polar Coordinates

Integrating directly in Cartesian coordinates would be very painful, so we introduce the **polar coordinate transformation** ($ x = r\cos\theta, y = r\sin\theta $):

- **Jacobian Determinant Compensation**: $ dx dy \to r \, dr d\theta $.
    
- **Simplification of the Integrand**: The denominator becomes $\sqrt{R^2 - r^2}$.
    

The calculation process for the upper hemisphere area $ S_{\text{up}}$ is as follows:

$$
S_{\text{up}} = \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta \int_0^R \frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 - r^2}} r \, dr
$$

Using the method of completing the differential $\int \frac{r}{\sqrt{R^2 - r^2}} dr = -\sqrt{R^2 - r^2}$:

- **Inner Integral Result**: $[-R\sqrt{R^2 - r^2}] \Big|_0^R = 0 - (-R^2) = R^2 $.
    
- **Outer Integral Result**: $ 2\pi \cdot R^2 $.
    

Finally, since this is only the upper hemisphere, the total surface area is $ S_{\text{total}} = 2 \cdot 2\pi R^2 = \mathbf{4\pi R^2}$.

---

## III. Second Type Surface Integral: The Direction of Flux

The normal vector $\vec{n}$ sows the seeds for the second type integral.

- **Physical Essence**: The flux of a fluid passing through a surface.
    
- **Core Difference**: The second type integral is directional (the concept of flux).
    
- **Relation Formula**: 

$$
\iint_S \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_S (\vec{F} \cdot \vec{n}) \, dS
$$

This shows that the second type integral is essentially the **projection weight** of a vector field onto the surface normal.
    

How to calculate the normal vector? We can consider the cross product of two tangent lines.

$$
\vec{n}=\frac{\vec{r_u}\times \vec{r_v}}{|\vec{r_u}\times \vec{r_v}|}
$$

If it is easier and easier to write out $ z=f(x,y)$, taking $ u=x,v=y $, then:

$$
\vec{r_u}=(1,0,f_x),\vec{r_v}=(0,1,f_y)
$$

It is not difficult to obtain the normal vector:

$$
\vec{n}=\frac{(-f_x,-f_y,1)}{\sqrt{1+f_x^2+f_y^2}}
$$

This looks much better. Of course, we still consider the general case and substitute $\vec{n}$ to see:

$$
\iint_S \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{S} = \iint_S (\vec{F} \cdot \vec{n}) \, dS=\iint_S\vec{F}\cdot (\vec{r_u}\times\vec{r_v})d\sigma
$$

Notice that the ratio coefficients of $ dS $ and $ d\sigma $ are exactly the denominator of $\vec{n}$, which is the magnitude of the cross product, so they cancel out, and we only need to perform a double integral on the flat $ u,v $ plane. However, we still need to calculate a cross product, which is a bit troublesome.

We can simplify the form $ fancy $ a bit. Suppose $\vec{F} = (P, Q, R)$, the parametric equations are $\vec{r}(u, v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v), z(u,v))$, then the mixed product can be written directly as:

$$
\iint_{D} \begin{vmatrix} P & Q & R \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \end{vmatrix} du dv
$$

Or write it in component form:

$$
\iint_{D} \left( P \frac{\partial(y, z)}{\partial(u, v)} + Q \frac{\partial(z, x)}{\partial(u, v)} + R \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} \right) du dv
$$

Why do textbooks often show $ P \, dydz + Q \, dzdx + R \, dxdy $? Why write $ dydz $ and not $ dzdy $? In fact, this should be the exterior derivative, which we will discuss later. One can consider it simply that the cross product in the order of $ y-z $ is exactly in the direction of $ x $, and similarly for the other coordinates.

Then the actual calculation is nothing to say, it's completely following the procedure: calculate the cross product, dot product, and integrate. However, attention must be paid to the direction and symmetry simplification.
