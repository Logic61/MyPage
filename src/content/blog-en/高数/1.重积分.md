# Definition
In single-variable calculus, we defined the definite integral using Riemann sums. Similarly, we can define multiple integrals using it:

**Definition of the Riemann sum for a double integral:**

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
=\lim_{\max \Delta S_{ij}\to 0}
\sum_{i,j} f(\xi_{ij},\eta_{ij})\,\Delta S_{ij}
$$

**Triple Integral:**

$$
\iiint_\Omega f(x,y,z)\,dV
=\lim_{\max \Delta V_{ijk}\to 0}
\sum_{i,j,k} f(\xi_{ijk},\eta_{ijk},\zeta_{ijk})\,\Delta V_{ijk}
$$

---

The idea is the same in each case. Subdivide the region into small pieces so we can approximate curved shapes by straight ones and make the calculation easier. The maximum diameter of the pieces must tend to zero.
## Basic Properties of Double Integrals

This part can be skimmed quickly; it almost entirely aligns with intuition ()


Suppose $ f,g $ are integrable on the region $ D $ (or $\Omega $), and $\lambda,\mu \in \mathbb{R}$.

---

### 1. Linearity

$$
\iint_D (\lambda f + \mu g)\,dA
=
\lambda \iint_D f\,dA
+
\mu \iint_D g\,dA
$$

Triple integrals similarly:

$$
\iiint_\Omega (\lambda f + \mu g)\,dV
=
\lambda \iiint_\Omega f\,dV
+
\mu \iiint_\Omega g\,dV
$$

> The integral is the limit of weighted sums. Its linearity comes from the linearity of those sums.

---

### 2. Additivity over domain

If $ D = D_1 \cup D_2 $ and $ D_1,D_2 $ are non-overlapping, then

$$
\iint_D f\,dA
=
\iint_{D_1} f\,dA
+
\iint_{D_2} f\,dA
$$

More generally:

$$
D = \bigcup_{k=1}^n D_k \quad (\text{Pairwise non-intersecting})
\Rightarrow
\iint_D f\,dA = \sum_{k=1}^n \iint_{D_k} f\,dA
$$

> Essence: The Riemann sum can be split into blocks.

---

### 3. Monotonicity

If $ f(x,y) \le g(x,y)$, then

$$
\iint_D f\,dA \le \iint_D g\,dA
$$

Specifically:

$$
f(x,y) \ge 0 \Rightarrow \iint_D f\,dA \ge 0
$$

> Essence: The value on each small block does not exceed it, and the sum naturally does not exceed it.

---

### 4. Bounding

If

$$
m \le f(x,y) \le M
$$

Then

$$
m \cdot |D|
\le
\iint_D f\,dA
\le
M \cdot |D|
$$

where $|D|$ represents the area of the region.

Three-dimensional correspondence:

$$
m|\Omega| \le \iiint_\Omega f\,dV \le M|\Omega|
$$

> Essence: If the function is bounded, ⇒ the integral is bounded.

---

### 5. Absolute Value Inequality

$$
\left| \iint_D f\,dA \right|
\le
\iint_D |f|\,dA
$$

> Essence: The integral will not be larger than 

---

### 6. Integral Mean Value Theorem

If $ f $ is continuous on the bounded closed region $ D $, then there exists $(\xi,\eta)\in D $ such that

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
=
f(\xi,\eta)\cdot |D|
$$

Three-dimensional:

$$
\iiint_\Omega f\,dV
=
f(\xi,\eta,\zeta)\cdot |\Omega|
$$

> Essence: Integral = “some representative value × volume”.

---

### 7. Relationship with Constant Functions

$$
\iint_D 1\,dA = |D|
$$

$$
\iiint_\Omega 1\,dV = |\Omega|
$$

> Essence: The integral unifies the concept of “area/volume”.

---
So how to calculate it? Let's first look at two dimensions.

# Calculation
What we calculate is essentially a single integral, so naturally, we think about whether we can convert a double integral into a single integral, which is what is called iterated integration:

**Fubini's Theorem (Basic Form):**

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
=
\int_a^b \left(\int_{c(x)}^{d(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\right)\,dx
$$

Or

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
=
\int_c^d \left(\int_{a(y)}^{b(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\right)\,dy
$$

---
### Division
However, unlike the one-dimensional case, multiple variables constrain each other, so the limits of integration may include other variables, which is often the source of complex forms.

We should divide the region well so that the limits of the variables are clear:

**Type I Region (Vertical Slicing):**

$$
D=\{(x,y)\mid a\le x\le b,\ c(x)\le y\le d(x)\}
$$

Corresponds to

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
=
\int_a^b \int_{c(x)}^{d(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx
$$

**Type II Region (Horizontal Slicing):**

$$
D=\{(x,y)\mid c\le y\le d,\ a(y)\le x\le b(y)\}
$$

Corresponds to

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA
=
\int_c^d \int_{a(y)}^{b(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
$$

**Variable Dependency Relationship (Essential Constraint):**

$$
\text{The upper and lower limits of the inner integral variable can include the outer variable,But not vice versa}
$$

---

Thus, applying the definite integral repeatedly solves all problems. Three dimensions, and even higher dimensions, are just repetitions.

**Triple Integral Iterated Form:**

$$
\iiint_\Omega f(x,y,z)\,dV
=
\int_a^b \int_{c(x)}^{d(x)} \int_{e(x,y)}^{g(x,y)} f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx
$$

---
### Change of Variables
Then, we can also use a change of variables to simplify the integral. In one dimension:

**One-Dimensional Change of Variables Formula:**

$$
\int f(x)\,dx = \int f(x(t))\,x'(t)\,dt
$$

Then, how to change $ dxdy $?

Let the parameter transformation be:

$$
(x,y) = (x(u,v),\,y(u,v))
$$

Consider the differential change:

$$
\mathbf r(u+du,v)\approx \mathbf r + \mathbf r_u\,du
$$

$$
\mathbf r(u,v+dv)\approx \mathbf r + \mathbf r_v\,dv
$$

Then the area element is:

**Area Element (Cross Product Form):**

$$
dA = |\mathbf r_u \times \mathbf r_v|\,dudv
$$

The calculation yields, which is exactly the Jacobian determinant:

**Jacobian Determinant (Two Dimensions):**

$$
\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}
=
\begin{vmatrix}
\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\\
\frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}
\end{vmatrix}
$$

Thus we get:

**Double Integral Change of Variables Formula:**

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dxdy
=
\iint_{D'} f(x(u,v),y(u,v))
\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|
\,dudv
$$

---

In three dimensions, we become the mixed product:

$$
V = |\mathbf{A} \cdot (\mathbf{B} \times \mathbf{C})|
$$

Thus the change of variables becomes:

$$
\begin{aligned} dV' &= |(\mathbf{r}_u du) \cdot ((\mathbf{r}_v dv) \times (\mathbf{r}_w dw))| \\ &= |\mathbf{r}_u \cdot (\mathbf{r}_v \times \mathbf{r}_w)| \cdot du dv dw \end{aligned}
$$

Which is exactly the Jacobian determinant:

$$
\mathbf{r}_u \cdot (\mathbf{r}_v \times \mathbf{r}_w) = \det \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial w} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial w} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} \end{pmatrix}
$$

**Jacobian Determinant in Three Dimensions:**

$$
\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}
=
\begin{vmatrix}
x_u & x_v & x_w\\
y_u & y_v & y_w\\
z_u & z_v & z_w
\end{vmatrix}
$$

**Change of Variables Formula for Triple Integrals:**

$$
\iiint_\Omega f(x,y,z)\,dV
=
\iiint_{\Omega'} f(x(u,v,w),y(u,v,w),z(u,v,w))
\left|\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)}\right|
\,dudvdw
$$

---

We can calculate common coordinate transformations:

**Polar Coordinates:**

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta
$$

$$
dxdy = r\,dr\,d\theta
$$

---

**Cylindrical Coordinates:**

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\quad z=z
$$

$$
dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz
$$

---

**Spherical Coordinates:**

$$
x=\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\quad
y=\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\quad
z=\rho\cos\varphi
$$

$$
dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta
$$

---

Is it a Jacobian determinant for general dimensions? We can see it this way:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} dx \\ dy \end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} du \\ dv \end{pmatrix}
$$

Connecting to linear algebra, we know that the determinant represents volume, so indeed, for the general case, we can just use the determinant!

# Practical Application

So, we can summarize as follows:

1. Consider symmetry and change variables

2. Divide the variable region

3. Integrate

Let's work through an example.

### 1. Consider symmetry and simplify the integrand

Observe the numerator of the integrand $(x+y+z)^2 = x^2+y^2+z^2 + 2xy + 2yz + 2zx $.

Since the integration region $\Omega $ has symmetry with respect to the three coordinate planes $ Oyz $, $ Oxz $, and $ Oxy $, based on the property that **the integral of an odd function over a symmetric region is zero**:

- The cross-term $\iiint_{\Omega} 2xy \, dV = \iiint_{\Omega} 2yz \, dV = \iiint_{\Omega} 2zx \, dV = 0 $.
    

Therefore, the complex numerator is simplified by 

$$
f(x,y,z) = \frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^2} = \frac{1}{x^2+y^2+z^2}
$$

---

### 2. Change variables and divide the variable region

To handle the $ r^2 $ in the denominator and the rotational symmetry of the region, we introduce a spherical coordinate change of variables:

- **Sphere:** $\rho = 2 $ - **Paraboloid:** $ z = \frac{1}{3}(x^2+y^2) \implies \rho \cos\phi = \frac{1}{3}\rho^2 \sin^2\phi \implies \rho = \frac{3\cos\phi}{\sin^2\phi}$ By solving the system of equations $ 2 = \frac{3\cos\phi}{\sin^2\phi}$, we get the critical angle at the intersection line, **$\phi = \frac{\pi}{3}$**.

Based on this, we divide the integration region $\Omega $ into two clearly ordered parts in the $\phi $ direction:

- **Region I ($ 0 \le \phi \le \frac{\pi}{3}$):** Radially limited by the sphere, $ 0 \le \rho \le 2 $.
    
- **Region II ($\frac{\pi}{3} < \phi \le \frac{\pi}{2}$):** Radially limited by the paraboloid, $ 0 \le \rho \le \frac{3\cos\phi}{\sin^2\phi}$.
    

---

### 3. Integration Calculation (Jacobi Cancellation and Piecewise Summation)

Using the spherical coordinate volume element $ dV = \rho^2 \sin\phi \, d\rho d\phi d\theta $, the $ 1/\rho^2 $ in the integrand cancels with the Jacobi factor, simplifying the integral to a piecewise iterated integral over $\sin\phi $:

$$
I = \int_{0}^{2\pi} d\theta \left[ \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{3}} d\phi \int_{0}^{2} \sin\phi \, d\rho + \int_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} d\phi \int_{0}^{\frac{3\cos\phi}{\sin^2\phi}} \sin\phi \, d\rho \right]
$$

**Step-by-step solution:**

- **Part 1:** $ 2\pi \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{3}} 2\sin\phi \, d\phi = 4\pi [-\cos\phi]_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{3}} = 4\pi (\frac{1}{2}) = 2\pi $ - **Part 2:** $ 2\pi \int_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \sin\phi \cdot \frac{3\cos\phi}{\sin^2\phi} \, d\phi = 6\pi \int_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{\cos\phi}{\sin\phi} \, d\phi = 6\pi [\ln(\sin\phi)]_{\frac{\pi}{3}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}$
    
    Substituting yields: $ 6\pi (\ln 1 - \ln \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}) = -6\pi \ln \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$
    

**Final Result:**

$$
I = 2\pi - 6\pi \ln \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2\pi \left( 1 - 3\ln\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \right)
$$
