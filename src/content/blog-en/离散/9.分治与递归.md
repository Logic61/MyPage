#### Tower of Hanoi
The Tower of Hanoi is a very famous game with three pegs. Initially, one peg has $ n $ disks stacked on it, with the largest at the bottom and the smallest at the top. Each move consists of moving one disk, and the topmost disk must be moved first. The disks must move to a peg with a smaller disk on top. How many moves are needed to move all the disks to another peg? Assume it is $ T_n $ moves.

The classic approach is **recursion**. To move $ n $ disks, you can first move the top $ n-1 $ disks to another peg, move the largest disk to the remaining peg, and then move the remaining $ n-1 $ disks to the target peg. Each process is equivalent to moving disks of a smaller number. Thus, we have:

$$
T_n\leq 2 T_{n-1}+1
$$

In fact, this is the best method we can achieve. We must always move the last disk, at which point, the other disks must be on the remaining pegs, meaning the number of moves at this point is $\ge T_{n-1}$. Then there must be at least one move, and finally, it must be at least $ T_{n-1}$, therefore $ T_n\ge 2T_{n-1}+1 $, so:

$$
T_n=2T_{n-1}+1
$$

How to solve it? In high school, we learned undetermined coefficients, and the credit from doing problems is that due to excessive familiarity, we mechanically construct a geometric sequence by adding one to both sides. But generally, if we haven't seen it?

**Guess and verify** is a very common mathematical method. After guessing the answer, proving it using mathematical induction is a very good path. We calculate the first few terms and can easily find that it is $ 1,3,7 $, so we naturally guess that the general term is $ 2^n-1 $, and then we can use mathematical induction to prove it.

If you still can't notice it at this point, you can directly use **nested recurrence relations** layer by layer, and it is easy to observe such a structure:

$$
T_n=1+2+4+...+2^{n-2}+2^{n-1}T_1
$$

Where $ T_1 $ is also $ 1 $, so we can also get the result using the sum of a geometric series.

#### Merge Sort

To sort $ n>1 $ numbers $ x_1,...x_n $, for convenience, let $ n=2^t $. Divide into two steps:

 - Sort $ x_1,...,x_{n/2}$ and $ x_{n/2+1},...,x_n $
 - Merge the two sides by comparing the minimum of the two sides.

Let $ T(n)$ be the number of comparisons required by merge sort in the worst case.

During the merge, we compare $ n-1 $ times in the worst case. Sorting separately takes $ 2T(\frac{n}{2})$. Thus:

$$
T(n)=2T(\frac{n}{2})+n-1
$$

This formula cannot be seen at a glance. Usually, we calculate a few terms and observe. The first few terms are $ T(1)=0,T(2)=1,T(4)=5,T(8)=17,T(16)=49 $
It doesn't look easy to guess, so we try to substitute the recurrence relation.

$$
T(n)=n-1+2T(\frac{n}{2})=n-1+2(\frac{n}{2}-1+2T(\frac{n}{4}))
$$

Substituting again gives

$$
T(n)=n-1+n-2+n-4+8T(\frac{n}{8})
$$

If you can bear to simplify the previous expression, you can always guess it (

$$
T(n)=n-1+n-2+\dots+n-2^{i-1}+2^iT(\frac{n}{2^i})
$$

Finally, under our beautiful assumption, let $ i=log_2n=t $, at which point the last one is $ T(1)=0 $, giving

$$
T(n)=\sum_{i=0}^t(n-2^i)=nlog_2n-n+1
$$

Almost linear, much faster than comparing one by one.

Comparison shows:

$$
T(n)=2T(n-1)+1\implies T(n)\sim2^n
$$

$$
T(n)=2T(\frac{n}{2})+n-1\implies T(n)\sim nlog_2n
$$

The two are similar in form but vastly different, due to the difference in $ T()$.

>[!Induction]
>Base step: $ S(1)=1-1=0 $, which is true.
>

Although we don't know the parity of $ n $, $\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \rfloor + \lceil \frac{n}{2} \rceil $ will always be $ n $, so $ S(n)=n-1 $ also holds.
>
>Then, using the principle of mathematical induction, the original proposition is proven.

So, that means $ S(n)\sim n $, from $ n-1 $ to $ 1 $, the logarithm disappears.

#### Akra-Bazzi Theorem

A recurrence relation of the form:

$$
T(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i T(b_i x + h_i(x)) + f(x)
$$

- **$ a_i $**: The coefficient of the subproblem (like $ 2 $ before $ T(x/2)$ in your diagram).
    
- **$ b_i $**: The reduction ratio of the scale (like $ 1/2 $ and $ 3/4 $).
    
- **$ f(x)$**: The merging cost, which must be a polynomial, and requires $|f'(x)|\leq x^c $.
    
- **$ h_i(x)$**:Microscopic perturbations (like the previous rounding symbol $\lfloor \dots \rfloor $)), requirement $|h_i(x)|\leq O(\frac{x}{log_2x})$.

##### Step 1: Find the critical exponent $ p $. This is the most crucial step. You need to find a $ p $ such that the following equation holds:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i b_i^p = 1
$$

This $ p $ determines the growth rate of the "leaf nodes" in the recursion tree.

##### Step 2: Substitute into the formula

$$
T(x) = \Theta\left( x^p \left( 1 + \int_{1}^{x} \frac{f(u)}{u^{p+1}} du \right) \right)
$$

The recurrence relation that looks scary becomes mindless calculation. But $ p $ is not always that pretty, for example:

$$
T(x)=3T(\frac{x}{3})+4T(\frac{x}{4})+x^2
$$

We have to consider:

$$
3(\frac{1}{3})^p+4(\frac{1}{4})^p=1
$$

$ p $ is not an integer. Calculating $ p=2 $, we find that the left side is smaller than $ 1 $, which means $ p<2 $, and similarly we find $ p>1 $. But the advantage is that we can look directly at the integral:

$$
T(x)=\Theta(x^p+x^p\int_1^x\frac{u^2}{u^{1+p}}du)
$$

The integral term is very simple:

$$
x^p\int_1^x\frac{u^2}{u^{1+p}}du=x^p(x^{2-p}-1)\frac{1}{2-p}
$$

We get $ x^2 $, and since $ p<2 $, we can discard it, which gives:

$$
T(x)=\Theta(x^2)
$$

We do not need to know the specific value of $ p $. In fact, this is a theorem:

$$
\text{If: }f(x)=\Theta(x^t),t\ge 0, \text{such that} \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_ib_i^t< 1,\text{then } T(x)=\Theta(g(x))
$$
