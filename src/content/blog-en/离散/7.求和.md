Let's briefly introduce some summation.



# Precise Summation
One year you get $ 5 $ ten thousand, continuously for $ 20 $ years, or all at once you get $ 50 $ ten thousand? What would you choose? Because of the factor of inflation, this requires detailed consideration.

Simply assuming an annual inflation rate of $ p $, then $ n $ years later, $ m $ yuan would only be equivalent to $\frac{m}{(1+p)^n}$ yuan today. So the total value of the first option is:

$$
v = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{m}{(1+p)^i}
$$

This is the geometric series summation that we are familiar with.

##  Perturbation Method

The method of subtracting staggered terms multiplied by a ratio, which we are familiar with, is actually the so-called perturbation method. We will omit the details. Then, by performing the summation and simplifying, we get:

$$
v=m(\frac{1+p-\frac{1}{(1+p)^{n-1}}}{p})
$$

At $ n\to \infty $, $ v\to m(1+\frac{1}{p})$, this is equivalent to earning $ 5 $ ten thousand every year, a perpetuity, even if paid infinitely, the total amount is finite and may not be very large. 

Sometimes what we encounter is not so friendly, for example, $\sum\limits_{i=1}^n ix^i $. In this case, applying the perturbation method turns it into the common staggered subtraction in high school, which we will not show.

## Differentiation Method

For $ x\neq 1 $, $\sum\limits_{i=0}^n x^i=\frac{1-x^{n+1}}{1-x}$, differentiating gives

$$
\sum\limits_{i=0}^n ix^{i-1}=\frac{1-(n+1)x^n+nx^{n+1}}{(1-x)^2}
$$

Multiplying both sides by $ x $ gives $\sum\limits_{i=0}^n ix^i $. Then setting $ n\to \infty $ gives $\sum\limits_{i=0}^\infty ix^i = \frac{x}{(1-x)^2}$. Substituting our annuity $ m $ and interest rate $ p $ gives $ v=\frac{m(1+p)}{p^2}$ .

Similarly, integration can yield $\sum\limits_{i=0}^n \frac{t^i}{i}$:

$$
\int_{0}^{t} \left( \sum_{i=0}^{n} x^i \right) dx = \int_{0}^{t} \frac{1-x^{n+1}}{1-x} dx
$$

Which is

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n+1} \frac{t^k}{k} = -\ln(1-t) - \int_{0}^{t} \frac{x^{n+1}}{1-x} dx
$$

**When $ n \to \infty $ and $|t| < 1 $:**

Since $ x^{n+1}$ tends to 0, the complex error term integral vanishes. Thus, we obtain the famous **Taylor series** in calculus:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{t^k}{k} = \ln \left( \frac{1}{1-t} \right)
$$

# Integral Estimation
Often, summation cannot be solved precisely, so we usually use integral estimation. By drawing an arbitrary **monotonically increasing** curve, we can try to separate the area enclosed by it using intervals of $ 1 $ squares. If we shift the curve by $ 1 $ length, we cover all the squares, and adding the last square gives:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n f(i) \leq f(n) + \int_1^n f(x)dx
$$

Similarly, we have:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n f(i) \geq f(1) + \int_1^n f(x)dx
$$

Combining these gives the upper and lower bounds of $\sum_{i=1}^n f(i)$. For example, applying this to $ f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ gives:

$$
\frac{2}{3}n^{3/2}+\frac{1}{3}\leq \sum_{i=1}^n \sqrt{i} \leq \frac{2}{3}n^{3/2} + \sqrt{n}-\frac{2}{3}
$$

We call the remainder term what comes after, because it is much smaller than the main term, and we denote it as:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \sqrt{i} = \frac{2}{3}n^{3/2} + \delta (n) \text{, where } \frac{1}{3}\leq \delta(n) \leq \sqrt{n}-\frac{2}{3}
$$

And:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n \sqrt{i} \sim 
\frac{2}{3}n^{3/2}
$$

The formal definition is:

$$
g(x) \sim h(x) \text{ means } \lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{g(x)}{h(x)}=1
$$

Here, $ g(x),h(x)$ is called asymptotic equality.

For a **monotonically decreasing** curve, we just need to change the direction of the inequality:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n f(i) \geq f(n) + \int_1^n f(x)dx
$$

And

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n f(i) \leq f(1) + \int_1^n f(x)dx
$$

Similarly, let's try $ f(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{i}}$:

$$
f(n) + 2\sqrt{n} - 2 \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{i}} \le f(1) + 2\sqrt{n} - 2
$$

$$
\implies \frac{1}{\sqrt n}+2\sqrt{n} - 2 < \quad \dots \quad \le 2\sqrt{n} - 1
$$

Then it is:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{i}} \sim 2\sqrt n
$$
