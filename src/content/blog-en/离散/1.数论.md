# Euclidean Algorithm
Knowing that the measurable water is the greatest common divisor, how to calculate it? Using the fact that:
$\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$
allows us to quickly find the greatest common divisor using the Euclidean Algorithm.

>[!Proof]
>Without loss of generality, let $ a \leq b $.
>Let $ a = qb + r $, where $ r = a \bmod b $. We want to prove $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r)$.
>We consider proving that they divide each other to show that the two are equal.
>First, prove that the left side divides the right side. Without loss of generality, let the left side be $ x $ and the right side be $ y $. Then from $ a=qb+r $, we have $ k_1x = k_2x + r $, which means $ r=(k_1-k_2)x $, so there is $ r=0,a=qb $ or $ x|r $. If $ r=0 $ actually has $ x|r $, and also $ x|b $, then $ x|gcd(b,r)=y $.
>Next, prove that the right side divides the left side. It can be done similarly, $ a=(k_3+k_4)y $, so as above, it is proven.
>Combining both aspects proves the original proposition.

## Corollary

#### 1
If $ a|bc $, and $(a,b)=1 $, then $ a|c $.

>[!Proof]
>Using Bézout's Identity, $ au+bv=1 $, and multiplying by $ c $ gives $ acu+bvc=c $. Since the left side is divisible by $ a $, the right side must also be.

#### 2
If $ a|c $, $ b|c $, and $(a,b)=1 $, then $ ab|c $.

>[!Proof]
>From $ a|c $, we can set $ c=qa $, so $ b|c=qa $. By the corollary $ 1 $, we get $ b|q $, so we can set $ q=mb $, then $ c=mab $, so the proposition is true.

#### 3
If $(a,b)=1 $, $(a,bc)=(a,c)$.

>[!Proof]
>It is enough to prove that the two divide each other.
>Let $ d_1=(a,bc),d_2=(a,c)$.
>On one hand, $ d_2|a,d_2|c $, so $ d_2|bc $ and $ d_2|a $, so $ d_2|d_1 $.
>On the other hand, $ d_1|bc $, from $ d_1 $ being a divisor of $ a $ and the corollary $ 1 $, $(d_1,b)=1 $, $ d_1|c $, so there is also $ d_1|d_2 $.
>Combining both aspects proves the proposition.

# Some Definitions
### Relatively prime
If the greatest common divisor of $ a $ and $ b $ is $ 1 $, that is, $\exists x, y \in Z , st, ax+by=1 $.
### Congruent
If $ n|(a-b)$. Written as $ a \equiv b \pmod n $.  
### Inverse
If $ xx^{-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$ and $ x^{-1} \in \{1,2,...,n-1\}$, then the two are modular inverses of each other.
##### Bézout's Identity and Modular Inverse
Bézout's identity gives, $ gcd(a,b)=ax+by $, if $ a,b $ are coprime, then there exists $ ax+by=1 $, and thus we have $ ax\equiv 1 \pmod y $ or $ by \equiv 1 \pmod x $, which means we can find the inverse of $ a,b $.
### Remainder
$ m=kd+r $, then $ r $ is the remainder, which is $ rem(m,d)$. It is not difficult to see that $ rem(m,d) \equiv m \pmod d $.

# Cryptography and Number Theory
Although computer calculation speed is fast, it is still limited. Factoring a large integer is still difficult, and this can be utilized for information encryption.

Next, let's look at two simple encryption methods.
### Encryption Method 1
First, convert the information into an integer $ m $, for example, by numbering letters from $ 01 $ to $ 26 $, and then by calculation, add a few numbers appropriately to make it a prime number. Next, choose a large prime number $ k $, and the encryption method is $ m'=mk $. $ m'$ is the encrypted information, and as long as the prime number is large enough, it is almost impossible to crack. To decrypt, one only needs to divide by $ k $.

But if a person intercepts two pieces of information, they have $ m_1'=m_1k, m_2'=m_2k $, then by calculating $ gcd(m,m')$, they get the key $ k $, which is obviously very insecure.

### Encryption Method 2
This time, let $ m'=rem(mk,p)$. $ p $ is a prime number.
If we want to decrypt, using the remainder and $ m'$ congruence, we have $ m' \equiv mk \pmod p $, if $ k^{-1}$ exists, then multiplying both sides by it gives $ m \equiv m' \cdot k^{-1} \pmod p $, by appropriate selection, let $ m<p $ then we can determine $ m $ . 
Using the relationship between congruence and remainder can also be written as $ m=rem(m'k^{-1},p)$.

If a person intercepts both $ m,m'$, then at this time they can also crack $ k $.
>[!Cracking Method ]
>Since $ p $ is a prime number, and $ m,p $ are coprime, we can obtain $\exists x,y \in Z ,mx+py=1 $, and here $ x $ is the inverse of $ m $ modulo $ p $.
>by $ m' \equiv mk \pmod p $ multiplying both sides $ m $ the inverse of, we get $ k\equiv m'm^{-1} \pmod p $ .

### Euler's Totient Function
Usually denoted as $\phi(n)$, it represents the number of integers in $\{1,2,...,n-1\}$ that are coprime to $ n $.
For example, $\phi(12)=4,\phi(15)=8 $.
#### Euler's Theorem
Although we do not know how many Euler's theorems there are,
This Euler's theorem is:

$$
\text{if}gcd(n,k)=1,\text{then}k^{\phi(n)}\equiv 1 \pmod n
$$

Proof:
>[!Lemma1]
>If $ gcd(n,k)=1 $, then $ ak \equiv bk \pmod n $ $\implies $ $ a\equiv b \pmod n $ 
>Proof:
> Since $ n,k $ are coprime, by using Bézout's identity, we know that $ k^{-1}$ exists, and multiplying both sides gives the result.

>[!Lemma2]
>If $ gcd(n,k)=1 $, then let $ r=\phi(n)$, and assume that $ k_1,...,k_r $ and $ n $ are coprime, then we have $\{rem(k_1k,n),...rem(k_rk,n)\}=\{k_1,...,k_r\}$.
>Proof:
>① Prove that the first set also has $ r $ elements.
>Assume there are repetitions, let's assume $ k_i\neq k_j , rem(k_ik,n)=rem(k_jk,n)$, then $ k_ik\equiv k_jk \pmod n $, by $ Lemma1 $, then we have $ k_i=k_j $, a contradiction, so the assumption is false, meaning the first set has $ r $ distinct elements.
>② Prove that every element in the first set is coprime to $ n $.
>Consider an arbitrary $ k_i $, we have $ rem(k_ik, n)\equiv k_ik \pmod n $. Since all $ k_i,k $ are coprime to $ n $ and non-zero, the proposition is true.
>Combining ① and ②, we know that the proposition is true.

>[!Proof]
>Now, using $ Lemma2 $, we have;
>

$$
\begin{aligned} k_1 \cdot k_2 \cdot \cdot \cdot k_r&=rem(k_1k,n) \cdot rem(k_2k,n) \cdot \cdot \cdot rem(k_3k,n) \\ &\equiv k_1k \cdot k_2k \cdot \cdot \cdot k_rk \pmod n \\ &\equiv k_1 \cdot k_2 \cdot \cdot \cdot k_r \cdot k^r \pmod n\end{aligned}
$$

>Using $ Lemma1 $, cancel out $ k_1 \cdot k_2 \cdot \cdot \cdot k_r $ from both sides, and recalling $ r=\phi(n)$, we get
>

$$
k^{\phi(n)}\equiv 1 \pmod n
$$

>This completes the proof.

The second $ Lemma $ seems like an antelope with no tracks, but in fact, it can be viewed as multiplying the $ k_i $ coprime to $ n $ by $ k $, which are still coprime, but in order to restrict it to $ n $, we take a modulo, so formally it can be written as a remainder. Thus, the core of the proof is that the numbers after taking the modulo are not repeated. Then we apply 'counting twice' to get the magical Euler's theorem. #### Fermat's Little Theorem
Let $ p $ be a prime number, then $\phi(p)=p-1 $, so we have

$$
k^{p-1}\equiv1 \pmod p
$$

This is Fermat's Little Theorem. By using Fermat's Little Theorem, we can directly get the inverse of $ k $, noting that $ k^{p-1}=kk^{p-2}$, so $ k^{-1}=k^{p-2}$.

### RSA Encryption
#### 1. The Mathematical Foundation of RSA

The security of RSA is based on a simple number theory problem: **Integer Factorization**.

- Multiplying two large prime numbers $ p $ and $ q $ to get $ n $ is very fast.
    
- But if you are only given $ n $, it is extremely difficult to factor it back into $ p \times q $ (when $ n $ is large enough).
    

---

#### 2. Algorithm Flow: How to Use Euler's Theorem?

##### Step 1: Prepare the "Lock" and the "Key"

1. Select two large prime numbers $ p, q $, and calculate $ n = p \times q $.
    
2. Calculate the Euler's totient function value of $ n $: $\phi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$.
    
3. Select a public exponent $ e $ (usually 65537), which requires $ gcd(e, \phi(n)) = 1 $.
    
4. **Key Point:** Find the **modular multiplicative inverse** $ d $ of $ e $ modulo $\phi(n)$.
    
    That is, it must satisfy: $ e \cdot d \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}$.
    

- **Public Key (The visible lock):** $(n, e)$ - **Private Key (The hidden key):** $(n, d)$
    

##### Step 2: Encryption Process

Suppose you want to send the message $ M $:

$$
C = M^e \pmod n
$$

Here, $ C $ is the ciphertext.

##### Step 3: Decryption Process (Euler's Theorem Appears!)

After receiving $ C $, the recipient calculates using the private key $ d $:

$$
M = C^d \pmod n
$$

---

#### 3. Why is this decryption successful?

This is the magic of mathematics. Let's expand the decryption formula:

$$
C^d = (M^e)^d = M^{ed} \pmod n
$$

Because we previously constructed $ ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}$, it can be written as $ ed = k \cdot \phi(n) + 1 $.

Then:

$$
M^{ed} = M^{k\phi(n) + 1} = (M^{\phi(n)})^k \cdot M^1 \pmod n
$$

According to **Euler's Theorem**, $ M^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n $, so:

$$
(1)^k \cdot M \equiv M \pmod n
$$

**The message is perfectly restored!**

---

#### 4. Why is it secure?

- **The attacker knows:** $ n $ and $ e $ (the public key).
    
- **The attacker wants:** $ d $ (the private key).
    
- **Obstacle:** To calculate $ d $, the attacker must first know $\phi(n)$.
    
- **Dead End:** To know $\phi(n)$, one must know $ p $ and $ q $. In other words, the attacker must **factor $ n $**.
    

Currently, factoring a 2048-bit RSA key is impossible in real-time.
