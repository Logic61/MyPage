An introduction to summation and asymptotic notation.

# The harmonic series

How far past the edge of a table can a stack of wooden blocks reach? One approach is greedy: start from the top and slide each block as far as it can go.

Let $ r_k $ be the distance from the edge of the table to the far edge of block $ k $ in the final configuration. With the greedy approach, the center of mass of the top $ k-1 $ blocks sits right over the edge of block $ k $, so $ c_k=r_{k+1}$. By the definition of the center of mass,

$$
c_k=\frac{(k-1)c_{k-1}+1\cdot (r_k-\frac{1}{2})}{k}
$$

Substituting $ c_k=r_{k+1}$ gives

$$
r_{k+1}=r_k-\frac{1}{2k}
$$

In other words, block $ k $ sticks out $\frac{1}{2k}$ farther than block $ k+1 $. Since $ r_{n+1}=0 $,

$$
r_1=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{2i}
$$

The sum

$$
H_n=\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{i}
$$

gives the partial sums of the harmonic series. It diverges, since adding up $\frac{1}{i}$ from $ k $ to $ 2k $ gives more than $\frac{1}{2}$. If that slips past us while we're spacing out, we can estimate it by integration instead. We have $ f(1)=1,f(n)=\frac{1}{n}$, and we compute

$$
\int_1^n \frac{dx}{x}dx=ln(n)
$$

which gives

$$
\frac{1}{n}+ln(n)\leq H_n\leq 1+ln(n)
$$

So $ H_n\sim ln(n)$, which is another way to see that $ H_n $ diverges. Their difference converges to Euler's constant:

$$
\gamma = \lim_{n \to \infty} \left( \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{k} - \ln(n) \right)
$$

# Factorials

The familiar formula

$$
n!=\prod_{i=1}^ni
$$

doesn't give us much of a feel for how large $ n!$ is. A simple idea is to take logarithms:

$$
ln(n!)=\sum_{i+1}^nln(i)
$$

Then we use an integral estimate. With $ f(x)=ln(x)$, we get

$$
\int_1^nlnxdx=(xln(x)-x)\bigg|_1^n=nln(n)-n+1
$$

and hence

$$
nln(n)-n+1\leq ln(n!)\leq ln(n)+nln(n)-n+1
$$

Converting back to factorials gives

$$
\frac{n^n}{e^{n-1}}\leq n!\leq \frac{n^{n+1}}{e^{n-1}}
$$

That's much clearer. In fact, we can be more precise.

## Stirling's formula

$$
n!=(\frac{n}{e})^n\cdot \sqrt{2\pi n}\cdot e^{\epsilon(n)}
$$

Here $\frac{1}{12n+1}\leq \epsilon(n)\leq \frac{1}{12n}$, so for large $ n $ the factor $ e^{\epsilon(n)}$ is close enough to one to ignore. This gives

$$
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(\frac{n}{e})^n
$$

# Asymptotic notation

We already know the tilde:

#### Tilde

$$
f(x)\sim g(x)\ \ \text{if } \lim_{x\to \infty} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=1
$$

#### Big $ O $ notation

$$
f(x)=O(g(x))\ \ \text{ if} \lim_{x\to \infty}|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}|<\alpha \ \ \text{(finite)}
$$

This says that $ f(x)$ grows no faster than $ g(x)$ up to a constant factor. You might see $ f(x)\leq O(g(x))$ (we wouldn't write the inequality the other way around), $ f(x)\ \text{is  } O(g(x))$, or $ f(x)\in O(g(x))$. To establish this with a limit, check whether the ratio has a finite limit.

For example, $ 10^i x^2=O(x^2)$, $ x^i=O(e^x)$, and $ 10=O(1)$, where $ i $ is constant.

#### $\Omega $ notation

$$
f(x)=\Omega (g(x))\ \ \text{ if} \lim_{x\to \infty}|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}|>0
$$

This is the counterpart to big $ O $, giving a lower bound on growth. Accordingly, one can write $ f(x)\geq \Omega(g(x))$.

**Theorem.** $ f(x)=O(g(x)) \text{ if and only if } g(x)=\Omega(f(x))$.

#### $\Theta $ notation

We write $ f(x)=\Theta(g(x))$ if both $ O $ and $\Omega $ hold. In terms of the ratio, a finite limit greater than $ 0 $ gives both.

$$
\begin{aligned} \text{Summary: } & \text{Ignore the constants and think of it this way:} \\ O & \text{ means } \le \\ \Omega & \text{ means } \ge \\ \Theta & \text{ means } = \end{aligned}
$$

The next thing that comes to mind is that there ought to be strict inequalities as well. Those are little $ o $ and little $\omega $.

#### Little $ o $ notation

$$
f(x)=o(g(x))\ \ \text{if } \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=0
$$

#### Little $\omega $ notation

$$
f(x)=\omega(g(x))\ \ \text{if } \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\infty
$$

A mysterious proof:

Put $ f(n)=\sum_{i=1}^n i $. Then suppose we could show $ f(n)=O(n)$.

> **Proof.** Use induction.  
> The induction hypothesis is $ P(n): f(n)=O(n)$.  
> Base case: $ f(1)=O(1)$ obviously holds.  
> Inductive step: assuming $ P(k)$, we get $ f(k+1)=f(k)+k+1=O(n)+O(n)=O(n)$.  
> By induction the claim holds.

The induction hypothesis fixes $ n $, which doesn't actually make sense for an asymptotic claim.

So it's best to be careful about using asymptotic notation inside an induction.
