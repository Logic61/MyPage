As a prerequisite, if there exists a bijection between two sets, they are said to be equipotent, denoted as $A\approx B$. Based on this, we can play around with some well-known sets.

## Open Sets and Closed Sets

For any two open sets or any two closed sets, we can simply scale the length of the interval by multiplying by a coefficient, and then shift them to align them, so naturally, we can show they are equipotent using a linear function as a bijection.

To deduce to infinity, perhaps a slight transformation of form is needed, selecting from elementary functions. $\tan x$ is a natural choice. Selecting $[-\pi /2,\pi /2]$, then $\tan x$ serves as a bijection linking it to infinity. Thus, any closed interval is equipotent to $R$.

Then, for open intervals, intuitively removing the endpoints does not affect equipotence, but how to achieve this technically? Consider the easily demonstrable $(0,1)$ and $[0,1]$. What we need to do is remove the endpoints. In the sequence of infinities, we know that adding a few numbers does not affect much. For an interval, we don't have a specific handle, so we revert to sequences. We take $1/n$, specifically mapping $0$ to $1/2$, $1$ to $1/3$, and then $1/2$ to $1/4$, $1/k$ to $1/(k+2)$, and the remaining numbers remain unchanged under the function. Such a relationship is naturally a bijection, which also shows that the two are equipotent. Of course, we could also select a sequence like $1/2^n$ or something else.

In summary, any non-empty interval is equipotent to $R$.
## N $\not \approx$ R

The set of natural numbers is not equipotent to the set of real numbers.

It seems naturally true, but how to prove it using the definition? To negate it, we can only use proof by contradiction. Assume they are equipotent, then there exists a bijection between them. This means that every real number in $R$ can be numbered. For simplicity, let's only consider $(0,1)$.

We list these real numbers, $r_1=0.a_{11}a_{12}...,r_2=0.a_{21}a_{22}...,...$. Then, we hope to find a number that is different from every $r_i$. Let $r=0.k_1k_2...$ be defined such that its $k_i$-th digit depends on the $r_i$-th digit of $i$: if it is $a_{ii}=1$, then it is $k_i=2$, otherwise it is $k_i=1$. This way, it is different from every listed real number, thus leading to a contradiction.

## A $\not \approx$ P(A)

A set is not equipotent to its power set. Thus, there does not exist the 'largest' set.

This is not so obvious; the proof is the same, using proof by contradiction. Assume they are equipotent, then there exists a bijection. Then, $\exists f, \forall C \in P(A), \exists a \in A, s.t., f(a)=C$, so can we find an $B\in P(A), \not \exists a\in A ,s.t., f(a)=B$?

We cannot list all elements, but we can pick any $a\in A$ and try to make $f(a)\not =B$. This only requires one element to be different, so let $a\in B$ be $a\not \in f(a)$, and vice versa. That is, if $B=\{x|x\not \in f(x)\}$ satisfies this, it leads to a contradiction.

The two proofs above have a certain commonality; we construct a counterexample by negating digit by digit, which is concise and elegant, without needing to know any information. This perhaps has some continuation.

## The Halting Problem

If the elements here are several programs $P_i$, we ask if there exists a decider $H(P,x)$ that can determine whether $P$ halts on input $x$.

Still singing a contrary tune, consider a mysterious program $D(P)$ that outputs the negation of $H(P,P)$. Then $D(D)$ is the negation of $H(D,D)$, which makes it impossible for $H$ to judge correctly.

Why construct such a $D$? The author deduced it as follows: the most natural motivation was to find a $f(x)$ as the negation of $H(f,x)$, but this leads to self-reference in the definition of $f$, so we settled for considering $D(P,x)$ to give the negation of $H(P,x)$, and nesting it gives $D(D,x)$ as the non-negation of $H(D,x)$, but the parameters are not aligned well this way, so in order for $D$ to accept a single parameter, we directly let $D(P)$ correspond to the negation of $H(P,P)$, which makes it very smooth.

## Russell's Paradox

Similar to the self-negation above, there is actually a more direct construction: $R=\{x|x\not \in x\}$. Then $R$ can neither belong to itself nor not belong to itself, otherwise it will lead to a contradiction.
