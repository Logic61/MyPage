Similarly, this can be generalized to the case of $ n $ sets, and we have the following theorem:

$$
\begin{aligned}
|A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \dots \cup A_n| = & \sum_{i=1}^{n} |A_i| \\
& - \sum_{1 \le i_1 < i_2 \le n} |A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2}| \\
& + \sum_{1 \le i_1 < i_2 < i_3 \le n} |A_{i_1} \cap A_{i_2} \cap A_{i_3}| \\
& - \dots + (-1)^{n-1} |A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \dots \cap A_n|
\end{aligned}
$$

More elegantly, we write it as:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k+1} \sum_{\substack{S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\} \\ \text{s.t. } |S|=k}} \left| \bigcap_{i \in S} A_i \right|
$$

This is the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle.

Ex. There is a set of integers containing $ 0 $ to $ 9 $. How many permutations of these integers satisfy at least one of the following three conditions: $ 4 $ is followed by $ 2 $ (abbreviated), $ 60 $, $ 04 $?

It's still a high school problem, but let's carefully think about what we are doing:

First, we need to calculate the number of permutations satisfying the condition $ S $, which is not easy to find. We construct a bijection to transform it into the easily calculable $ P $. $ P $ is the permutation of $ 42 $ and other remaining numbers, and it is easy to verify that this is a bijection, so $|P|=|S|$, then we calculate $|P|$. Since the elements of $ P $ are all different, this is a full permutation, so $|S|=|P|=9!$.

Next, let's see how many permutations satisfy both $ 42,60 $. Similarly, we establish a bijection to get the permutation of $ 42 $, $ 60 $ and other numbers, resulting in a count of $ 8!$. What about $ 04,60 $? That is the permutation of $ 604 $ and other numbers, which is still $ 8!$. Similarly, we can get the intersection of three sets. Finally, using the Inclusion-Exclusion Principle, the number of elements in the union of the three sets is $ 3\times 9!-3\times 8!+7!$.

In short, if a sequence has $ n_i $ repeated characters $ l_i $, then the number of strings that can be formed is:

$$
\frac{(n_1+n_2+...+n_k)!}{n_1!\cdot n_2!\cdot \cdot \cdot n_k!}=\dbinom{n_1+...+n_k}{n_1,...,n_k}
$$

When calculating $ k=2 $, it is actually the binomial coefficient

$$
\dbinom{n}{k}=\dbinom{n}{n,n-k}
$$

#### Binomial Theorem:

$$
\forall n,(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n \dbinom{n}{k}a^kb^{n-k}
$$

Splitting it into the product of $ n $ things can nicely match the meaning of choosing $ n $ from $ k $.

To select $ k $ representatives from $ n $ people, either $ X $ is selected or not selected, corresponding to $\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ and $\binom{n-1}{k}$ respectively, so there are

$$
\dbinom{n-1}{k-1}+\dbinom{n-1}{k}=\dbinom{n}{k}
$$

This is a common proof method for combination problems. Another problem:

$$
\sum_{r=0}^n\dbinom{n}{r}\dbinom{2n}{n-r}=\dbinom{3n}{n}
$$

>[!Proof]
>Let $ S $ be the set of selecting $ n $ balls from $ n $ red balls and $ 2n $ green balls.
>$|S|=\dbinom{3n}{n}$, because this is selecting $ n $ balls from $ 3n $ balls.
>
>Consider the case of selecting $ r $ red balls, then we have selected $ n-r $ green balls, so there are $\dbinom{n}{r}\dbinom{2n}{n-r}$ ways. Summing over all cases gives $|S|$.
>
>Combining the above two algorithms proves the original identity.
