Roughly speaking, the indicator function is like

$$
I_i=\text{Event}i\text{Occurrence}? 1:0
$$

a function that converts the occurrence of an event into a number. This might provide interesting ideas in probability theory, as well as in graph theory and algorithmic problems.

## Calculation of Expectation and Variance

### Binomial Distribution
For the binomial distribution, which we are familiar with, performing $n$ Bernoulli experiments with a success probability of $p$, the binomial distribution gives the probability of the number of successes. The most direct method for the expected number of successes is to calculate

$$
E[X]=\sum_{k\ge 0} \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\cdot k=\sum_{k\ge 0} \frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k)!}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}=np\sum_{k'\ge0}\binom{n-1}{k'}p^{k'}(1-p)^{n-1-k'}=np
$$

We can also decompose it like this:

$$
X=I_1+I_2+...+I_x,\text{where } I_i=\text{i succeed}?1:0
$$

Since each Bernoulli trial is independent, both the expectation and the variance can be added separately. The expectation and variance of a Bernoulli experiment are $p$ and $p(1-p)$, so directly multiplying by $n$ gives the result for the binomial distribution.

### Geometric Distribution
The probability distribution of the number of trials needed to get the first success in a sequence of Bernoulli trials, where the probability of success in each trial is $p$. If we perform $n$ Bernoulli experiments, the probability that the $k$th one is the first success. Actually, it is easy to write directly: $P(X=k)=p(1-p)^{k-1}$. As for the expectation and variance, we can calculate them directly:

$$
E[X]=\sum_{k\ge 1} kp(1-p)^{k-1}=p\sum_{k\ge 1}k(1-p)^{k-1}
$$
It is not easy to calculate directly, noting that this is the derivative of $f(p)=\sum_{k\ge 0}(1-p)^k=\frac{1}{p}-1$ multiplied by $-p$, which gives $E[X]=\frac{1}{p}$.
Using the indicator function, we let it indicate whether the $i$th experiment needs to be done, then

$$
X=\sum_{k\ge 1} I_k
$$

The expectation of each indicator function is the probability $(1-p)^{k-1}$, and if it fails to succeed in the previous $k-1$ times, the $k$th experiment will be needed. Then, using the linearity of expectation, we get

$$
E[X]=\sum_{k\ge 1}(1-p)^{k-1}=\frac{1}{p}
$$
The variance is not that simple. No matter how, it involves $Var[X]=E[X^2]-(E[X])^2$, so we need to calculate $E[X^2]$. Generally, we directly decompose $X^2=X(X-1)+X$, and then similarly construct an auxiliary function to find the second derivative twice. We can also use the characteristic function, but it is a bit more mysterious. Notice that

$$
X^2=\sum_{k=1}^X(2k-1)=\sum_{k\ge 1} (2k-1)I_k
$$

To calculate a more complex geometric series that requires differentiation (in short, it can also be calculated

$$
E[X^2]=\sum_{k\ge 1} (2k-1)(1-p)^{k-1}=\frac{2-p}{p^2}
$$

### Negative Binomial Distribution

The distribution of the number of trials required to achieve a success in a Bernoulli experiment $r$ times. We can decompose this into $r$ geometric distributions, because due to the memoryless property of the geometric distribution, we can directly multiply the expectation and variance by $r$.

## Number of edges in a random graph

Consider an undirected simple graph with n vertices. For each pair of distinct vertices, an edge is independently decided to exist with probability p. Let the indicator function indicate whether each edge exists, and directly obtain the expectation of $\binom{n}{2}p$.
