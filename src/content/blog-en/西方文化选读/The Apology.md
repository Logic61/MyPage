# "Crime"

We are not unfamiliar with Socrates; he was like a gadfly, bothering passersby in the streets, questioning them about seemingly strange things. When he was accused, others listed three charges. These three charges were:

1. **Impiety.** Socrates was accused of failing to worship in the ways the city recognized. Religion and political life were bound up with each other, so this counted as disloyalty to his own city.
    
2. **Introducing new divinities.** This referred to the divine sign, or *daimonion*, that Socrates said he heard. The prosecution treated it as a heresy that might undermine the city's religion.
    
3. **Corrupting the youth:** This was the most socially damaging charge. It accused him of causing social disorder by teaching young people to question tradition and challenge authority.

Of course, this image is not the historical Socrates, but an exaggeration deliberately created by Aristophanes to satirize the "philosopher class", $ caricature $but it greatly shaped public perception.

Socrates questioned the gods of his day and introduced what was alleged to be a new divinity, his *daimonion*. He understood it as a divine voice. A few features of how it worked are worth noting:

1. **A voice that holds him back.** It never tells him what to do. It stops him before he does something wrong, such as joining a political debate or saying something he ought to keep to himself.
    
2. **Irrational Intuition:** Socrates believed this voice was beyond deduction. He did not need to prove why he should stop through syllogism; as long as the voice sounded, he would obey unconditionally.
    
3. **Its private character.** Socrates alone could hear the voice. This source of truth, addressed to one person alone, challenged Athens’ claim to interpret divine signs and made conservative Athenians see him as a threat.

This is indeed a miraculous god. Socrates' description of the daimonion has led to various interpretations in later generations: seen as a metaphor for inner moral intuition, or understood as a personal replacement for the traditional oracle system—in any case, this private divine experience constituted a challenge within the city-state's religious framework.

Today we might praise Socrates for his thoughtfulness, his love of learning, and his habit of questioning everything. In Athens, impiety was an offense almost like violating the constitution. Religion, politics, and law formed a single whole in the Greek city. There was no separation of religion and civic government.

## I. The "Logical Foundation" of the Polis: The Divine Covenant

Ancient Athens was not a society built on purely secular contracts, but an organization built on a **ritual community**.

- **Guardian Deity System:** Athens is named Athens because it is the city-state of the goddess Athena. If someone openly disbelieved in the gods (Atheism), in the eyes of people at the time, this was not "freedom of personal belief," but rather **a violation of the covenant between the city-state and the gods**.
    
- **Collective Responsibility:** People at the time believed that if the city-state tolerated the existence of someone who was disrespectful to the gods, the gods would send plagues, wars, or famine as punishment. Therefore, reporting someone who was disrespectful to the gods was considered an act of "patriotism" at the time.
    

---

## II. The "Crime of Impious Behavior" (Asebeia) in Law

Although there was no constitution in the modern sense, Athens passed the **Decree of Diopithes** around 432 BCE.

- **Legal Clauses:** This decree stipulated that anyone who did not believe in the gods or who attempted to explain heavenly matters scientifically would be subject to prosecution.
    
- **Logic of Conviction:** 

1. **Sacrilege:** Destroying statues of gods or revealing sacred rituals. 

2. **Impiety:** This was the charge brought against Socrates. It was more like a form of "political incorrectness," meaning that one's words threatened the cohesion of the city-state.
    

---

## III. Why Was Socrates' "Divine Inspiration" Illegal?

There is an extremely hard conflict point here: **Official Authority vs. Personal Intuition**.

- **Religious Monopoly:** The oracles, rituals, and divinations of Athens were controlled by the city-state.
    
- **Logical Conflict:** When Socrates said he was following an inner "divine inspiration" ($ Daimonion $), this was legally interpreted as **"establishing a private god."**
    
- **Political Risk:** If everyone claimed to have heard commands from the gods and did not follow the unified direction of the city-state, the decision-making mechanism of democracy would become paralyzed. For Athens, which had just experienced a coup, this "freedom of thought" was simply a fatal virus.

## Regarding disbelief in the gods

In his defense, Socrates did the opposite, saying that all his actions were based on divine oracles, so how could he say he didn't believe in the gods?

Socrates' friend **Chaerephon** once went to the Temple of Apollo.

- **Question (Input):** In the temple, Chaerephon boldly asked the priestess Pythia: "**Is there anyone wiser than Socrates?**"
    
- **Oracle (Output):** The priestess conveyed Apollo's will: "**No one is wiser than Socrates.**"

When Socrates heard this news, his first reaction was not "I'm amazing," but rather falling into a huge **logical confusion**. According to $ The \ Apology $'s account, his thought process was as follows:

1. **Axiom:** The gods cannot lie (the nature of divinity requires truth).
    
2. **Self-Observation:** I am very clear that I have no wisdom except "knowing nothing."
    
3. **Contradiction:** How can a person who "knows nothing" be the "wisest" person?
    

To solve this paradox, Socrates embarked on a **empirical investigation** that lasted for decades. He went out to interview those who considered themselves extraordinary: 

- **Politicians:** Discussing justice, but full of logical loopholes.
    
- **Poets:** Discussing beauty, but relying entirely on inspiration, unable to explain the underlying logic.
    
- **Craftsmen:** Although they had skills, they thought they knew everything in every field.

After extensive comparative sampling, Socrates finally grasped the underlying logic of the oracle:

> **Conclusion:** Those people thought they knew, but actually didn't; and although I don't know, I **know that I don't know**. **Definition of Wisdom:** Recognizing the limitations of human wisdom; this state of "knowing nothing" is the highest wisdom recognized by the gods.

> He did not mean, I think, that Socrates is wise: he only took me as an example, and made use of my name, as though  he would say to men: “He among you is wisest, who, like Socrates, is convinced that for wisdom he is verily worthless.”

We can examine this oracle story:

- **Political Defense (Advantage):** In court, Socrates used this oracle to show that his wandering to question people was not because of a strange personality, but to **fulfill the mission of the gods (verify the oracle)**. This gave his actions religious legitimacy. It also showed that he was not disbelieving in the gods; he was acting strictly according to the oracle.
    
- **Logical Risk (Disadvantage):** This defense sounded extremely grating to the jury. To say you are the "wisest person chosen by the gods" was simply **open mockery (Aggro)** in the Athenian democracy, which valued equality!

## After this, there was a vote, and the difference between those who found him innocent and those who found him guilty was 60 votes. If just 30 people changed their minds, he could have been acquitted.

Let's briefly introduce the trial process in Athens.

1. 🏛️ Stage One: Accusation and Preliminary Hearing (The Preliminary)
    
2. In Athens, there was no public prosecutor; all criminal lawsuits were initiated by individual citizens (i.e., _Graphe_).
    
3. **Summons:** The accuser (like Meletus) had to orally summon the defendant (Socrates) in front of witnesses to appear before the judges on a specified date.
    

---

## **Preliminary Hearing (Anakrisis):** Presided over by an archon. Both sides submitted written testimonies, oaths, and legal statutes. These materials were sealed in a pottery jar called _Echinos_ to prevent last-minute additions during the trial.

**Public Notice:** The accusation was written on a wooden board and posted in the central public square, so that all of Athens could see it.

- ⚖️ Stage Two: The Trial and Debate (The Trial)
    
- The Athenian court had no professional judges, only **jurors** (_Dikastai_).
    
- **Jury Size:** For a major case like Socrates', the jury was as high as **501 people** (an odd number to avoid a tie). They were randomly selected from citizens over 30 years old.
    

---

## **Time-Limited Speaking:** Both sides took turns speaking. The time was controlled by a **water clock (Klepsydra)**. When the water ran out, you had to be silent!

**Debate Logic:** There were no lawyers to speak on behalf of the parties. Socrates had to appear himself. This is also why he could directly cross-examine Meletus on the spot, causing the opponent's logic to collapse.

### 🗳️ Stage Three: The Two-Stage Voting (The Two-Stage Voting)

This is the most hardcore part, the one with the most game theory. The judgment in Athens was divided into two rounds:

- Round One: Guilty or Not Guilty?
    
- The jurors held two bronze voting plates (solid center representing acquittal, hollow center representing guilt) and cast them into the corresponding jars.
    

### **Socrates' Result:** 281 votes guilty, 220 votes not guilty.

**Regarding the Votes:** If about 31 more people had switched sides, Socrates could have been released on the spot. This shows that his defense actually convinced quite a few people, but it truly angered the other half.

1. Round Two: Sentencing Game (The Sentencing)
    
2. Athenian law stipulated that the accuser proposed a penalty, and the defendant proposed an alternative penalty, and the jury had to choose one.
    

**Accuser (Meletus):** Death penalty.

# Reflection

Socrates’ death was more complicated than that. Athens was still recovering from the rule of the Thirty, and some of Socrates’ own pupils had been among the tyrants. At such a politically sensitive moment, his doubts about democracy looked like a political threat.

## Background

To understand Socrates' death, one cannot only look at the trial.

Athens at that time was in an extremely fragile, sensitive, and emotionally wounded period. We can break down the underlying logic of this historical background using three keywords: **Defeat, Civil War, and Reconstruction**.

---

### I. The Disastrous Defeat in the Peloponnesian War (The Shadow of Defeat)

Five years before Socrates was executed (404 BCE), Athens had just ended the **Peloponnesian War** of 27 years with Sparta.

- **The Fall of Civilization:** Athens, the once glorious "Greek School," was utterly defeated; its long walls were torn down, and its navy was disbanded.
    
- **The loss of confidence.** Athenians had thought themselves chosen by the gods and found themselves reduced to a second-rate city. The shock sent them looking for someone to blame. Had they failed to honor their gods? Was there something wrong with their education?
    

---

### II. The Terror of the Thirty Tyrants and the Trauma of Civil War

After the defeat, Sparta supported 30 Athenian nobles sympathetic to Sparta, who came to power, known as the **

- **Bloody Purge:** These people killed 1500 democratic citizens in less than a year, confiscated property, and exiled dissidents.
    
- **Socrates' Awkward Position:** Critias, the leader of the tyrants, was once Socrates' student. Although Socrates refused to carry out the illegal orders of the tyrants (to arrest an innocent person), the public logic was simple: **“What kind of good person can be the teacher of such a tyrant?”**
    

---

### III. The Fragile Reconstruction of Democracy (Collective Anxiety)

In 403 BCE, the democrats recovered power through bloody struggle and declared an **Amnesty**.

- **The legal dilemma.** Because of the amnesty, the democrats could not openly put Socrates to death as a settling of political scores.
    
- **A legal workaround.** Political charges were unavailable, so the prosecution chose impiety and corruption of the young.
    
- **Social Psychology:** The restored democracy was very self-deprecating and xenophobic. They desperately needed an **“ideological consistency”** to unite the populace. Socrates' constant questioning of tradition and deconstruction of authority was seen as a secondary blow to the stability of the city-state.

---

We cannot deny the very positive role of Socrates' teaching in inspiring the intellect and promoting thought, but this did indeed have quite a shock on the fragile, newly reconstructed democracy. And it cannot be entirely said that the Athenians executing Socrates was completely foolish.

Furthermore, Socrates did not believe that the democracy of Athens at the time was reasonable.

## Contradiction in Political Legitimacy: Professionalism vs. Lottery Democracy

- **The logic of radical democracy.** The restored democracy relied on selection by lot. Since every citizen was considered qualified to govern, the lottery stood for democratic equality.
    
- **Socrates' Logic (Expert Rule):** He had a famous analogy: If you are sick, would you see a doctor or draw lots to find someone to treat you on the street? Obviously, you would see an expert. So, how can such a complex task as governing a state be entrusted to an amateur drawn by lot?
    
- **The point of conflict.** Socrates’ insistence on expertise went to the heart of democratic legitimacy. To people who had just wrested power back from the Thirty, he could sound like someone who might defend a tyrant.

Numerous contradictions existed, and the Athenians' decision to eliminate Socrates at that time likely had their own considerations. However, if a regime relies on suppressing the voices of questioners to maintain stability, it will probably only weaken in the long run, even if it is effective in the short term.
Of course, if you read $ The \ Apology $, and immerse yourself in the feeling, you might also be angered by Socrates, which is probably also an important part of the death sentence. Socrates was quite irritating.

## Comparing to Today

### The Tyranny of the Majority in the Age of Social Media

In Athens, 501 jurors decided life and death with stones; today, hundreds of millions of netizens execute "moral judgments" through likes, shares, and cyberbullying.

- **Logical Isomorphism:** The Athenian court lacked professional judges and relied entirely on emotion; the modern internet public sphere also lacks the buffer of fact-checking, often devolving into **“whoever is louder is right”**.
    
- **Reflection:** When we type judgmental comments on the screen, we are actually one of those 501 jurors. Socrates' death reminds us that **procedural justice** (legal process) often becomes the first sacrifice when hijacked by **mass passion**.
    

---

### Filter Bubbles in the Post-Truth Age

Aristophanes' Clouds successfully smeared Socrates because the masses were only willing to watch comical "stereotypes," and not to read dry philosophical debates.

- **Logical Isomorphism:** Today's **recommendation algorithms** are more efficient than Aristophanes! They precisely feed you the information you want to see, trapping you in an echo chamber.
    
- **Reflection.** If Socrates lived today, platforms might ban him for inappropriate speech or label him an extremist. Are we, like the Athenians, so afraid of giving offense that we refuse to hear truths that grate on us?
    

---

### Expertism vs. Polarized Democracy

Socrates questioned government by lottery. He thought specialized work ought to be done by people with the right expertise. The Athenians saw this as a betrayal of equality.

- **Logical Isomorphism:** In modern society, when dealing with complex issues like climate change and public health, **expert advice** (rational) often clashes violently with **populist demands** (emotional).
    
- **Reflection.** How can we keep democratic equality while making decisions informed by evidence? The death of Socrates suggests how close a system comes to collapse once a society loses all regard for expertise and puts absolute mediocrity and conformity in its place.

## Appendix: Cancel Culture

Socrates' death may be the earliest 'cancellation' in human history.

In contemporary times, there is what is called Cancel Culture.

---

###  1. Logical Definition: From 'Boycott' to 'Erasure'

Cancel Culture is not just about not buying a brand's products; it tends more towards **stripping away an individual's 'social presence'**.

- **Boycott:** Directed at economic behavior. I won't buy your product until you improve.
    
- **Cancel:** Directed at personality and platform. I won't allow you in any public view; I want your employer to fire you, the platform to ban you, and your friends to distance themselves from you.
    
- **Core Logic:** Achieving a kind of **'social death'** by severing the individual's social ties.
    

---

### 2. The 'Three-in-One' Game Model of Cancel Culture

Why is Cancel Culture so potent today? Because it satisfies needs on three dimensions:

#### A. Psychological Dimension: Cheap Moral Self-Licensing

Participants in cancellations often gain a sense of nobility that 'I am upholding justice.' This feeling is **low-cost, even zero-cost**—you don't need to protest on the streets; you just need to click a retweet or post a #Cancel tag. This psychological compensation mechanism allows people to 'cyber-vent' their frustrations in real life.

#### B. Algorithmic Dimension: Polarization Loop

Algorithms naturally favor 'anger.' An angry accusation garners several times higher click-through rates and weight than a rational defense.

- **Signal Amplification:** As long as 5% of people are extremely angry, the algorithm will let those 5% of voices cover the remaining 95%.
    
- **Cognitive Loop:** When you like a post canceling someone, the algorithm will push more similar content, making you feel that 'the whole world hates him,' thereby reinforcing your judgment.
    

#### C. Economic Dimension: Traffic and Risk Aversion

- **Platforms:** Banning controversial figures is the easiest public relations strategy (Risk Aversion).
    
- **Capital:** Brands, in order to protect their stock prices, will quickly sever ties with the 'canceled,' and this 'cutting' often happens before the truth has fully emerged.
    

---

### 3. Historical Isomorphism: The Modern 'Ostracism' 

The trial in Athens was actually the ancestor of Cancel Culture. Athens had a **Ostracism**: 

1. **No Specific Crime:** As long as people felt you threatened the equality of the city-state (or simply disliked you), they could write your name on a shard of pottery.
    
2. **Majority Rule:** If enough votes were cast, you had to leave the city-state for 10 years.
    
3. **Comparison:** Today's 'Cancel Culture' is the digital version of ostracism, except that the 'shard of pottery' is your likes and comments, and the 'place of exile' is the wilderness of the internet.
    

---

### 4. Critical Assessment

Evaluating it from the perspective of **probability and risk**:

- **Positive Effect:** It has indeed punished some big shots who have long evaded legal sanctions by abusing power, breaking the arrogance of power.
    
- **Negative Risk:**
    
    - **Missing Evidence Chain:** 'Accusation equals conviction.' In cancel culture, there is no presumption of innocence; there is only 'who posts first claims the high ground.'
        
    - **Zero Tolerance for Error:** It denies human evolution. An inappropriate statement from ten years ago will be dug up to negate all of a person's current achievements. This is biologically unreasonable, because cells regenerate every seven years, let alone thoughts?
        
    - **Mediocrity of Intellectual Life:** In order not to be 'canceled,' elites and creators begin to self-censor, only saying the safest words. The result is the **collapse of cultural diversity** in the entire society.
