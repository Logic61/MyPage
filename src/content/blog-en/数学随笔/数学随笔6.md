## LU Decomposition of a Matrix

The most practical method is based on a variation of **Gaussian elimination**. During the elimination process, we construct $ L $ and $ U $ simultaneously.

#### Step 1: Construction by Elimination $ U $ Transform the matrix $ A $ into an upper triangular matrix using elementary row operations (only using the operation of "adding a multiple of one row to another row").

- This finally obtained upper triangular matrix is **$ U $**.
    
- **Note:** In standard LU decomposition, row swapping is not allowed. If row swapping is necessary, a permutation matrix $ P $ must be introduced, resulting in $ PA=LU $.
    
- If we change all the diagonal elements of $ U $ to $ 1 $, and write $ U $ as $ DU $, we get the $ LDU $ decomposition. Here, $ D $ is a diagonal matrix, and $ U $ is an upper triangular matrix with 1s on the diagonal. For a symmetric matrix, it becomes $ LDL^T $.
#### Step 2: Construct $ L $ using the elimination coefficients.

When performing elimination, if you used the operation: $ R_i \leftarrow R_i - k \cdot R_j $ (subtracting $ k $ times the $ j $ row from the $ i $ row), then this coefficient $ k $ should be placed at the position of the $ i $ row and the $ j $ column in the matrix $ L $.

- The diagonal elements of $ L $ are defaulted to $ 1 $.
    
- All other positions (the upper triangular part) of $ L $ are $ 0 $.
    

---
	
#### Example (3x3 Matrix)

Suppose we want to decompose the matrix:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{pmatrix}
$$

##### 1. Eliminate the first column:

- $ R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - \mathbf{2}R_1 $, obtaining the new element in the second row $[0, 1, 1]$. The coefficient is $ L_{21} = 2 $.
    
- $ R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \mathbf{4}R_1 $, obtaining the new element in the third row $[0, 3, 5]$. The coefficient is $ L_{31} = 4 $.
    

##### 2. Eliminate the second column:

- The matrix becomes $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ at this point.
    
- $ R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \mathbf{3}R_2 $, obtaining the new element in the third row $[0, 0, 2]$. The coefficient is $ L_{32} = 3 $.
    

##### 3. Obtain the result:

- Upper triangular matrix $ U $ (result after elimination):

$$
U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

- Lower triangular matrix $ L $ (filling in coefficients and diagonal 1s):

$$
L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \mathbf{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{4} & \mathbf{3} & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

## QR Decomposition of a Matrix

### 1. Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Process
This is the process of transforming a set of basis vectors $\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\}$ into an orthogonal basis $\{\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3\}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_1 &= \beta_1 \\
\alpha_2 &= \frac{(\alpha_2, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|^2}\beta_1 + \beta_2 \\
\alpha_3 &= \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|^2}\beta_1 + \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_2)}{\|\beta_2\|^2}\beta_2 + \beta_3
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!info] Complexity Explanation
> The time complexity of this algorithm is usually **$ O(n^3)$ multiplications**.

---

### 2. QR Decomposition Representation
The matrix $ A $ can be decomposed into the product of an **orthogonal matrix (Q)** and an **upper triangular matrix (R)**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &= [\alpha_1 \quad \alpha_2 \quad \alpha_3] \\
&= \underbrace{\left[ \frac{\beta_1}{\|\beta_1\|} \quad \frac{\beta_2}{\|\beta_2\|} \quad \frac{\beta_3}{\|\beta_3\|} \right]}_{\text{Orthogonal Matrix } Q} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
\|\beta_1\| & \frac{(\alpha_2, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|} & \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|} \\
0 & \|\beta_2\| & \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_2)}{\|\beta_2\|} \\
0 & 0 & \|\beta_3\|
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{Upper Triangular Matrix } R}
\end{aligned}
$$

---
### Specific Example of QR Decomposition (Gram-Schmidt)

#### 1. The matrix to be decomposed is $ A $. Let the column vectors of the matrix $ A $ be $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3 $:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

---

#### 2. Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Steps (Finding $\beta_i $)

1. **Calculate $\beta_1 $**:

$$
\beta_1 = \alpha_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \|\beta_1\| = \sqrt{2}
$$

2. **Calculate $\beta_2 $**:

$$
\beta_2 = \alpha_2 - \frac{(\alpha_2, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|^2}\beta_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{2}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \|\beta_2\| = \sqrt{3}
$$

3. **Calculate $\beta_3 $**:

$$
\beta_3 = \alpha_3 - \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|^2}\beta_1 - \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_2)}{\|\beta_2\|^2}\beta_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{0}{2}\beta_1 - \frac{-1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2/3 \\ 2/3 \\ 4/3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \|\beta_3\| = \sqrt{\frac{24}{9}} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3}
$$

---

#### 3. Decomposition Result: $ A = QR $#### Orthogonal Matrix $ Q $ (Orthonormalized Basis)

$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} 
1/\sqrt{2} & 1/\sqrt{3} & -1/\sqrt{6} \\ 
1/\sqrt{2} & -1/\sqrt{3} & 1/\sqrt{6} \\ 
0 & 1/\sqrt{3} & 2/\sqrt{6} 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

#### Upper triangular matrix $ R$

$$
$ R = \begin{bmatrix} 
\|\beta_1\| & \frac{(\alpha_2, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|} & \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|} \\ 
0 & \|\beta_2\| & \frac{(\alpha_3, \beta_2)}{\|\beta_2\|} \\ 
0 & 0 & \|\beta_3\| 
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 
\sqrt{2} & \sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 
0 & \sqrt{3} & -1/\sqrt{3} \\ 
0 & 0 & 2\sqrt{6}/3 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

> [!check] Geometric Interpretation Verification
> * $ R_{11} = \sqrt{2}$ is the length of the first vector $\alpha_1 $.
> * $ R_{12} = \sqrt{2}$ is the projection component of $\alpha_2 $ onto the direction of $\alpha_1 $.
> * $ R_{33} = 2\sqrt{6}/3 $ is the perpendicular distance from $\alpha_3 $ to the plane $\text{span}\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2\}$.
## Cholesky Decomposition of a Matrix $ LL^T $

### 1. Definition and Prerequisites
If the matrix $ A $ is a **Symmetric Positive Definite matrix**, then it can be uniquely decomposed into the product of a lower triangular matrix $ L $ and its transpose:

$$
A = LL^T
$$

Where $ L $ is a **lower triangular matrix** whose diagonal elements are all positive:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots \\
a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots
\end{bmatrix} = 
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
l_{11} & 0 & 0 \\
l_{21} & l_{22} & 0 \\
l_{31} & l_{32} & l_{33}
\end{bmatrix}}_{L}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
l_{11} & l_{21} & l_{31} \\
0 & l_{22} & l_{32} \\
0 & 0 & l_{33}
\end{bmatrix}}_{L^T}
$$


For a real symmetric matrix $A$ with positive leading principal minors $\Delta_1,\ldots,\Delta_n$,
symmetric elimination gives the successive pivots
$a_{11}=\Delta_1$, $b_{22}=\Delta_2/\Delta_1$, $c_{33}=\Delta_3/\Delta_2$, and so on.
Every pivot is positive. The right-hand elimination factor $C$ is upper triangular with ones on the diagonal.


Write $C^TAC=D^2=D^TD$, with $D$ diagonal and positive, and put $U=DC^{-1}$.
The diagonal entries of the upper triangular $U$ are
$\sqrt{\Delta_1},\sqrt{\Delta_2/\Delta_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\Delta_n/\Delta_{n-1}}$.
The Cholesky factorization is
$$
A=(C^T)^{-1}D^TDC^{-1}=(DC^{-1})^T(DC^{-1})=U^TU.
$$


---

### 2. Calculation Formula (Solving column by column)
By equating corresponding elements of the matrix product, we can derive the calculation method for the elements of the $ L $ matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
l_{jj} &= \sqrt{a_{jj} - \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} l_{jk}^2} \\
l_{ij} &= \frac{1}{l_{jj}} \left( a_{ij} - \sum_{k=1}^{j-1} l_{ik}l_{jk} \right), \quad i > j
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!info] Complexity Explanation
> The time complexity of Cholesky decomposition is approximately **$\frac{1}{3}n^3 $ multiplications**, which is twice as efficient as standard LU decomposition, and halves the storage space (only requiring storage for $ L $).
### Cholesky Decomposition ($ LL^T $) Specific Calculation Example

#### 1. Matrix to be Decomposed $ A $ Let $ A $ be a symmetric positive definite matrix:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 12 & -16 \\ 12 & 37 & -43 \\ -16 & -43 & 98 \end{bmatrix}
$$

---

#### 2. Calculation Steps (Solving the lower triangular matrix column by column $ L $)
Let $ L = \begin{bmatrix} l_{11} & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & l_{22} & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & l_{33} \end{bmatrix}$, and derive step-by-step based on $ A = LL^T $:

##### Calculation for the first column:
* $ l_{11} = \sqrt{a_{11}} = \sqrt{4} = \mathbf{2}$*$ l_{21} = a_{21} / l_{11} = 12 / 2 = \mathbf{6}$*$ l_{31} = a_{31} / l_{11} = -16 / 2 = \mathbf{-8}$

##### Calculation for the second column:
* $ l_{22} = \sqrt{a_{22} - l_{21}^2} = \sqrt{37 - 6^2} = \sqrt{1} = \mathbf{1}$*$ l_{32} = \frac{1}{l_{22}}(a_{32} - l_{31}l_{21}) = \frac{1}{1}(-43 - (-8 \times 6)) = -43 + 48 = \mathbf{5}$

##### Calculation for the third column:
* $ l_{33} = \sqrt{a_{33} - (l_{31}^2 + l_{32}^2)} = \sqrt{98 - ((-8)^2 + 5^2)} = \sqrt{98 - (64 + 25)} = \sqrt{9} = \mathbf{3}$

---

#### 3. Decomposition Result

$$
L = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 6 & 1 & 0 \\ -8 & 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad 
L^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 & -8 \\ 0 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

> [!check] Verification of Calculation
>

$$
> LL^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 6 & 1 & 0 \\ -8 & 5 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 & -8 \\ 0 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 12 & -16 \\ 12 & 37 & -43 \\ -16 & -43 & 98 \end{bmatrix} = A
>
$$

> [!tip] Core Observation
> * **Positive Definiteness Test**: During the decomposition process, if the value under the square root is negative, it means the original matrix $ A $ is not positive definite.

## Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of a Matrix

### 1. Core Definition
For any $ m \times n $ real matrix $ A $, there exists the following decomposition:

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T
$$

The dimensions and properties of each part are as follows:
* **$ U $**: An **orthogonal matrix** of order $ m \times m $, whose column vectors are called the left singular vectors.
* **$\Sigma $**: A **diagonal matrix** of order $ m \times n $, whose diagonal elements $\sigma_i \ge 0 $ are called **singular values** (usually arranged in descending order).
* **$ V^T $**: The transpose of an **orthogonal matrix** of order $ n \times n $, whose column vectors are called the right singular vectors.

See the essay on SVD [[Mathematical Essay 11]]

---

### 2. Construction Relationship
SVD has a profound connection with the Eigenvalue Decomposition (EVD) of a matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^T A &= (V \Sigma^T U^T)(U \Sigma V^T) = V (\Sigma^T \Sigma) V^T \\
A A^T &= (U \Sigma V^T)(V \Sigma^T U^T) = U (\Sigma \Sigma^T) U^T
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!info] Conclusion
> * **The column vectors of $ V $**: are the eigenvectors of $ A^T A $.
> * **The column vectors of $ U $**: are the eigenvectors of $ A A^T $.
> * **The singular values $\sigma_i $**: are the square roots of the non-zero eigenvalues $\lambda_i $ of $ A^T A $ (or $ AA^T $), i.e., $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

---

### 3. Geometric Meaning: Decomposition of Linear Transformations
SVD decomposes a complex linear transformation into three simple geometric steps:

1. **Rotation/Reflection ($ V^T $)**: Rotating the input vector to a new coordinate system.
2. **Stretching ($\Sigma $)**: Scaling along the new coordinate axes.
3. **Another Rotation/Reflection ($ U $)**: Mapping the scaled vector to the coordinate system of the target space.

---

### 4. Application Scenarios
> [!tip] Why is SVD important?
> * **PCA (Principal Component Analysis)**: Obtaining the rank-k best approximation by retaining the first k singular values.
> * **Pseudo-inverse Calculation**: $ A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T $.

## Polar Decomposition of a Matrix

### 1. Core Theorem
Every real square matrix $ A $ can be decomposed into the product of a **semidefinite real symmetric matrix** and an **orthogonal matrix**.

$$
A = P S Q^T = (P S P^T) (P Q^T)
$$

Where:
* $\underbrace{(P S P^T)}_{\text{Symmetric Positive Semi-definite}}$: Represents a **symmetric transformation** (stretching or compressing the space).
* $\underbrace{(P Q^T)}_{\text{Orthogonal Matrix}}$: Represents an **orthogonal transformation** (rotating or mirroring the space).

---

### 2. Geometric Intuition of the Transformation Process
The process of polar decomposition can be seen as the basis undergoing a series of continuous linear transformations:

$$
(\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_n) \xrightarrow{\text{Orthogonal Transformation}} (\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n) \xrightarrow{\text{Symmetric Transformation}} (\sigma_1 \beta_1, \dots, \sigma_n \beta_n)
$$

> [!tip] Physical Analogy
> In continuum mechanics, polar decomposition is used to decompose an object's **deformation** into pure **Rotation** and pure **Stretch**. This is analogous to the polar coordinate representation of a complex number $ z = r e^{i\theta}$, where $ r $ corresponds to the scaling of the symmetric matrix, and $ e^{i\theta}$ corresponds to the rotation of the orthogonal matrix.

---

### 3. Relationship with SVD
Polar decomposition is actually a re-combination method of Singular Value Decomposition (SVD):
* Let $ A = U \Sigma V^T $* Then $ A = (U \Sigma U^T) (U V^T)$* Here $ U \Sigma U^T $ is a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, and $ U V^T $ is an orthogonal matrix.
