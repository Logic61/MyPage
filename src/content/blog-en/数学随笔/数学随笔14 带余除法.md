### Feature

Let $F$ be a field of characteristic $p>0$.

1. For every $a\in F$,
   $$pa=a+a+\cdots+a=(p1_F)a=0.$$
2. If $0\ne a\in F$, $m\in\mathbb Z$, and $ma=0$, then $p\mid m$.
   Otherwise write $m=kp+r$ with $0<r<p$. Then $ra=0$, so $(r1_F)a=0$.
   Since $a\ne0$, this gives $r1_F=0$, contradicting the characteristic of $F$.




### Bezout's Theorem

### Roots

Let $f(x)\in\mathbb Q[x]$ be irreducible of degree $n$ and let $\alpha$ be a complex root.

1. Every element of $\mathbb Q[\alpha]$ can be written uniquely as
   $c_0+c_1\alpha+\cdots+c_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1}$ with $c_i\in\mathbb Q$.
   To see existence, divide any representing polynomial $g$ by $f$ and evaluate at $\alpha$.
   For uniqueness, the difference between two representations would give a polynomial
   of degree less than $n$ with root $\alpha$. A nonzero such polynomial is coprime to $f$,
   so Bézout's identity would give $1=0$ on evaluation at $\alpha$.
   Thus $\mathbb Q[\alpha]$ is an $n$-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb Q$.
2. $\mathbb Q[\alpha]$ is a field. For a nonzero $h(\alpha)$ with $\deg h<n$,
   irreducibility gives $\gcd(h,f)=1$. Write $u(x)h(x)+v(x)f(x)=1$.
   Evaluating at $x=\alpha$ shows that $u(\alpha)$ is the inverse of $h(\alpha)$.
