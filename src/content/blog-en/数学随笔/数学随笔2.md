It's just an organization, without much thought.



##### The determinant of a block matrix
If we want to find the determinant of a block matrix, we usually perform row operations like with a normal determinant to form a triangular matrix, and we can operate like this:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 - BD^{-1}R_2} \begin{pmatrix} A-BD^{-1}C & O \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}
$$

At this point, we can get

$$
\det(M) = \det(D) \cdot \det(A - BD^{-1}C)
$$

#####  Woodbury Identity

$$
(A - BD^{-1}C)^{-1} = A^{-1} + A^{-1}B(D - CA^{-1}B)^{-1}CA^{-1}
$$

The proof is given by matrix transformation: 
Let $ M = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}$, assuming $ A $ is invertible, then $ D $ is invertible. 
First, we can perform the matrix transformation:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 - BD^{-1}R_2} \begin{pmatrix} A-BD^{-1}C & O \\ C & D \end{pmatrix}
$$

This gives the $(A - BD^{-1}C)$ on the left side. How to find its inverse? 
We only need to find the inverse of $ M $, and its top-left corner is the inverse of $ A-BD^{-1}C $. So it's just simple elimination, constructing the augmented matrix:

$$
\left( \begin{array}{cc|cc} A & B & I & 0 \\ C & D & 0 & I \end{array} \right)
$$

Multiply the first row by $ A^{-1}$:

$$
\left( \begin{array}{cc|cc} I & A^{-1}B & A^{-1} & 0 \\ C & D & 0 & I \end{array} \right)
$$

Eliminate $C$ in the bottom left corner.

$$
$\left( \begin{array}{cc|cc} I & A^{-1}B & A^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & D - CA^{-1}B & -CA^{-1} & I \end{array} \right)
$$

Let $\Delta_A=D - CA^{-1}B $, and then multiply by its inverse:

$$
\left( \begin{array}{cc|cc} I & A^{-1}B & A^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & I & -\Delta_A^{-1}CA^{-1} & \Delta_A^{-1} \end{array} \right)
$$

Finally, eliminate the top right corner:

$$
\left( \begin{array}{cc|cc} I & 0 & A^{-1} + A^{-1}B\Delta_A^{-1}CA^{-1} & -A^{-1}B\Delta_A^{-1} \\ 0 & I & -\Delta_A^{-1}CA^{-1} & \Delta_A^{-1} \end{array} \right)
$$

Substitute $\Delta_A=D - CA^{-1}B $, and we get:

$$
(A - BD^{-1}C)^{-1} = A^{-1} + A^{-1}B(D - CA^{-1}B)^{-1}CA^{-1}
$$

This completes the proof. If we substitute some simple values, we get a beautiful identity:

$$
(I_n - BA)^{-1} = I_n + B(I_m - AB)^{-1}A
$$

Change the matrix to the vector $\alpha, \beta^T $:

$$
(I_n - \alpha \beta^T)^{-1} =I_n+\frac{\alpha \beta^T}{1-\beta^T \alpha} (\beta^T \alpha \neq 1)
$$

##### Schur complement
The above

$$
D - CA^{-1}B
$$

is called the Schur complement. This is usually a good way to calculate the determinant of a block matrix, which can be seen from its origin.
###### Schur complement and the discriminant
Quadratic function $ ax^2 + bx + c $. We can write it as a quadratic form of a symmetric matrix:

$$
f(x, 1) = \begin{pmatrix} x & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a & b/2 \\ b/2 & c \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

For this $ 2 \times 2 $ matrix, if we calculate the Schur complement of $ a $:

$$
S = c - (b/2)a^{-1}(b/2) = c - \frac{b^2}{4a} = \frac{4ac - b^2}{4a}
$$

You will find that the numerator of the Schur complement is exactly the negative of the discriminant $\Delta $.
This can actually be generalized to the positive definiteness of block matrices.
For a symmetric matrix $ M = \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ B^T & C \end{pmatrix}$, $ M \succ 0 $ (positive definite) if and only if:
1. $ A \succ 0 $ 2.$ S = C - B^T A^{-1} B \succ 0 $
Because this is actually equivalent to us performing a congruence transformation to turn it into a diagonal matrix:

$$
M' = \begin{pmatrix} A & O \\ O & C-B^TA^{-1}B \end{pmatrix}
$$

thereby preserving positive definiteness. As long as $ M'$ is positive definite, then $ M $ is positive definite.
###### Positive definite plus semi-definite determinant does not decrease
Assume $ A,B $ are two complex $ Hermitian $ matrices, where $ A $ is positive definite, and $ B $ is semi-definite, then:

$$
det(A+B) \ge det(A)
$$

Proof:
We hope to see the difference of $ A+B\text{with}A $ more intuitively, using the Schur complement:

$$
det(A+B)=det(A) \cdot det(I+A^{-1}B)
$$

Of course, this can also be seen at a glance ( 
Then we only need to prove:

$$
det(I+A^{-1}B) \ge 1
$$

At this point, using $ A\text{positive definite, thus}A^{-1}\text{positive definite} $ (all eigenvalues are greater than 0), we can split:

$$
det(I+A^{-1/2} \cdot A^{-1/2} \cdot B)
$$

Treat the last two matrices as one block and swap the multiplication order with the first:

$$
det(I+A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})
$$

This is equivalent to performing a congruence transformation on B, which preserves eigenvalues, so $ A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}$ is semi-definite, and clearly it is symmetric, so it can be diagonalized:

$$
A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2} = Q \Sigma Q^*
$$

Then $\Sigma $ has non-negative eigenvalues, so the original determinant is equal to

$$
det(I+ \Sigma) >= det(I) = 1
$$

And the proof is complete.
