When studying vectors in high school, teachers often say that one must have the concept of a basis and learn to represent all vectors using a set of bases. When learning linear algebra, the basis is still a very good means of abstracting into the concrete. An abstract linear map often leaves one at a loss, but by setting up a basis, we can see individual, tangible objects.
Usually, the method is to take a set of bases, extend it to the entire space, and then analyze. For multiple spaces, we often set up the smallest space and then expand it one by one; this is usually easier to describe.

## Preparation: Basis Extension Theorem
Let $ V $ be a finite-dimensional vector space,$ n = \dim(V)$.

if the set of vectors $\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_m\}$ is $ V $ a linearly independent set in

1. then there must exist $ m \le n $;
    
2. If $ m < n $, $ n-m $ a vector $\alpha_{m+1}, \dots, \alpha_n \in V $such that the set of vectors $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m, \alpha_{m+1}, \dots, \alpha_n\}$ Construction $ V $ of a **basis**.

Proof:
Let the initial linearly independent set be $ S_m = \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m\}$.

- If $ S_m $ can span $ V $(that is, $\text{span}(S_m) = V $), then by definition,$ S_m $ is $ V $ a basis, and the extension process ends.
    
- If $ S_m $ cannot span $ V $, then there must exist a vector $\beta \in V $, such that $\beta \notin \text{span}(S_m)$.

thus we can take $\beta $ to add $ S_m $ to form a new linearly independent set of vectors, below we only need to prove that this process can span the entire space in a finite number of steps, which means we only need to prove that a $ n $ dimensional space has at most $ n $ linearly independent vectors. Using proof by contradiction, assume $ V $ there are $ n+1 $ linearly independent vectors, consider

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i \alpha_i + \lambda_{n+1} \beta=0
$$

then the number of equations is greater than the number of unknowns, $\lambda $ There is a non-zero solution, if $\lambda_{n+1}=0 $then it is linearly dependent on $ S_m $ is contradictory to linear independence, therefore $\lambda_{n+1} \neq 0 $and by moving terms, we know that $\beta $ can be expressed as $ S_m $ is a contradiction.
Thus, we know a $ n $ dimensional space has at most $ n $ linearly independent vector, so the process only requires a finite number of steps.
Thus, the original proposition is established.

## Extension Example 1: Rank-Nullity Theorem
Consider a linear map $\mathcal{A} : V \to W $we always have:

$$
dim(Im(\mathcal{A}))+dim(Ker(\mathcal{A}))=dim(V)
$$

Proof:
We take $ Ker(\mathcal{A})$ a set of basis ${\alpha_1, \alpha_2 ... \alpha_k}$, and expand it to $ V $ a set of basis ${\alpha_1, \alpha_2 ... \alpha_k, \alpha_{k+1}, \alpha_{n}}$, next use $\mathcal{A}$ acting on this set of basis, we get:

$$
\mathcal{A}({\alpha_1, \alpha_2 ... \alpha_k, \alpha_{k+1}, \alpha_{n}})=(0,0...0, \mathcal{A}\alpha_{k+1}, \mathcal{A}\alpha_{n})
$$

To prove the original theorem, it is sufficient to prove that the following $ n-k $ vectors can form a basis for $\mathcal{A}$ the image space. First,$\forall \beta \in V $, since ${\alpha_1, \alpha_2 ... \alpha_k, \alpha_{k+1}, \alpha_{n}}$ is $ V $ a basis for

$$
\beta=\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\alpha_i
$$

Use $\mathcal{A}$ acting on both sides, we get:

$$
\mathcal{A}\beta=\sum_{i=k+1}^n \lambda_i\mathcal{A}\alpha_i
$$

That is, for $\forall \mathcal{A}\beta \in V $ they can all be $\mathcal{A}\alpha_{k+1},..., \mathcal{A}\alpha_{n}$ expressed, it is sufficient to prove $\mathcal{A}\alpha_{k+1},..., \mathcal{A}\alpha_{n}$ that they are linearly independent to show that they form $ Im\mathcal{A}$ a basis. We consider:

$$
\sum_{i=k+1}^n \lambda_i\mathcal{A}\alpha_i=0
$$

By linearity, we can write $\mathcal{A} = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i T(v_i)$.

$$
$\sum_{i=k+1}^n \lambda_i\mathcal{A}\alpha_i=\mathcal{A}\sum_{i=k+1}^n \lambda_i\alpha_i=0
$$

This shows

$$
\sum_{i=k+1}^n \lambda_i\alpha_i \in Ker(A)
$$

In other words, this can be $ Ker(A)$ expressed by the basis:

$$
\sum_{i=k+1}^n \lambda_i\alpha_i=\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i\alpha_i
$$

Rearranging the terms, we get:

$$
\sum_{i=k+1}^n \lambda_i\alpha_i-\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i\alpha_i=0
$$

Using $ V $ the linear independence of the basis, the above equation holds if and only if $\forall i, \lambda_i=0 $, this is precisely $\mathcal{A}\alpha_{k+1},..., \mathcal{A}\alpha_{n}$ an equivalent condition for linear independence. Thus, we have completed the proof.

## Extension Example 2: Dimension Theorem for Matrix Product Rank Proof

### Theorem Statement
Let the matrix $ A $ the number of columns of the matrix $ B $ the number of rows of the matrix. Let $ B $ the column space of $ V $,$ A $ the solution space (null space) of $ W $is:

$$
\text{rank}(B) = \text{rank}(AB) + \dim(V \cap W)
$$

---
### Proof Process

### 1. Construct Basis Vectors
By augmenting $ V \cap W $ a set of basis $\alpha_1, \cdots, \alpha_r $ to $ B $ the column space $ V $ a basis for:

$$
\alpha_1, \cdots, \alpha_r, \beta_1, \cdots, \beta_s
$$

Clearly, at this point, we have:
* $\dim(V \cap W) = r $*$\text{rank}(B) = \dim(V) = r + s $* Our goal is to prove $\text{rank}(AB) = s $### 2. Determining $ AB $ the generating set for the column space
Since $ B $ the column group is equivalent to $\alpha_1, \cdots, \alpha_r, \beta_1, \cdots, \beta_s $ is equivalent to, by the properties of matrix multiplication,$ AB $ the column group is equivalent to $ A\alpha_1, \cdots, A\alpha_r, A\beta_1, \cdots, A\beta_s $ is equivalent.
Also because $\alpha_i \in W $($ A $ of the solution space), so we have $ A\alpha_1 = \cdots = A\alpha_r = \mathbf{0}$.
Therefore,$ AB $ the column space can be generated by the remaining vectors:

$$
\text{Range}(AB) = \text{span}\{ A\beta_1, \cdots, A\beta_s \}
$$

### 3. Proof $ A\beta_1, \cdots, A\beta_s $ linearly independent
Suppose a linear combination:

$$
k_1 A\beta_1 + \cdots + k_s A\beta_s = \mathbf{0}
$$

By the properties of linear maps, we get:

$$
A(k_1 \beta_1 + \cdots + k_s \beta_s) = \mathbf{0}
$$

This shows that the vector $\sum_{i=1}^s k_i \beta_i $ is in $ A $ the solution space $ W $ and is in $ B $ the column space $ V $ the intersection. Thus, it belongs to the intersection. $ V \cap W $, can be obtained from its basis vectors $\alpha_1, \cdots, \alpha_r $ Linear representation:

$$
k_1 \beta_1 + \cdots + k_s \beta_s = l_1 \alpha_1 + \cdots + l_r \alpha_r
$$

Rearranging the terms, we get:

$$
k_1 \beta_1 + \cdots + k_s \beta_s - l_1 \alpha_1 - \cdots - l_r \alpha_r = \mathbf{0}
$$

Notice that $\alpha_1, \cdots, \alpha_r, \beta_1, \cdots, \beta_s $ is $ V $ the basis (linearly independent), so its coefficients must all be 0:

$$
k_1 = \cdots = k_s = 0
$$

From this, $ A\beta_1, \cdots, A\beta_s $ linearly independent.

### 4. Conclusion
Since $ A\beta_1, \cdots, A\beta_s $ linearly independent and generate $ AB $ the column space, then they form $ AB $ a basis for the column space. Therefore:

$$
\text{rank}(AB) = s
$$

Substitute into the previous equation:

$$
\text{rank}(B) = r + s = \dim(V \cap W) + \text{rank}(AB)
$$

Rearranging the terms, we get:

$$
\text{rank}(AB) = \text{rank}(B) - \dim(V \cap W) \quad
$$

## Extension Example 3: Dimension Formula for Sum Space

$$
dim(V)+dim(W)=dim(V\cap W)+dim(V+ W)
$$

where $ V + W = \{v + w \mid v \in V, w \in W\}$ is the smallest vector space containing $ V $ and $ W $ the smallest vector space containing.
### Proof Steps

1. **Set a basis for the intersection**:
Let $\dim(V \cap W) = k $, and take its basis to be $\{u_1, \dots, u_k\}$.

2. **Expanded Basis Space**:
   - The basis expanded to $ V $ is: $\{u_1, \dots, u_k, v_1, \dots, v_m\}$, then $\dim(V) = k + m $.
   - The basis expanded to $ W $ is: $\{u_1, \dots, u_k, w_1, \dots, w_n\}$, then $\dim(W) = k + n $.

3. **Basis of the Sum Space**:
Prove that the set $ B = \{u_1, \dots, u_k, v_1, \dots, v_m, w_1, \dots, w_n\}$ is a basis for $ V+W $.
- **Generative Property**:Clearly, any element in $ V+W $ can be linearly represented by $ B $.
    
- Linear Independence: Let

$$
\sum a_i u_i + \sum b_j v_j + \sum c_l w_l = 0
$$

transform to: $\sum c_l w_l = -(\sum a_i u_i + \sum b_j v_j)$.
    
The left side of the equation belongs to $ W $, and the right side belongs to $ V $, therefore this vector belongs to $ V \cap W $.
    
This means that the $\sum c_l w_l $ on the left must be linearly represented by $\{u_1, \dots, u_k\}$. Since $\{u_i, w_l\}$ is a basis for $ W $, it is linearly independent, so all the $ c_l $ must be $ 0 $. By induction, all coefficients are $ 0 $.

4. **Verification of Algebraic Identity**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
   \dim(V) + \dim(W) &= (k + m) + (k + n) \\
   &= k + (k + m + n) \\
   &= \dim(V \cap W) + \dim(V + W)
   \end{aligned}
$$

## Expanded Example 4: Algebraic Multiplicity of Eigenvalue $\ge $ Geometric Multiplicity
Let us assume that $\lambda $ is an eigenvalue of the matrix $ A $. The algebraic multiplicity is the multiplicity of the root $\lambda $ in $\det(A-\lambda I)=0 $, and the geometric multiplicity is the number of solutions to $(A-\lambda I)\alpha=0 $, which is $\alpha $. We always have algebraic multiplicity $\ge $ geometric multiplicity.

Proof:
Take a set of basis vectors for the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda $, which is $\alpha_1,...,\alpha_k $. $ k $ is the geometric multiplicity. Extend the basis to the entire space to get $\alpha_1,...,\alpha_n $. We apply $ A $:

$$
A(\alpha_1,...,\alpha_n)=(\alpha_1,...,\alpha_n)\begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_k & B \\ O & C \end{pmatrix}
$$

Thus, $ A $ is similar to $\begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_k & B \\ O & C \end{pmatrix}$, so they have the same characteristic polynomial. Consider its characteristic polynomial:

$$
det\begin{pmatrix} (\lambda-x) I_k & B \\ O & C \end{pmatrix}
$$

Expanding gives that there is at least an $ k $ power term of $\lambda $. Combining this with the unknown $ C $, we know that the algebraic multiplicity $\ge $ geometric multiplicity.
## Change of Basis and Transition Matrix

### 1. Definition of the Transition Matrix
Let $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n $ and $\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_n $ be two bases for the linear space $ V $.
If the basis $\beta $ can be linearly represented by the basis $\alpha $:

$$
\begin{cases}
\beta_1 = b_{11}\alpha_1 + b_{12}\alpha_2 + \dots + b_{1n}\alpha_n \\
\beta_2 = b_{21}\alpha_1 + b_{22}\alpha_2 + \dots + b_{2n}\alpha_n \\
\quad \dots \quad \dots \\
\beta_n = b_{n1}\alpha_1 + b_{n2}\alpha_2 + \dots + b_{nn}\alpha_n
\end{cases}
$$

Then the **transition matrix** $ B $ from basis $\{\alpha_i\}$ to basis $\{\beta_i\}$ is defined as:

$$
(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) 
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}
b_{11} & b_{21} & \dots & b_{n1} \\
b_{12} & b_{22} & \dots & b_{n2} \\
\vdots & \vdots & B & \vdots \\
b_{1n} & b_{2n} & \dots & b_{nn}
\end{bmatrix}}_{B}
$$

> [!cite] Note
> The $ j $ column of the transition matrix $ B $ is the coordinate of the new basis $\beta_j $ in the old basis $\alpha $.

**Two-way Transformation Relationship**
If the transition matrix from $\alpha $ to $\beta $ is $ B $, and the transition matrix from $\beta $ to $\alpha $ is $ A $, then:
* $(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) A $ * Combined with $(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) B $
* We get: $(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) BA \implies BA = I_n $
* Therefore: **$ A = B^{-1}$
---

### 2. Coordinate Transformation Formula
**Theorem:** If the coordinates of a vector $\alpha $ in the basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ are $ X $, and the transition matrix from the old basis to the new basis $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ is $ B $, then the coordinates of $\alpha $ in the new basis are $ B^{-1}X $.

**Derivation Process:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\alpha &= [\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n] X \\
&= ([\beta_1 \dots \beta_n] B^{-1}) X \\
&= [\beta_1 \dots \beta_n] (B^{-1}X)
\end{aligned}
$$

---

### 3. Basis Determination Theorem
**Theorem:** Let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ be a basis for the linear space $ V $, and $ B $ be a square matrix of order $ n $.
Let:

$$
(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) B
$$

Then:
> [!important] Conclusion
> $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ is a basis for $ V $, and $\iff $ $ B $ is invertible

Sufficiency: Right multiplication by $ B^{-1}$ shows it. Necessity: Thus, the set of vectors $\beta_i $ expresses the basis $\alpha_i $, and by the two-way transformation relationship in 1, it is known.

## Linear Mapping, Matrix, and Basis
### To study a linear mapping, one only needs to look at the image of the basis
Consider the linear mapping $\mathcal{A} : V \to M $. We want to consider the image of an arbitrary $\beta \in V $ mapping, which can first be represented by the basis: 

$$
\beta = \sum \lambda_i\alpha_i
$$

Then apply $\mathcal{A}$ to both sides, getting:

$$
\mathcal{A}\beta=\sum\lambda_i(\mathcal{A}\alpha_i)
$$

Thus, as long as we know the image of the basis, we can determine the image of any $\beta \in V $.
### Linear mapping can be represented by a matrix
In the linear space $ V $, the same linear mapping $\mathcal{A}$ corresponds to different matrices under different bases.

Let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ be a basis for $ V $. The action of the linear mapping $\mathcal{A}$ on the basis vectors can be represented as:

$$
\mathcal{A}(\alpha_j) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ij}\alpha_i
$$

Written in matrix form:

$$
\mathcal{A}(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n) A
$$

Here, $ A $ is the matrix of the mapping $\mathcal{A}$ in the basis $\{\alpha_i\}$.
The $ j $ column of matrix $ A $ is the coordinate of the image $\mathcal{A}(\alpha_j)$ of the $ j $ basis vector in the original basis.
The "logical operation" between linear mappings can be perfectly transformed into the "algebraic operation" between matrices. This turns complex geometric research into simple arithmetic:

Let $\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{B}$ be a linear transformation on $ V $, and the matrices in the basis $\alpha $ are $ A, B $, then:

- **Linear Combination:** The matrix corresponding to $ k\mathcal{A} + m\mathcal{B}$ is $ kA + mB $.
    
- **Composite Mapping:** The matrix corresponding to $\mathcal{A} \circ \mathcal{B}$ (doing $\mathcal{B}$ then $\mathcal{A}$) is **$ AB $**.
    
    - _Note: The order of matrix multiplication is consistent with the order of composition of mappings, which is the fundamental reason why matrix multiplication is defined so "strangely".
        
- **Inverse Mapping:** If $\mathcal{A}$ is invertible, the matrix corresponding to its inverse mapping $\mathcal{A}^{-1}$ is $ A^{-1}$.
### Origin of Similar Matrices: Change of Basis Formula
Theorem: Let the matrix of a linear mapping $\mathcal{A}$ in the basis $\{\alpha_i\}$ be $ A $, and in the basis $\{\beta_i\}$ be $ A'$. If the transition matrix from $\{\alpha_i\}$ to $\{\beta_i\}$ is $ B $, then:

$$
A' = B^{-1}AB
$$

Imagine you want to calculate the action of $\mathcal{A}$ on a vector, but you only have the coordinates in the new basis $ X'$:

1. **$ B X'$**: First, restore the new coordinates to the old basis coordinates (because $ A $ only recognizes the old basis).
    
2. **$ A (B X')$**: Perform the linear mapping operation in the old basis.
    
3. **$ B^{-1} (A B X')$**: Then, transform the resulting result back to the coordinates in the new basis.
    

This is why the definition of **matrix similarity** is $ A' = P^{-1}AP $. So-called similar matrices are actually **different "incarnations" of the same linear mapping in different bases**.
Since the matrix of the same mapping is different in different bases, we naturally ask: **Can we find a basis that makes the matrix $ A'$ as simple as possible?**

- **Diagonalization:** If we can find a basis such that $ A'$ is a diagonal matrix, then this basis is the **eigenvector** basis.
    
- **Jordan Form:** If it cannot be diagonalized, this basis is the generalized eigenvector basis.

At the same time, in the context of continuous functions, we can also choose beautiful bases.
### Fourier Polynomials, Legendre Polynomials, and Chebyshev Polynomials
We define the inner product for functions as:

$$
(f(x),g(x))=\int_{-1}^1 f(x)g(x)dx
$$

Similarly, we can perform operations like those for vectors, but now we are dealing with infinite-dimensional vectors (with infinitely many values in the domain). Thus, a natural idea is whether we can use $ 1,x,x^2...x^n..$ as a set of basis vectors, but in reality, this basis corresponds to the $ Hilbert $ matrix, which is not a good matrix. Just like with vectors, if we choose an orthogonal basis, it is very beautiful; the corresponding coefficients can be obtained simply by taking the inner product.
#### Fourier Polynomials
For periodic functions, we can choose $ sinx, cosx, sin2x, cos2x...$ as a basis. It can be verified that this is an orthogonal basis, so we can perform a projection:

$$
f(x)=c_{1}sinx+c_{2}cosx+c_{3}sin2x+c_4cos2x+...
$$

To solve for the coefficients, we only need to take the inner product on both sides, for example:

$$
c_1=\frac{\int_{-1}^1f(x)\cdot sinxdx}{\int_{-1}^1sinx \cdot sinxdx}
$$

This way, the corresponding coefficients are found, which is very convenient.
#### Legendre Polynomials
If we want an orthogonal basis, we usually perform Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization on an existing basis. We do the same thing for functions:
##### 1. Preparation

- **Definition of Interval**: We usually choose $[-1, 1]$ (which are called standard Legendre polynomials on this interval).
    
- **Definition of Inner Product**: $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} f(x)g(x) dx $.
    
- **Original Basis Vectors**: $ v_0 = 1, v_1 = x, v_2 = x^2, \dots $
    

---

##### 2. Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Steps

###### Step 1: Determine the first basis vector $ P_0 $, which is directly taken as $ P_0(x) = v_0 = 1 $.

###### Step 2: Construct $ P_1 $ We need to subtract the projection of it onto the direction of $ P_0 $ from $ v_1 = x $:

$$
P_1(x) = x - \frac{\langle x, P_0 \rangle}{\langle P_0, P_0 \rangle} P_0
$$

- Calculate the inner product: $\langle x, 1 \rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} x dx = 0 $ (The integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is 0).
    
- Conclusion: **$ P_1(x) = x $**.
    

###### Step 3: Construct $ P_2 $ We need to subtract the projections of it onto the directions of $ P_0 $ and $ P_1 $ from $ v_2 = x^2 $:

$$
P_2(x) = x^2 - \frac{\langle x^2, P_0 \rangle}{\langle P_0, P_0 \rangle} P_0 - \frac{\langle x^2, P_1 \rangle}{\langle P_1, P_1 \rangle} P_1
$$

1. $\langle x^2, P_0 \rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} x^2 \cdot 1 dx = \left[ \frac{1}{3}x^3 \right]_{-1}^{1} = \frac{2}{3}$ 2.$\langle P_0, P_0 \rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} 1 \cdot 1 dx = 2 $ 3.$\langle x^2, P_1 \rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} x^2 \cdot x dx = 0 $ (The integral of an odd function is 0)
    
4. Substitute into the formula: $ P_2(x) = x^2 - \frac{2/3}{2}(1) - 0 = \mathbf{x^2 - \frac{1}{3}}$
    

---

##### 3. Normalization and Standard Form

Mathematicians often scale the result for convenience (to make $ P_n(1) = 1 $).

- For $ P_2(x) = x^2 - \frac{1}{3}$, multiplying by $\frac{3}{2}$ gives: $\mathbf{P_2(x) = \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1)}$.
    

By extension, you will get:

- $ P_0(x) = 1 $-$ P_1(x) = x $-$ P_2(x) = \frac{1}{2}(3x^2 - 1)$-$ P_3(x) = \frac{1}{2}(5x^3 - 3x)$
    
This yields the Legendre polynomials. At this point, performing a projection still only requires taking the inner product, which is much more convenient.
#### Chebyshev Polynomials

$$
T_n(\cos \theta) = \cos(n\theta)
$$

Simply put, $ n $ the polynomial corresponding to the double angle formula, due to the orthogonality of trigonometric functions, this is also an orthogonal basis:

$$
\int_{0}^{\pi} \cos(m\theta) \cos(n\theta) d\theta = 0 \quad (m \neq n)
$$

Furthermore, Chebyshev is superior to Legendre in numerical analysis because we can use $ FFT $ to greatly optimize the computation speed.
