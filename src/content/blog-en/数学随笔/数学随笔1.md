# Constructing matrices to connect various elements, and thus further processing
#### For example, in the calculation of determinants, we have what is called adding rows.
##### For the determinant of a matrix where all elements except the diagonal elements are the same, solving

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a & t & \cdots & t \\
t & a & \cdots & t \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
t & t & \cdots & a
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The determinant of a matrix where all elements except the diagonal elements are the same, one way is that we can directly perform row operations, and another operation is to construct a larger matrix:

$$
\left(
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
1 & t & t & \cdots & t\\ \hline
0 & a & t & \cdots & t \\
0 & t & a & \cdots & t \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & t & t & \cdots & a
\end{array}
\right)
$$

At this point, the determinant can be viewed as expanding along the first column, which remains unchanged. At this point, performing elimination seems simple.

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & t & t & \cdots & t \\
-1 & a-t & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
-1 & 0 & a-t & \cdots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
-1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & a-t
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Then, we can eliminate the first row or the first column.
##### Adding rows + algebraic cofactor
(PKU 25 Fall Linear Algebra Midterm) Given $|A| = 5, a_{11} = 2 $,$ A_{ij}$ is the element $ a_{ij}$ of the algebraic cofactor.

$$
\text{calculate:} 
\begin{vmatrix}
A_{22} & A_{23} & \cdots & A_{2n} \\
A_{32} & A_{33} & \cdots & A_{3n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
A_{n2} & A_{n3} & \cdots & A_{nn}
\end{vmatrix}
$$

We easily think of the orthogonality of cofactors, adding rows:

$$
\begin{vmatrix}
A_{11} & A_{12} & A_{13} & \cdots & A_{1n} \\
0 & A_{22} & A_{23} & \cdots & A_{2n} \\
0 & A_{32} & A_{33} & \cdots & A_{3n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & A_{n2} & A_{n3} & \cdots & A_{nn}
\end{vmatrix}
$$

At this point, using the classic $ det(AB) = det(A) \cdot det(B)$, we left-multiply the original matrix by its transpose:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{21} & a_{31} & \cdots & a_{n1} \\ a_{12} & a_{22} & a_{32} & \cdots & a_{n2} \\ a_{13} & a_{23} & a_{33} & \cdots & a_{n3} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{1n} & a_{2n} & a_{3n} & \cdots & a_{nn} \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
A_{11} & A_{12} & \cdots & A_{1n} \\
0 & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{2n} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & A_{n2} & \cdots & A_{nn}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Utilizing orthogonality, we also have:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
a_{11}A_{11} & a_{11}A_{12} + \dots & \cdots & a_{11}A_{1n} + \dots \\
0 & |A| & \cdots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & |A|
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Thus

$$
a_{11}A_{11}|A|^{n-1} = |A| \cdot A_{11} \cdot \det(\text{submatrix}) \implies \text{cofactor} = |A|^{n-2} \cdot a_{11}
$$

#### In matrix operations, composing large matrices is also common
##### Basic preparation: Rank and determinant of block matrices
According to the Laplace expansion, we know:

$$
\det \begin{pmatrix} A & O \\ C & B \end{pmatrix} = \det(A) \det(B)
$$

At the same time, we also have:

$$
rank \begin{pmatrix} A & O \\ O & B \end{pmatrix} = rank(A)+rank(B)
$$

Can be obtained by performing row operations, and:

$$
rank \begin{pmatrix} A & O \\ C & B \end{pmatrix} \ge rank(A)+rank(B)
$$

##### Proof of the Frobenius inequality

$$
\text{rank}(ABC) + \text{rank}(B) \ge \text{rank}(AB) + \text{rank}(BC)
$$

We construct the following $ 2 \times 2 $ block matrix $ M $:

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} AB & O \\ B & BC \end{pmatrix}
$$

It is not difficult to see here that$ rank(M)>=rank(AB)+rank(BC)$, if we construct the bottom-left corner as $ O $, although it is directly equal, we have no room for operations. At this point, we perform row and column operations to eliminate the top-left and bottom-right corners:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} AB & O \\ B & BC \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 - AR_2} \begin{pmatrix} O & -ABC \\ B & BC \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{C_2 - C_1C} \begin{pmatrix} O & -ABC \\ B & O \end{pmatrix}
$$

Then we obtain the desired conclusion
###### Sylvester's inequality
Although it is unrelated to the theme, as long as we take $ B $ to be $ I $, we also obtain Sylvester's inequality:

$$
\text{rank}(AC) + \text{rank}(I) \ge \text{rank}(A) + \text{rank}(C)
$$

That is:

$$
\text{rank}(AC) \ge \text{rank}(A) + \text{rank}(C) - n
$$

This inequality can be greatly useful in rank-related problems. For example, when proving $\text{rank}(A) = n-1 $ the companion matrix has rank 1:
- Because $\text{rank}(A) = n-1 $, there exists a $ n-1 $ subdeterminant that is not $ 0 $therefore, $ A^* \neq O $, we get $\text{rank}(A^*) \ge 1 $.
    
- At this point, $\det(A) = 0 $, substituting into the definition formula gives $ A \cdot A^* = O $.
    
- Constructing the inequality of rank:
    
    According to $\text{rank}(AB) = 0 \implies \text{rank}(A) + \text{rank}(B) \le n $:

$$
\text{rank}(A) + \text{rank}(A^*) \le n
$$

$$
(n-1) + \text{rank}(A^*) \le n \implies \text{rank}(A^*) \le 1
$$

##### Proof $\det(I+AB) = \det(I+BA)$
Similarly, we construct:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} I & A \\ O & I+BA \end{pmatrix}
$$

Then perform elimination operations:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} I & A \\ O & I+BA \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - BR_1} \begin{pmatrix} I & A \\ -B & I \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 + AR_2} \begin{pmatrix} I-AB & O \\ B & I \end{pmatrix}
$$

Thus proving the conclusion
##### $ AB $ and $ BA $ the relationship with the characteristic polynomial
Let $ A \in \mathbb{F}^{m \times n}, B \in \mathbb{F}^{n \times m}$, then:

$$
\lambda^n \det(\lambda I_m - AB) = \lambda^m \det(\lambda I_n - BA)
$$

By the same method, construct the matrix:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_m - AB & O \\ B & I_n \end{pmatrix}
$$

Elimination:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_m - AB & O \\ B & I_n \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_1 + AR_2} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_m & A \\ B & I_n \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 - \frac{1}{\lambda}BR_2} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda I_m & A \\ O & I_n - \frac{1}{\lambda}B A \end{pmatrix}
$$

Thus

$$
\det(\lambda I_m - AB) = \lambda^{m-n} \det(\lambda I_n - BA)
$$

This is obviously equivalent to the conclusion.
##### Proof of the Binet-Cauchy formula
The specific formula is: if $ A $ is $ n \times m $ Matrix,$ B $ is $ m \times n $ Matrix($ n \le m $), then:

$$
\det(AB) = \sum_{1 \le j_1 < j_2 < \dots < j_n \le m} \det(A_{j_1, \dots, j_n}) \det(B^{j_1, \dots, j_n})
$$

where $ A_{j_1, \dots, j_n}$ is the matrix formed by taking $ A $ the $ j_1, \dots, j_n $ column, formed by the row.$ B^{j_1, \dots, j_n}$ is the matrix formed by taking $ B $ the $ j_1, \dots, j_n $ Proof:
Construct a $(n+m) \times (n+m)$ block matrix:

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix} I_m & B \\ -A & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Expand $ M $ directly by Laplace expansion. Since $ I_m $ is the identity matrix, selecting rows and columns gives a nonzero contribution only when they match. This naturally leads to the form $\sum \det(A_J) \det(B^J)$.

# Vandermonde determinant
Determining the determinant form through polynomial factorization
##### Vandermonde determinant
Let $ n $ The n-th order Vandermonde determinant is:

$$
V_n = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n \\ x_1^2 & x_2^2 & \cdots & x_n^2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_1^{n-1} & x_2^{n-1} & \cdots & x_n^{n-1} \end{vmatrix}
$$

We want to prove:$ V_n = \prod_{1 \le i < j \le n} (x_j - x_i)$.
When $ n=2 $ Time:

$$
V_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ x_1 & x_2 \end{vmatrix} = x_2 - x_1
$$

The formula holds.
Assume the formula holds for $ n-1 $ the Vandermonde determinant.
Expanding along the last column, it is clearly a polynomial of the highest degree $ x_n^{n-1}$ The polynomial is of degree $ V_n = \prod_{1 \le i < j \le n-1} (x_j - x_i)$The highest degree coefficient is, by the induction hypothesis, $ x_n $ equals $\forall i < n, i \in \mathbb{N}^+$ when the polynomial is $ 0 $which means it can be factored as:

$$
\prod_{1 \le i \le n-1}(x_n - x_i)\prod_{1 \le i < j \le n-1} (x_j - x_i)
$$

The conclusion can be obtained by combining them.
##### Cauchy Determinant
Proof of the $ n $ order Cauchy Determinant: (Provided by AI, too lazy to type)

$$
C_n = \begin{vmatrix} \frac{1}{a_1+b_1} & \cdots & \frac{1}{a_1+b_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{1}{a_n+b_1} & \cdots & \frac{1}{a_n+b_n} \end{vmatrix} = \frac{\prod_{1 \le i < j \le n} (a_j - a_i)(b_j - b_i)}{\prod_{i=1}^n \prod_{j=1}^n (a_i + b_j)}
$$

###### Step 1: Basic Steps

When $ n=1 $:

- Left side $= \frac{1}{a_1+b_1}$ - Right side: The numerator is an empty product (defined as $ 1 $), and the denominator is $ a_1+b_1 $.
    
- The equality holds.
    

###### Step 2: Inductive Hypothesis and Determinant Transformation

Assume the formula holds for the $ n-1 $ order determinant. For the $ n $ order determinant, we need to use elementary row operations to reduce its order.

1. Eliminate elements other than those in the first row:

Perform a column transformation on the determinant. Subtract the $ n $ column from the $ j $ column ($ j=1, 2, \dots, n-1 $):

$$
c_{ij}' = \frac{1}{a_i+b_j} - \frac{1}{a_i+b_n} = \frac{b_n - b_j}{(a_i+b_j)(a_i+b_n)}
$$

**2. Extracting Common Factors:**

- Extract the common factor $(b_n - b_j)$ from every column $ j $ ($ j < n $).
    
- Extract the common factor $\frac{1}{a_i+b_n}$ from every row $ i $.
    
- Extract the common factor $\frac{1}{a_n+b_j}$ from every column $ j $ ($ j < n $) (to make the last row simpler).
    

After a series of complex extractions (or direct algebraic manipulation of each term), the determinant can be transformed into:

$$
C_n = \frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(b_n-b_j) \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(a_n-a_i)}{\prod_{i=1}^n(a_i+b_n) \cdot \prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(a_n+b_j)} \cdot C_{n-1}
$$

###### Step 3: Recursive Solving

Substitute the $ C_{n-1}$ from the inductive hypothesis into the above recurrence relation:

1. **Numerator part**: $ C_{n-1}$ contributes the difference product of $ 1 \le i < j \le n-1 $, and the recurrence term contributes the difference product containing $ n $ (such as $ a_n-a_i $ and $ b_n-b_j $). Combined, they exactly form the complete difference product of $ 1 \le i < j \le n $.
    
2. **Denominator part**: $ C_{n-1}$ contributes all combinations of the first $ n-1 $ rows, and the recurrence term exactly completes all combinations of the $ n $ row and the $ n $ column, $(a_i+b_n)$ and $(a_n+b_j)$.
    

**Conclusion**: The equality holds for the $ n $ order.
