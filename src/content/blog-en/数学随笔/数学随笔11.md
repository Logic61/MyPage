Quoting Grandpa Gilbert:
>The Singular Value Decomposition is a highlight of linear algebra.



The singular value decomposition writes a matrix $ A $ as $ A=U\Sigma V^T $, where $ U,V $ are orthogonal and $\Sigma $ is diagonal. We can think of this as choosing new bases in the input and output spaces so that we get a nice diagonal matrix. Geometrically, the map breaks into an orthogonal transformation, a stretch, and another orthogonal transformation. If we expand $\Sigma $, we get:

$$
A=\sigma_1u_1v_1^T+\sigma_2u_2v_2^T+…
$$

We can decompose $ A $ into a sum of several rank-one matrices. If we need to, we can discard $\sigma $, that is, the matrices with smaller singular values, thus obtaining a beautiful approximation. We can prove that retaining the matrices corresponding to the top $ k $ singular values is the best rank $ k $ approximation of $ A $, which is the $ PCA $ Theorem.

## Any matrix can be decomposed this way
We know that real symmetric matrices can be orthogonally diagonalized, but general matrices do not have such nice properties. Fortunately, symmetric matrices are easy to construct. Grandpa Gilbert said that the problem concerning $ A $ often reduces to the problem concerning $ A^TA $.

$$
A^TA=PDP^T
$$

Rearranging terms to reveal a diagonal matrix:

$$
P^TA^TAP=D
$$

At this point, we can view $ AP $ as a whole, and denote it as $ G $. Then we have $ G^T G=D $, which shows that $ G $ is an orthogonal matrix! Because the inner product of the off-diagonal elements is $ 0 $, we can get:

$$
AP=G
$$

is an orthogonal matrix. Let $ P=[\beta_1, \beta_2 ... \beta_n]$, $ G=[\alpha_1, \alpha_2 ... \alpha_n]$. Then we have:

$$
[A\beta_1, A\beta_2 ... A\beta_n]=[\alpha_1, \alpha_2 ... \alpha_n]
$$

Using the non-negativity of eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix, $ D $ we set it to $ diag (\sigma_1^2 ... \sigma_n^2)$, then we have $||\alpha_i||=\sigma_i $. We can then divide by $\sigma_i $ to construct a standard orthonormal basis, denoted as $\gamma_i=\alpha_i / \sigma_i $. Then we have:

$$
AP=[\gamma_1, \gamma_2 ... \gamma_n]\begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \sigma_3 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & \sigma_n \end{pmatrix}
$$

Because $ P $ is also an orthogonal matrix, multiplying on the right by $ P^T $ we obtain the form we want.

## SVD and the four fundamental subspaces
We arrange them in order of $\sigma $ size, letting $\sigma_1 $ be the largest and $\sigma_n $ be the smallest. By using the non-negativity of $\sigma $, we know that starting from the $ r+1 $-th singular value, $\sigma $ are all $ 0 $, and $ r $ is the rank of the matrix. Thus, we can also see:

$$
A\beta_{r+1}=...=A\beta_n=0
$$

This shows that $\beta_{r+1}...\beta_n $ forms the null space of $ A $. Then the first $ r $ $\beta $ is the orthogonal complement of the later vectors, which means the preceding vectors form the row space of $ A $. Similarly, by transposing, we know that the first $ r $ $\gamma $ form the column space of $ A $, and the remaining $\gamma $ form the left null space of $ A $. SVD directly exposes the four fundamental subspaces of $ A $ to us. Then, by using $ A\beta_i=\sigma_i \gamma_i $, we obtain the mapping between the row space and the column space. We can take a transpose:

$$
A^T=V\Sigma U^T
$$

And then multiply by $ U $ from the right to get:

$$
A^T \gamma_i=\sigma_i\beta_i
$$

This means that the row space and the column space have a bijection. This is consistent with them having the same dimension.

## Pseudo-inverse quick solution
When a matrix $ A $ is not a square matrix, or it is a square matrix but is not invertible (a singular matrix), we cannot define the standard inverse matrix $ A^{-1}$. To solve this problem, mathematicians proposed the concept of the **generalized inverse**, the most commonly used and accurate one of which is called the **Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse** (usually denoted as $ A^+$).

---

### 1. Intuitive definition based on SVD

This is the simplest and most practical way to understand the pseudo-inverse. Assume the singular value decomposition of the matrix $ A $ is:

$$
A = U \Sigma V^T
$$

Then the pseudo-inverse of $ A $, $ A^+$, is defined as:

$$
A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T
$$

**How is the $\Sigma^+$ here obtained?**

1. Transpose the matrix $\Sigma $ (the shape changes from $ m \times n $ to $ n \times m $).
    
2. Take the reciprocal of all **non-zero** singular values on the diagonal ($\frac{1}{\sigma_i}$).
    
3. Keep all **0** elements unchanged.
    

---

### 2. The four conditions that the pseudo-inverse must satisfy

For a matrix $ G $ to be the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of $ A $, it must strictly satisfy the following four Penrose equations:

1. **$ AGA = A $
    
2. **$ GAG = G $
    
3. **$(AG)^H = AG $
    
4. **$(GA)^H = GA $**

It is easy to verify that the pseudo-inverse given by SVD satisfies these four equations and is easy to compute. At this point, if we want to solve $ Ax = b $, we just multiply both sides by the pseudo-inverse to get the least squares solution.

## Principal Component Analysis (PCA) Theorem and Proof

### 1. Theorem Description
**Theorem (Principal Component Analysis):**

$$
\min_{\text{dim}V=k} Q(A, V) = Q(A, V_k) = \lambda_{k+1} + \dots + \lambda_m
$$

> [!abstract] Physical Meaning
> In the Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^m $ of all $ k $ subspaces, the point $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ to the main subspace $ V_k = \langle \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k \rangle $ the sum of the squares of the distances is minimized.

---

### 2. Proof Process

#### Step 1: Subspace Projection Representation
Let $ V $ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m $ of dimension $ k $, and let $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_k $ be an orthonormal basis of $ V $.
Note $ B = [\omega_1 \dots \omega_k]$, then $ B^T B = I_k $.

$ B B^T \alpha_i $ be $\alpha_i $ the orthogonal projection onto the subspace $ V $ :

$$
\begin{aligned}
& (\alpha_i, \omega_1)\omega_1 + \dots + (\alpha_i, \omega_k)\omega_k \\
= & \omega_1(\omega_1^T \alpha_i) + \dots + \omega_k(\omega_k^T \alpha_i) \\
= & (\omega_1 \omega_1^T + \dots + \omega_k \omega_k^T)\alpha_i = B B^T \alpha_i
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Step 2: Pythagorean Theorem and Target Transformation
applying the Pythagorean theorem and summing: $\alpha_i (i=1, \dots, n)$ To make the sum of the squares of the perpendicular lines

$$
\underbrace{\sum \|\alpha_i\|^2}_{\text{tr}(A^T A) = \lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_m \text{ (Fixed)}} = \underbrace{\sum \|B B^T \alpha_i\|^2}_{\text{Sum of squares of orthogonal projections}} + \underbrace{\sum \|\alpha_i - B B^T \alpha_i\|^2}_{\text{Sum of squares of perpendicular lines} = Q(A, V)}
$$

take the minimum, it is sufficient to make the sum of the squares of the orthogonal projections take the maximum, that is, maximize: $ Q(A, V)$ matrix

$$
\sum_{1 \le i \le n} \|B B^T \alpha_i\|^2 = \|B B^T A\|_F^2
$$

#### Step 3: Simplification of Matrix Trace
Derivation using the relationship between the Frobenius norm and the trace:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|B B^T A\|_F^2 &= \text{tr}((B B^T A)^T (B B^T A)) & \color{green}{\text{Frobenius Norm properties}} \\
&= \text{tr}(A^T B \underbrace{B^T B}_{I_k} B^T A) & \color{green}{B^T B = I_k} \\
&= \text{tr}(A^T B B^T A) \\
&= \text{tr}(B^T A A^T B) \\
&= \text{tr}(B^T P D P^T B) & \color{green}{\text{Let } AA^T = PDP^T} \\
&= \text{tr}(C^T D C) & \color{green}{\text{Let } C = P^T B} \\
&= \text{tr}(C C^T D)
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Step 4: Analysis of Constraint Conditions
Note $ m \times k $ , $ C = P^T B $by

$$
C^T C = B^T P P^T B = B^T B = I_k
$$

Know $ C $ the $ k $ column is composed of pairwise orthogonal unit vectors. Therefore, $ C $ the column set can be extended to $\mathbb{R}^m $ a standard orthonormal basis, forming an orthogonal matrix. Therefore, $ C $ the $ i $ row elements squared sum $ c_i $ satisfies the condition $ 0 \le c_i \le 1 $and:

$$
c_1 + \dots + c_m = \text{tr}(C C^T) = \text{tr}(C^T C) = k
$$

#### Step 5: Drawing the Conclusion
by $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_m $ and the ordering inequality, we get:

$$
\text{tr}(C C^T D) = c_1 \lambda_1 + \dots + c_m \lambda_m \le \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_k
$$

The equality can be obtained at $ C = \begin{bmatrix} I_k \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$,$ B = P C = [\beta_1 \dots \beta_k]$, that is, $ V = \langle \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k \rangle = V_k $ when.

> [!important] Uniqueness
> When $\lambda_k > \lambda_{k+1}$ the equality is obtained only at $ V_k = \langle \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k \rangle $ obtained, and at this time, $ k $ the principal subspace is unique.

# Best Low-Rank Approximation

## 1. Theorem Description
Let $ A = P S Q^T = [\beta_1 \beta_2 \dots \beta_m] \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & & & \\ & \ddots & & \\ & & \sigma_r & \\ & & & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \gamma_1^T \\ \gamma_2^T \\ \vdots \\ \gamma_n^T \end{bmatrix}$ is a real matrix $ A \in \mathbb{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})$ the **SVD decomposition**.

Where:
* $ P, Q $ are $ m $ and $ n $ orthogonal matrix of degree.
* $ r $ is a matrix $ A $ of rank ($ r = \text{rank}(A)$).
* $\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0 $ be $ A $ singular value.

Note $ B_k = [\beta_1 \dots \beta_k], 1 \le k \le r $Definition:

$$
A_k = \sigma_1 \beta_1 \gamma_1^T + \dots + \sigma_k \beta_k \gamma_k^T = B_k B_k^T A
$$

> [!tip] **Core Conclusion**
> **$ A_k $ be $ A $ the best approximation in rank $ k $ under:
> that for any rank $ k $ matrix $ M \in \mathbb{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})$, which is:
>

$$
\|A - M\|_F^2 \ge \|A - A_k\|_F^2 = \sigma_{k+1}^2 + \dots + \sigma_r^2
$$

---

## 2. Related Definitions: Frobenius Norm
Here $\|A\|_F $ represents a real matrix $ A = [a_{ij}]$ the **Frobenius norm** of:

$$
\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\text{tr}(A^T A)} = \sqrt{\text{tr}(A A^T)} = \sqrt{\sum_{\substack{1 \le i \le m \\ 1 \le j \le n}} a_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \dots + \sigma_r^2}
$$

---

## 3. Proof Key Points
Note $ A = [\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n]$,$ M = [\xi_1 \dots \xi_n] \in \mathbb{M}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})$.
Let $ V = \langle \xi_1, \dots, \xi_n \rangle $ ($\dim V = k $) denote the column space of $ M $.

1. **Distance Inequality**:

$$
\|\alpha_i - \xi_i\|^2 \ge d(\alpha_i, V)^2, \quad 1 \le i \le n
$$

Where $ d(\alpha, V)$ represents a vector $\alpha $ to a subspace $ V $ Euclidean distance.

2. **Target Function Transformation**:

$$
\|A - M\|_F^2 \ge \sum_{i=1}^n d(\alpha_i, V)^2 = Q(A, V)
$$

According to the **PCA Theorem**:

$$
Q(A, V) \ge Q(A, V_k) = \sigma_{k+1}^2 + \dots + \sigma_r^2
$$

3. **Equality Condition**:
   * Here $ V_k = \langle \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k \rangle $ be $ A $ the principal subspace of a column vector distribution.
   * The equality sign in the above formula can be $ M = A_k = B_k B_k^T A $ when.
   * at this time $ M $ the column vector $ B_k B_k^T \alpha_i $ is exactly $\alpha_i $ on $ k $ the principal subspace $ V_k $ the orthogonal projection on.

4. **Final Result**:
   * $\|\alpha_i - B_k B_k^T \alpha_i\|^2 = d(\alpha_i, V_k)^2, \quad 1 \le i \le n $*$\|A - A_k\|_F^2 = Q(A, V_k) = \sigma_{k+1}^2 + \dots + \sigma_r^2 $

These results can be used for image compression, after converting the image to a grayscale matrix and retaining its first $ k $ The large singular values achieve the effect of image compression. Below is a Python code that implements image compression using singular value decomposition:

```
import numpy as np

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

from PIL import Image

  

# 1. Load the image and convert it to a grayscale matrix

img = Image.open('C:/Users/32372/Desktop/wallpaper/[Philosophical wallpaper]The Three-Body Problem-Wisdom Child.png').convert('L')

A = np.array(img)

  

# 2. Perform Singular Value Decomposition

U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)

  

# 3. Take the top k Reconstruct using the singular values

def reconstruct(k):

    # s is a one-dimensional vector,needs to be constructed into Sigma matrix

    Sigma_k = np.diag(s[:k])

    # matrix multiplication:U_k * Sigma_k * V^T_k

    A_k = U[:, :k] @ Sigma_k @ Vt[:k, :]

    return A_k

  

# 4. visualization comparison

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 4))

for i, k in enumerate([5, 20, 100]):

    plt.subplot(1, 3, i+1)

    plt.imshow(reconstruct(k), cmap='gray')

    plt.title(f'Top {k} Singular Values')

plt.show()
```
