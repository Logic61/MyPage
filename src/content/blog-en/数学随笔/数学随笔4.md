Related organization of the adjugate matrix.

 

The orthogonality of the cofactor allowed us to find the adjugate matrix when calculating the inverse of a matrix:

$$
A^*A = det(A) \cdot I
$$

That is to say, we can find the inverse of A as follows:

$$
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{det(A)} \cdot A^*
$$

Alternatively, we can also find the adjugate matrix in reverse:

$$
A^* = det(A) \cdot A^{-1}
$$

Then we can study the properties of the adjugate matrix based on this, such as its determinant:

$$
det(A^*) = det(A)^n \cdot det(A^{-1}) = det(A)^{n-1}
$$

Nesting dolls, the adjugate of the adjugate matrix:

$$
(A^*)^* = det(A^*) \cdot (A^*)^{-1}
$$

It is not difficult to notice that $ A \cdot \frac{1}{det(A)}$ is $(A^*)^{-1}$, substituting this gives:

$$
(A^*)^* = det(A)^{n-2} \cdot A
$$

It is an interesting thing.
The adjugate matrix can be used to **prove the Cayley-Hamilton theorem**, although it is a bit mysterious:
Let $ A $ be an $ n $ order matrix, whose characteristic polynomial is:

$$
p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A) = \lambda^n + c_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c_1\lambda + c_0
$$

Theorem statement: If the matrix $ A $ is substituted into the polynomial, the result is the zero matrix, i.e.:

$$
p(A) = A^n + c_{n-1}A^{n-1} + \dots + c_1A + c_0I = O
$$

Proof: To connect matrices and the characteristic polynomial, we might think of the adjugate matrix. For the matrix $ M = \lambda I - A $, we have:

$$
(\lambda I - A) \cdot (\lambda I - A)^* = \det(\lambda I - A) \cdot I
$$

The adjugate matrix is clearly a polynomial of degree at most $\lambda $, which is not greater than $ n-1 $:

$$
(\lambda I - A)^* = B_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + B_{n-2}\lambda^{n-2} + \dots + B_1\lambda + B_0
$$

Substitute the expansion into the identity:

$$
(\lambda I - A)(B_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + B_{n-2}\lambda^{n-2} + \dots + B_0) = (\lambda^n + c_{n-1}\lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c_0)I
$$

Expand the left side and arrange by the powers of $\lambda $:

$$
B_{n-1}\lambda^n + (B_{n-2} - AB_{n-1})\lambda^{n-1} + (B_{n-3} - AB_{n-2})\lambda^{n-2} + \dots + (B_0 - AB_1)\lambda - AB_0
$$

Compare the coefficient matrices of the same powers on both sides:
- $\lambda^n: B_{n-1} = I $-$\lambda^{n-1}: B_{n-2} - AB_{n-1} = c_{n-1}I $-$\lambda^{n-2}: B_{n-3} - AB_{n-2} = c_{n-2}I $
    
- ...
    
- $\lambda^1: B_0 - AB_1 = c_1I $-$\lambda^0: -AB_0 = c_0I $ To construct $ p(A)$, we left-multiply the above equations sequentially by $ A^n, A^{n-1}, \dots, A, I $:
- $ A^n(B_{n-1}) = A^n $-$ A^{n-1}(B_{n-2} - AB_{n-1}) = c_{n-1}A^{n-1}$-$ A^{n-2}(B_{n-3} - AB_{n-2}) = c_{n-2}A^{n-2}$
    
- ...
    
- $ A(B_0 - AB_1) = c_1A $-$ I(-AB_0) = c_0I $ Sum all these $ n+1 $ equations. You will be pleasantly surprised to find that all the $ B_i $ terms on the left side form canceling terms (for example, $ A^{n-1}B_{n-2}$ cancels with $-A \cdot A^{n-2}B_{n-2}$ in the next term).

Finally, the left side results in $ O $, and the right side is exactly $ p(A)$:

$$
O = A^n + c_{n-1}A^{n-1} + \dots + c_1A + c_0I
$$

End of proof. Although it is unrelated to the adjugate matrix, we provide another proof, which is a bit more natural: We know that any complex matrix can be similar to an upper triangular matrix, which can be proven by induction. Thus, we assume it is similar to $ U $, and we only need to prove that:

$$
\prod_{i=1}^n (\lambda_i I - U) = (\lambda_1 I - U)(\lambda_2 I - U)\cdots(\lambda_n I - U) = O
$$

First, it is obviously true when $ n=1 $, so assume it is true for degree $ n-1 $, we have:

$$
\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & x \\ \mathbf{0} & X \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{The } 1 \text{ factor }(U- \lambda_1 I) } \times \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & y \\ 0 & O \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{the product of other factors } } = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

This proves the conclusion, which is much shorter and more beautiful. Another proof can be seen in [[Notes 10. Proof: Cayley-Hamilton Theorem (Continuity Method)]]. Furthermore, there is a proof that starts directly from the structure, but requires more prerequisite knowledge, see [[Notes 6. Linear Transformation II—From Invariant Subspace to Hamilton Cayley to Primary Decomposition Theorem]].

Mysterious little formula:

$$
\frac{d}{da_{ij}}(ln(detA))=\frac{1}{detA}\cdot A_{ij}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{d}{dA}(ln(detA))=\frac{1}{detA} \cdot A^*=A^{-T}
$$
