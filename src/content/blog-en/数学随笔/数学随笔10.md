## Properties of diagonalizable matrices extended to non-diagonalizable matrices
Density Lemma:

in the matrix space $\mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$ the set of matrices formed by **matrices with distinct eigenvalues (diagonalizable matrices)** $ D $ is dense.

This means that for any matrix $ A $, there exists a sequence $\{A_k\} \subset D $ such that:

$$
\lim_{k \to \infty} A_k = A
$$

The lemma is proven as follows
**Equivalent Proposition:**

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{ M \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n} \mid M \text{ possesses } n \text{ distinct eigenvalues} \}$.
Then for any $ A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$, there exists in any neighborhood of it $ A_\epsilon \in \mathcal{D}$.

## **Cauchy's Interlacing Theorem**
that is, $ n-1 $ the characteristic values $ A $ of the main submatrix $\mu_j $ are strictly interleaved and distributed in $ n $ the symmetric matrix $ S $ of the main submatrix $\lambda_i $ between.

---

### 1. Constructing the Matrix and Characteristic Polynomial

Let $ S $ be a symmetric matrix of order $ n $. We write it in block form:

$$
S = \begin{bmatrix} A & \mathbf{b} \\ \mathbf{b}^T & c \end{bmatrix}
$$

Where $ A $ is the symmetric principal submatrix of $(n-1) \times (n-1)$, $ c $ is a scalar, and $\mathbf{b}$ is a column vector of dimension $ n-1 $. The characteristic polynomial of $ S $ is defined as $ P_S(\lambda) = \det(S - \lambda I)$. Using the determinant property of block matrices (Schur complement):

$$
P_S(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) \cdot \left( (c - \lambda) - \mathbf{b}^T (A - \lambda I)^{-1} \mathbf{b} \right)
$$

### 2. Expanding the Quadratic Form using Spectral Decomposition

Let the eigenvalues of $ A $ be $\mu_1 \leq \mu_2 \leq \dots \leq \mu_{n-1}$, and the corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors be $ v_1, v_2, \dots, v_{n-1}$.

As mentioned before, we perform the spectral expansion of $(A - \lambda I)^{-1}$:

$$
\mathbf{b}^T (A - \lambda I)^{-1} \mathbf{b} = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \frac{(v_j^T \mathbf{b})^2}{\mu_j - \lambda}
$$

Let $\beta_j^2 = (v_j^T \mathbf{b})^2 $ (which is a non-negative constant), substitute it into the original polynomial:

$$
P_S(\lambda) = P_A(\lambda) \left( c - \lambda - \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \frac{\beta_j^2}{\mu_j - \lambda} \right)
$$

### 3. Analyzing Sign Changes (The Core Proof)

To simplify the discussion, assume that $\beta_j \neq 0 $ and $\mu_j $ are different (the general case can be shown by limit approximation).

Consider the function $ f(\lambda) = c - \lambda - \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \frac{\beta_j^2}{\mu_j - \lambda}$.

- **Jump at the Pole**: As $\lambda $ approaches $\mu_i $ from the left ($\lambda \to \mu_i^-$), the term $\frac{\beta_i^2}{\mu_i - \lambda} \to +\infty $, and thus $ f(\lambda) \to -\infty $.
    
- **Jump at the Pole**: As $\lambda $ approaches $\mu_i $ from the right ($\lambda \to \mu_i^+$), the term $\frac{\beta_i^2}{\mu_i - \lambda} \to -\infty $, and thus $ f(\lambda) \to +\infty $.
    

In every open interval $(\mu_i, \mu_{i+1})$:

1. $ f(\lambda)$ changes continuously from $+\infty $ to $-\infty $.
    
2. By **continuity**, $ f(\lambda)$ must have at least one root in this interval.
    
3. Because $ P_S(\lambda) = P_A(\lambda) f(\lambda)$, and in this interval $ P_A(\lambda) \neq 0 $, therefore the roots of $ f(\lambda)$ are the roots of $ P_S(\lambda)$, which are the eigenvalues $\lambda_{i+1}$ of $ S $.
    

### 4. Boundary Analysis

- When $\lambda \to -\infty $, $ f(\lambda) \to +\infty $, and to the left of the first pole $ f(\mu_1^-) \to -\infty $, so there must be a root $\lambda_1 $ in $(-\infty, \mu_1)$.
    
- When $\lambda \to +\infty $, $ f(\lambda) \to -\infty $, and to the right of the last pole $ f(\mu_{n-1}^+) \to +\infty $, so there must be a root $\lambda_n $ in $(\mu_{n-1}, +\infty)$.
    

### 5. Conclusion

Combining the zero distribution in all the above intervals, we obtain:

$$
\lambda_1 < \mu_1 < \lambda_2 < \mu_2 < \dots < \mu_{n-1} < \lambda_n
$$

This completely proves that the eigenvalues of $ A $ are strictly interlaced between the eigenvalues of $ S $.
