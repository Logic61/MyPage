Vandermonde's Mysterious Appearance.



## Independence Proof of Feature Subspaces

### 1. Definition of Direct Sum
Let $ V_1, V_2, \dots, V_s $ be distinct feature subspaces of the matrix $ A $, $\alpha_i \in V_i, 1 \le i \le s $.
If the following condition is met:

$$
\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \dots + \alpha_s = \mathbf{0}
$$

Then it must be true that:

$$
\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_s = \mathbf{0}
$$

> [!abstract] Conclusion
> This shows that the sum of $ V_1, V_2, \dots, V_s $ is a **direct sum**.

---

### 2. Proof Derivation Process
Let the eigenvalues corresponding to $ V_1, V_2, \dots, V_s $ be $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_s $ (all distinct).

#### Step 1: Constructing a System of Linear Equations
Using $ A\alpha_i = \lambda_i\alpha_i $, we repeatedly multiply the vector and equation by the matrix $ A $ from the left:

1. $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \dots + \alpha_s = \mathbf{0}$ 2.$\lambda_1\alpha_1 + \lambda_2\alpha_2 + \dots + \lambda_s\alpha_s = \mathbf{0}$ 3.$\lambda_1^2\alpha_1 + \lambda_2^2\alpha_2 + \dots + \lambda_s^2\alpha_s = \mathbf{0}$ 4.$\dots $ 5.$\lambda_1^{s-1}\alpha_1 + \lambda_2^{s-1}\alpha_2 + \dots + \lambda_s^{s-1}\alpha_s = \mathbf{0}$

#### Step 2: Matrix Representation
Writing the above system of equations in matrix multiplication form:

$$
[\alpha_1 \ \alpha_2 \ \cdots \ \alpha_s] 
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & \lambda_1 & \lambda_1^2 & \cdots & \lambda_1^{s-1} \\
1 & \lambda_2 & \lambda_2^2 & \cdots & \lambda_2^{s-1} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
1 & \lambda_s & \lambda_s^2 & \cdots & \lambda_s^{s-1}
\end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}
$$

#### Step 3: Using the Vandermonde Determinant (Vandermonde)
The matrix on the right side is a **Vandermonde matrix**. Since the eigenvalues $\lambda_i $ are distinct, the determinant of this matrix is not 0, so the **matrix is invertible**.

Multiply both sides of the equation by the inverse of this matrix simultaneously from the right:

$$
\Rightarrow \alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \dots = \alpha_s = \mathbf{0}
$$

**Thus, it is proven:** Eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent, and consequently, the basis formed by combining distinct feature subspaces remains linearly independent.

# Proof for Nilpotent Matrices

## Proposition
If the $ n $ level matrix $ A $ satisfies the condition:

$$
\text{tr}(A^k) = 0, \quad k = 1, 2, \dots, n
$$

Then $ A^n = 0 $.

---

## Proof Process

### 1. Using Schur's Lemma
Proof: By **Schur's Lemma**, over the field of complex numbers, $ A $ is similar to an upper triangular matrix:

$$
A \sim \begin{bmatrix} 
\lambda_1 & * & \dots & * \\
0 & \lambda_2 & & * \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \lambda_n 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Thus, $ A^k $ is similar to:

$$
A^k \sim \begin{bmatrix} 
\lambda_1^k & * & \dots & * \\
0 & \lambda_2^k & & * \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \dots & \lambda_n^k 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

### 2. Relationship between Trace and Eigenvalues
Therefore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{tr}(A) &= \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \dots + \lambda_n = 0 \\
\text{tr}(A^2) &= \lambda_1^2 + \lambda_2^2 + \dots + \lambda_n^2 = 0 \\
&\dots \dots \\
\text{tr}(A^k) &= \lambda_1^k + \lambda_2^k + \dots + \lambda_n^k = 0 
\end{aligned}
$$

### 3. Combining Identical Eigenvalues
Group the identical eigenvalues together. Without loss of generality, let $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_s $ be the distinct eigenvalues of $ A $ over the field of complex numbers, and let the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda_i $ be $ k_i $. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{tr}(A) &= k_1 \lambda_1 + k_2 \lambda_2 + \dots + k_s \lambda_s = 0 \\
\text{tr}(A^2) &= k_1 \lambda_1^2 + k_2 \lambda_2^2 + \dots + k_s \lambda_s^2 = 0 \\
&\dots \dots \\
\text{tr}(A^s) &= k_1 \lambda_1^s + k_2 \lambda_2^s + \dots + k_s \lambda_s^s = 0 
\end{aligned}
$$

### 4. Vandermonde Determinant (Vandermonde Matrix) Form
Writing it in matrix form:

$$
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 
1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\
\lambda_1 & \lambda_2 & \dots & \lambda_s \\
\lambda_1^2 & \lambda_2^2 & \dots & \lambda_s^2 \\
\vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\
\lambda_1^{s-1} & \lambda_2^{s-1} & \dots & \lambda_s^{s-1} 
\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{Vandermonde matrix (invertible)}}
\begin{bmatrix} 
k_1 \lambda_1 \\
k_2 \lambda_2 \\
\vdots \\
k_s \lambda_s 
\end{bmatrix} = 
\begin{bmatrix} 
0 \\
0 \\
\vdots \\
0 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Since the eigenvalues $\lambda_i $ are distinct, this Vandermonde matrix is **invertible**, so the unique solution is:

$$
k_i \lambda_i = 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, s
$$

Because the multiplicity is $ k_i \geq 1 $, it must be that $\lambda_i = 0 $.

### 5. Conclusion
Thus, all eigenvalues of $ A $ over the field of complex numbers are $ 0 $.
This means that $ A $ is similar to an upper triangular matrix $ B $ whose main diagonal elements are all $ 0 $:

$$
A \sim B = \begin{bmatrix} 
0 & * & \dots & * \\
0 & 0 & & \vdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & * \\
0 & 0 & \dots & 0 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

And for the $ n $ degree strictly upper triangular matrix, $ B^n = 0 $, so **$ A^n = 0 $**.

### Transition Matrix of Lagrange Interpolating Polynomials

Let $ c_1, \cdots, c_n \in K $ be distinct, and define for $ i=1, 2, \cdots, n $:

$$
h_i(x) = \frac{(x-c_1) \cdots (x-c_{i-1})(x-c_{i+1}) \cdots (x-c_n)}{(c_i-c_1) \cdots (c_i-c_{i-1})(c_i-c_{i+1}) \cdots (c_i-c_n)}
$$

**Proof:** The interpolating polynomial $ h_1(x), h_2(x), \cdots, h_n(x)$ forms a basis for the linear space $ V_n $.

and find the transition matrix from this basis to $ V_n $ the other basis $ 1, x, \cdots, x^{n-1}$ of the **transition matrix $ P $**.

---

### Proof Process

**1. Proving Linear Independence**

Consider the linear combination:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i h_i(x)
$$

Assume that $\{h_i(x)\}$ is linearly dependent, then there exists a non-zero $\lambda_i $ such that $ f(x) \equiv 0 $.

By the definition of $ h_i(x)$, it has the following property:

$$
h_i(c_j) = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1, & i=j \\ 0, & i \neq j \end{cases}
$$

Substituting $ x = c_j $ into $ f(x)$ gives:

$$
f(c_j) = \lambda_j h_j(c_j) = \lambda_j \cdot 1 = 0
$$

From this, for all $ j $, we have $\lambda_j = 0 $.

This contradicts the assumption, so $ h_1(x), \dots, h_n(x)$ is **linearly independent**.

**2. Conclusion**

Also, because $\dim V_n = n $, and we know there are $ n $ linearly independent polynomials, then $\{h_i(x)\}$ forms a **basis** for $ V_n $.

---

### Find the transition matrix $ P $

By the definition of the transition matrix, let:

$$
(1, x, \cdots, x^{n-1}) = (h_1(x), \cdots, h_n(x)) P
$$

To determine $ P $, we substitute the distinct nodes $ c_1, c_2, \cdots, c_n $ into the above formula:

- Substituting $ x = c_1 $:

$$
(1, c_1, \cdots, c_1^{n-1}) = (h_1(c_1), h_2(c_1), \cdots, h_n(c_1)) P = (1, 0, \cdots, 0) P
$$

From this, it is clear that $(1, c_1, \cdots, c_1^{n-1})$ is exactly the **1st row** of the matrix $ P $.
    
- Substituting $ x = c_i $:

$$
(1, c_i, \cdots, c_i^{n-1}) = (0, \cdots, 1, \cdots, 0) P
$$

(where $ 1 $ is in position $ i $)
    
    Similarly, this gives **row $ i $** of the matrix $ P $.
    

**By induction:**

$$
P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & c_1 & \cdots & c_1^{n-1} \\ 1 & c_2 & \cdots & c_2^{n-1} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & c_n & \cdots & c_n^{n-1} \end{pmatrix}
$$

This matrix is the **Vandermonde matrix**.

> **Note:** If we set $\mathbf{h} = \{h_i\}$ as basis 1 and $\mathbf{e} = \{x^{i-1}\}$ as basis 2. According to the transformation formula $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{h}P $, we have $ P = V $. Since the transition matrix from basis 1 to basis 2 is $ V $, the inverse transition matrix from basis 2 to basis 1 is $ V^{-1}$. This also explains the dual relationship between the Vandermonde matrix and Lagrange interpolation from a construction perspective.
