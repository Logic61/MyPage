First, a linear transformation is actually a special type of linear map where the domain and codomain are the same set, so our discussion about linear maps can continue.

### The Core Definition of a Linear Map

If we map a space $ U $ to another space $ V $, to call it a "linear map," it must adhere to two fundamental rules:

- **Additivity**: Mapping after addition is equivalent to addition after mapping, i.e., $\mathscr{A}(\alpha + \beta) = \mathscr{A}\alpha + \mathscr{A}\beta $.
    
- **Homogeneity**: Scaling the map is equivalent to scaling after mapping, i.e., $\mathscr{A}(k\alpha) = k\mathscr{A}\alpha $.
    
When these two spaces coincide (i.e., $ U = V $), we usually call it a **linear transformation**.
    

### The Image of the Basis Determines the Entire Map

For a map from $ K^n $ to $ K^m $, the essence of its operation is **matrix multiplication**.

- **The Role of the Standard Basis**: By observing where the standard basis vectors (vectors with a 1 in one position and 0 elsewhere) are mapped, we can arrange these results vertically to construct the **matrix $ A $**.
    
- **Operational Equivalence**: Performing a linear map on a vector $ x $ is computationally equivalent to executing $ Ax $.
    

Let $\mathscr{A}: \mathbf{K}^n \to \mathbf{K}^m $ be a linear map

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \color{green}{\begin{bmatrix} a_{11} \\ a_{21} \\ \vdots \\ a_{m1} \end{bmatrix}} , \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \color{green}{\begin{bmatrix} a_{12} \\ a_{22} \\ \vdots \\ a_{m2} \end{bmatrix}} , \dots, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \color{green}{\begin{bmatrix} a_{1n} \\ a_{2n} \\ \vdots \\ a_{mn} \end{bmatrix}}
$$

$$
\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{array}{|cccc|} \hline \color{green}{a_{11}} & \color{green}{a_{12}} & \dots & \color{green}{a_{1n}} \\ \color{green}{a_{21}} & \color{green}{a_{22}} & \dots & \color{green}{a_{2n}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ \color{green}{a_{m1}} & \color{green}{a_{m2}} & \dots & \color{green}{a_{mn}} \\ \hline \end{array} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

**The matrix of $\mathscr{A}$ in the standard basis is the coefficient matrix.**

This is a very powerful conclusion in linear algebra: **As long as the destination of the basis is determined, the entire map is completely fixed.**

- **Theorem Implication**: $ U $ If you are given a basis $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n\}$, $ V $ and arbitrarily specify their images $\{\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n\}$, $\mathscr{A}$ then there exists and is unique a linear map
    
- **Construction Logic**: 
    
    1. Since any vector $\alpha $ can be uniquely written as a linear combination of the basis $\alpha = \sum k_i \alpha_i $.
        
    2. We utilize the linear "additivity" and "homogeneity" to forcibly define the map's result as $\mathscr{A}\alpha = \sum k_i \beta_i $.
        
    3. This method of definition guarantees the **well-definedness** of the map (i.e., one input corresponds to a uniquely determined output).

Let's prove it:

#### Verifying that $\mathscr{A}$ is a linear map

To prove that the map $\mathscr{A}$ we constructed (i.e., the map defined by the combination of basis images) is a valid linear map, we need to verify that it satisfies additivity and homogeneity.

**Given Conditions:**

Let $\alpha = k_1 \alpha_1 + \dots + k_n \alpha_n \in U $ be such that $\alpha' = l_1 \alpha_1 + \dots + l_n \alpha_n \in U $, then:

**Verification Process:**

- **Additivity:**

$$
\begin{aligned} \mathscr{A} (\alpha + \alpha') &= (k_1 + l_1) \beta_1 + \dots + (k_n + l_n) \beta_n \\ &= (k_1 \beta_1 + \dots + k_n \beta_n) + (l_1 \beta_1 + \dots + l_n \beta_n) \\ &= \mathscr{A} \alpha + \mathscr{A} \alpha' \end{aligned}
$$

- **Homogeneity:**

$$
\mathscr{A} (l \alpha) = l k_1 \beta_1 + \dots + l k_n \beta_n = l \mathscr{A} \alpha
$$

---

#### The Uniqueness of $\mathscr{A}$

This part proves that: once the images of the basis $\{\beta_i\}$ are determined, there does not exist a second different linear map that achieves the same correspondence.

**Proof Process:**

If there is a linear map $\mathscr{B}: U \to V $, it also satisfies:

$$
\mathscr{B} \alpha_i = \beta_i, \quad \forall i
$$

Then, for the mapping result of any vector in the space:

$$
\begin{aligned} \mathscr{B} (k_1 \alpha_1 + \dots + k_n \alpha_n) &= k_1 \mathscr{B} \alpha_1 + \dots + k_n \mathscr{B} \alpha_n \\ &= k_1 \beta_1 + \dots + k_n \beta_n \\ &= \mathscr{A} (k_1 \alpha_1 + \dots + k_n \alpha_n), \quad \forall k_i \end{aligned}
$$

**Thus:**

$$
\mathscr{B} = \mathscr{A}
$$

## The operation of a linear map

### Linear Operations: Addition and Scalar Multiplication

If $\mathscr{A}, \mathscr{B}$ is a linear map from $ U $ to $ V $, then we can define:

- **Addition**: $(\mathscr{A} + \mathscr{B}): \alpha \mapsto \mathscr{A}\alpha + \mathscr{B}\alpha $
    
- **Scalar Multiplication**: $ k\mathscr{A}: \alpha \mapsto k(\mathscr{A}\alpha) \quad (k \in K)$
    

The results of these two operations are still linear maps.

> **Core Conclusion**: The set of all linear maps from $ U $ to $ V $ forms a linear space under the above operations, denoted as **$\text{Hom}(U, V)$**.

### Composition Operation: Multiplication of Maps

Let $\mathscr{B} \in \text{Hom}(U, V)$, $\mathscr{A} \in \text{Hom}(V, W)$.

- **Definition**: The composite map $\alpha \mapsto \mathscr{A}(\mathscr{B}\alpha)$ of $\mathscr{B}$ and $\mathscr{A}$ is a linear map from $ U $ to $ W $.
    
- **Notation**: This is called the product of $\mathscr{A}$ and $\mathscr{B}$, denoted as **$\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B}$**.
    
- **Prerequisite**: The codomain of $\mathscr{B}$ and the domain of $\mathscr{A}$ are the same.
    
- **Associativity**: $(\mathscr{A}\mathscr{B})\mathscr{C} = \mathscr{A}(\mathscr{B}\mathscr{C})$.

### The Algebraic Structure of Linear Transformations

When the map occurs within the same space, i.e., $\mathscr{A}, \mathscr{B} \in \text{Hom}(V)$, the structure becomes richer:

- **Linear transformations on $\text{Hom}(V)$** (or denoted as $\text{End}(V)$) can not only be added and scaled, but they can also be multiplied.
    
- This multiplication satisfies **associativity**, **distributivity** over addition, and has an identity element $\mathscr{I}$ (the identity transformation).
    

> **Core Conclusion**: $\text{Hom}(V)$ forms a **$ K $-algebra**.

## Evolution of Basis Change and Matrix Representation

This is the key step in linear algebra moving from 'static matrices' to 'dynamic transformations'.

#### Core Question

When the basis of $ U $ changes from $\{\alpha_i\}$ to $\{\alpha'_i\}$ (with the transition matrix $ P $), and the basis of $ V $ changes from $\{\beta_j\}$ to $\{\beta'_j\}$ (with the transition matrix $ Q $), how does the matrix of the map $\mathscr{A}$ change?

#### Change of Basis Theorem

If the matrix of $\mathscr{A}$ in the original basis is $ A $, then the **matrix in the new basis** is:

$$
\mathbf{B} = Q^{-1} A P
$$

#### Proof Derivation

1. **Relation 1** : $\mathscr{A}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\beta_1 \dots \beta_m)A $
    
2. **Basis Change** : $(\alpha'_1 \dots \alpha'_n) = (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n)P $; $(\beta'_1 \dots \beta'_m) = (\beta_1 \dots \beta_m)Q \implies (\beta_1 \dots \beta_m) = (\beta'_1 \dots \beta'_m)Q^{-1}$
    
3. **Substitution Calculation**:

$$
\begin{aligned} \mathscr{A}(\alpha'_1 \dots \alpha'_n) &= \mathscr{A}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n)P \\ &= (\beta_1 \dots \beta_m)AP \\ &= (\beta'_1 \dots \beta'_m)Q^{-1}AP \end{aligned}
$$

In the context of linear transformations, what naturally appears is what is called **similarity**, $ A=PQP^{-1}$. Similarity actually describes the same linear transformation expressed in different bases.

With the most beautiful choice, what is called the standard form appears, written in blocks as the identity matrix and zero.
