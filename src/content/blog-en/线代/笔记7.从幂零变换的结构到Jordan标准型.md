Previously, we decomposed the linear transformation into invariant subspaces and considered the restricted and induced maps on them. The decomposition given by the root subspaces gives the restricted map a very nice property, for the restriction on the root subspace $ Ker(A-\lambda I)^r $ of the map $ A $, we construct $ B=(A-\lambda I)|_W $ which has

$$
\mathcal{B}^r \alpha = ((\mathcal{A} - \lambda I)|_W)^r \alpha = (\mathcal{A} - \lambda I)^r \alpha
$$

where $\alpha \in Ker(A-\lambda I)^r $, and thus $ B^r=0 $. That is, it is a nilpotent matrix, so we only need to study nilpotent transformations to know the properties of general transformations.

# Nilpotent Endomorphism

Let $\mathbf{B} \in \text{Hom}(V)$. If there is a positive integer $ t $ such that $\mathbf{B}^t = 0 $, then $\mathbf{B}$ is called a **nilpotent transformation**. The smallest integer $ t $ such that $\mathbf{B}^t = 0 $ holds is called the **nilpotency index** of $\mathbf{B}$.

The minimal polynomial of a nilpotent transformation $\mathbf{B}$ is $ x^t $.

## Cyclic Subspace of a Nilpotent Transformation
For a nilpotent transformation, we still look for cyclic subspaces.

---

For any $\alpha \neq 0 $, there exists $ r \geq 1 $ such that $\mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha \neq 0, \mathbf{B}^r\alpha = 0 $.

Then

$$
Z(\alpha, \mathbf{B}) = \langle \alpha, \mathbf{B}\alpha, \mathbf{B}^2\alpha, \dots, \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha \rangle
$$

is a $\mathbf{B}$-subspace, called a **strong cyclic subspace** generated by $\alpha $.

$$
\alpha, \mathbf{B}\alpha, \dots, \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha
$$

is called a **cyclic basis** for $ Z(\alpha, \mathbf{B})$, and $\mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha $ is called the **tail element** of the cyclic basis, which plays a crucial role in the following proof. We can prove that this forms a basis by considering the linear combination

$$
\sum_i c_iB^i\alpha=0
$$

When we apply $ B^{r-1}$, only $ c_0B^{r-1}\alpha=0 $ remains, so it can only be $ c_0=0 $. By the same method, we get all coefficients to be $ 0 $. 

---

If $ Z(\alpha, \mathbf{B}) = \langle \alpha, \mathbf{B}\alpha, \mathbf{B}^2\alpha, \dots, \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha \rangle $ is a $ r $-dimensional $\mathbf{B}$-strong cyclic subspace, then

$$
\mathbf{B}(\alpha \ \mathbf{B}\alpha \ \mathbf{B}^2\alpha \dots \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha)
$$

$$
= (\alpha \ \mathbf{B}\alpha \ \mathbf{B}^2\alpha \dots \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha) \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

(The matrix is in **lower triangular** form, and the basis vectors are arranged in the original order)

---

If $ Z(\alpha, \mathbf{B}) = \langle \alpha, \mathbf{B}\alpha, \mathbf{B}^2\alpha, \dots, \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha \rangle $ is a $ r $-dimensional $\mathbf{B}$-strong cyclic subspace, then

$$
\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha \ \mathbf{B}^{r-2}\alpha \dots \alpha)
$$

$$
= (\mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha \ \mathbf{B}^{r-2}\alpha \dots \alpha) \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \ddots & \vdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

(The matrix is in **upper triangular** form, and the cyclic basis is arranged in **descending order**)

That is, the invariant subspace must continue from some power up to $ r-1 $, with no gaps in between.

Therefore, a $ r $-dimensional (strong) cyclic subspace $ Z(\alpha, \mathbf{B}) = \langle \alpha \ \mathbf{B}\alpha \dots \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha \rangle $ has only $ r+1 $ $\mathbf{B}$-invariant subspaces:

$$
Z(\alpha, \mathbf{B}) \supset Z(\mathbf{B}\alpha, \mathbf{B}) \supset Z(\mathbf{B}^2\alpha, \mathbf{B}) \supset \dots \supset Z(\mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha, \mathbf{B}) \supset \{0\}
$$

These cyclic bases all have **the same tail element**.
Next, we want to get the original transformation, which requires piecing together individual subspaces, so naturally, we think of when it is a direct sum.

### Condition for the Direct Sum of Cyclic Subspaces

Given $\alpha, \beta \in W $, how can we guarantee

$$
Z(\alpha, \mathbf{B}) + Z(\beta, \mathbf{B}) \text{ is a direct sum?}
$$

That is: how to guarantee that the set of vectors

$$
\alpha, \mathbf{B}\alpha, \mathbf{B}^2\alpha, \dots, \mathbf{B}^{r-1}\alpha
$$

$$
\beta, \mathbf{B}\beta, \dots, \mathbf{B}^{s-1}\beta
$$

**is linearly independent**?

---

### Lemma: A necessary and sufficient condition for the sum of strong cyclic subspaces to be a direct sum

**Conclusion:** The sum of strong cyclic subspaces is a direct sum **if and only if the tail elements of the cyclic bases are linearly independent**.

### The property of the cyclic basis of dimension $ k $ and its tail element

For a cyclic basis of dimension $ k $:

$$
\alpha, \mathbf{B}\alpha, \dots, \mathbf{B}^{k-1}\alpha \quad (\mathbf{B}^k \alpha = 0)
$$

Its **tail element** $\mathbf{B}^{k-1}\alpha $ has a dual identity:

1. **Belongs to the image space:** $\mathbf{B}^{k-1}\alpha \in \text{Im} \mathbf{B}^{k-1}$ (it is the result of applying the operator $ k-1 $ times to some vector).
    
2. **Belonging to the kernel space:** $\mathbf{B}^{k-1}\alpha \in \text{Ker} \mathbf{B}$(Applying it again $\mathbf{B}$ will result in zero).
    

Therefore, the tail element lies in the intersection of these two spaces:

$$
\mathbf{B}^{k-1}\alpha \in \text{Im} \mathbf{B}^{k-1} \cap \text{Ker} \mathbf{B}
$$

---

### The nested structure of the sequence of spaces

Let $ t $ be the **nilpotency index** of the operator $\mathbf{B}$ (i.e., $\mathbf{B}^t = 0 $ and $\mathbf{B}^{t-1} \neq 0 $).

We can define a series of subspaces $ W_k $, which characterize the range where the tail elements of cyclic bases of different “lengths” are located:

$$
W_k = \text{Im} \mathbf{B}^{k-1} \cap \text{Ker} \mathbf{B}
$$

These subspaces form an ascending **nested chain** (Filtration):

$$
W_t = \text{Im} \mathbf{B}^{t-1} \subseteq W_{t-1} \subseteq \dots \subseteq W_2 \subseteq W_1 = \text{Ker} \mathbf{B}
$$

At the same time, using what we proved in the quotient space

$$
\text{Im}\mathcal{A} \big/ (\text{Im}\mathcal{A} \cap \text{Ker}\mathcal{B}) \cong \text{Im}(\mathcal{BA})
$$

When we substitute $ A=B^{k-1}$, we get

$$
dimImB^{k-1}-dim(ImB^{k-1}\cap KerB)=dimImB^k
$$

Which is $ dimW^k=dimImB^{k-1}-dimImB^k $. This gives the dimension formula.
## Space decomposition of nilpotent transformations

We can use a nilpotent transformation to decompose the space into cyclic subspaces.

To extend the basis, we start with the smallest subspace

$$
W_k = \text{Im}\mathcal{B}^{k-1} \cap \text{Ker}\mathcal{B}
$$

Among them, we take a set of bases $\eta_{1}^{(t)}, \dots, \eta_{m_t}^{(t)}$, and then we expand to the next level, by adding $\eta_{1}^{(t-1)}, \dots $ to $ W_{t-1}$; ... until $ W_1 $. **At this point, the set of all $\eta $ formed is a basis for $\text{Ker}\mathcal{B}$, and they are linearly independent.**

For every $\eta \in W_k $, there can be found a corresponding $\alpha $ satisfying $\mathcal{B}^{k-1}\alpha = \eta $. We collect all the cyclic chains generated by these $\alpha $. Since we specifically selected $\eta $ using the method of "basis expansion," the set of tail terms of all chains is linearly independent, and therefore the sum of these cyclic subspaces must be a **direct sum**.


> Figure to be added.

 We need to determine whether these direct sums fill the entire $ V $.

Calculating the dimension of the direct sum space:

$$
\sum (\text{the length of each chain}) = \sum_{k=1}^t k \times (\text{is exactly } k \text{ the number of chains of length})
$$

A chain of length $ k $ corresponds to a sequence in $ W_k $. What does $ dimW_t $ mean? Let's see:

- **The vectors chosen in $ W_t $:** They can be traced back $ t-1 $ steps (because in $\text{Im}\mathcal{B}^{t-1}$), so they generate the **longest chain** of length $ t $.
    
- **The vectors in $ W_{t-1}$ but not in $ W_t $:** They can only be traced back $ t-2 $ steps. They generate a chain of length $ t-1 $. $\dim W_k $ is actually the number of "cyclic chains of length $\ge k $." This reminds us of the expectation formula for a random variable of a positive integer

$$
E(X) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} P(X \ge k)
$$

Then when we calculate the sum of all $\dim W_k $ in the same way:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^t \dim W_k = \dim W_1 + \dim W_2 + \dots + \dim W_t
$$

- The chain of length $ 1 $ is counted **1 time** in $\dim W_1 $.
    
- The chain of length $ 2 $ is counted 1 time each in $\dim W_1 $ and $\dim W_2 $, totaling **2 times**.
    
- ...
    
- The chain of length $ k $ is counted 1 time each in $\dim W_1, \dots, \dim W_k $, totaling **$ k $ times**.

So,
by the dimension calculation formula:

$$
\text{the total dimension} = \sum_{k=1}^t \dim W_k = \sum_{k=1}^t (\text{rank}(\mathcal{B}^{k-1}) - \text{rank}(\mathcal{B}^k))
$$

We also get:

$$
\text{the total dimension} = \text{rank}(\mathcal{B}^0) - \text{rank}(\mathcal{B}^t) = \dim V - 0 = \dim V
$$

## Existence

Let V be a finite-dimensional K-linear space, and . If the characteristic polynomial of  can be factored into a product of linear factors over the field K, then there exists a basis for V such that the matrix of  in this basis is a Jordan form matrix.

Proof:

Decompose $ V $ into

$$
V=\bigoplus_i Ker(A-\lambda_i I)^{r_i}
$$

Then consider the restriction map $ A|_{V_i}=\lambda_i I|_{V_i}+B_i $ on $ V_i=Ker(A-\lambda_i I)^{r_i}$, so $ B_i $ is a nilpotent transformation on $ V_i $. From above, we know that $ V_i $ can be decomposed into a direct sum of $ B_i-$ subspaces.

$$
V_i=\bigoplus_j Z(\alpha_j,B_i)
$$

For $ V_{ij}=Z(\alpha_j,B_i)$, it is an invariant subspace of $ V_i $, and naturally also an invariant subspace of $ A-$. Moreover, the matrix of $ A $ in this basis is a Jordan block. Finally, piecing together all $ V_{ij}$ gives the direct sum decomposition of the entire space, and the corresponding $ A $ is the Jordan form matrix.

## Uniqueness

A Jordan matrix similar to $ A $ is uniquely determined by $ A $, up to the order of the diagonal blocks, and is called the Jordan canonical form of $ A $.

First, reordering the diagonal blocks is equivalent to multiplying by a permutation matrix, which is still similar.

#### Number of Jordan blocks

$$
\text{rank}(J - \lambda I)^{k-1} - 2 \times \text{rank}(J - \lambda I)^k + \text{rank}(J - \lambda I)^{k+1}
$$

(When $ k=1 $, take $(J - \lambda I)^{k-1}$ to be the identity matrix $ I $.)

This has been elaborated in detail before; intuitively, it means

How large $ J_0 $ is determines how many times the power can be sustained, thus differencing gives the number of $ J_0 $ greater than or equal to $ k $, and differencing again gives the number equal to $ k $.

This actually gives the uniqueness directly. Consider $J$ similar to $ A $,

$$
$ UAU^{-1}=J
$$

Using the classic conclusion

$$
UA^kU^{-1}=J^k
$$

The polynomial is also similar, which means

$$
U(A-\lambda I)^kU^{-1}=(J-\lambda I)^k
$$

Then there is

$$
rank(A-\lambda I)^k=rank(J-\lambda I)^k
$$

Thus, using the conclusion about the size of the Jordan blocks, we know that all Jordan blocks similar to $ A $ have the same size. Using the fact that similarity implies the same eigenvalues, the Jordan canonical form is determined.

### Corollary

A matrix is similar if and only if its Jordan canonical form is the same (up to reordering).
