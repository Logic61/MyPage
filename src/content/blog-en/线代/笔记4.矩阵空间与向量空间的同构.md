For a vector, we can view it as $ n $ coordinates, constructing an isomorphism from the vector to $ n $ coordinates. A matrix is actually a stack of vectors, so similarly, we can view it as $ n $ vectors and construct an isomorphism.

## Matrix Vectorization

- **Column Stacking $\text{cs}(A)$**: Arranging the elements of the $ m \times n $ matrix $ A $ from left to right, column by column, into a $ mn $ dimensional column vector.
    
    - **Example**: If $ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, then $\text{cs}(A) = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]^T $.
        
- **Row Stacking $\text{rs}(A)$**: Similarly, arranging the matrix in row order into a row vector.
    
    - **Example**: For the above matrix, $ rs(A) = [1, 4, 2, 5, 3, 6]$.
        

This is clearly an isomorphism, meaning we can completely view a matrix as a super-vector.

## Kronecker Product (Kronecker Product)

### Definition

Let matrix $ A = [a_{ij}] \in M_{m,n}(K)$ and matrix $ B \in M_{p,q}(K)$. Their Kronecker product (also called the tensor product) is denoted by $ A \otimes B $, which is a block matrix of size $ mp \times nq $:

$$
A \otimes B = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11}B & \dots & a_{1n}B \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{m1}B & \dots & a_{mn}B \end{pmatrix}
$$

That is, the block matrix obtained by multiplying each element $ a_{ij}$ of $ A $ by the matrix $ B $.

### Key Properties

- **Non-commutativity**: In general, $ A \otimes B \neq B \otimes A $.
    
- **Associativity**: $(A \otimes B) \otimes C = A \otimes (B \otimes C)$.
    
- **Special Case**: When $ A $ is a column vector and $ C $ is a row vector (or vice versa), it may satisfy $ A \otimes C = C \otimes A = AC $ in specific dimensions.
    

---

## Operation Example

### Basic Calculation

If $ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ and $ B = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, then:

$$
A \otimes B = \begin{pmatrix} B & 2B & 3B \\ 4B & 5B & 6B \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b & 2a & 2b & 3a & 3b \\ c & d & 2c & 2d & 3c & 3d \\ 4a & 4b & 5a & 5b & 6a & 6b \\ 4c & 4d & 5c & 5d & 6c & 6d \end{pmatrix}
$$

### Product between vectors

If $ A = [1, 2, 3]^T $ and $ B = [a, b]^T $, then:

- $ A \otimes B = [a, b, 2a, 2b, 3a, 3b]^T $-$ B \otimes A = [a, 2a, 3a, b, 2b, 3b]^T $
    
    (This intuitively shows the difference in results due to different orders)

## 4. Mixed-Product Property

This is one of the most important properties of the Kronecker product, connecting ordinary matrix multiplication and the tensor product:

$$
(A \otimes B)(C \otimes D) = (AC) \otimes (BD)
$$

- **Prerequisites**: Matrix multiplications $ AC $ and $ BD $ must be meaningful (i.e., the number of columns of $ A $ must equal the number of rows of $ C $, and the number of columns of $ B $ must equal the number of rows of $ D $).
    
- **Derivation Logic**: It can be proven through the rules of block matrix multiplication that the sub-block term $\sum a_{ik} c_{kj} B D $ on the left side corresponds exactly to the construction of $(AC) \otimes (BD)$ on the right side.

## Core Properties of the Kronecker Product

- **Associativity**: $(A \otimes B) \otimes C = A \otimes (B \otimes C)$
    
- **Mixed-Product Property**: $(A \otimes B)(C \otimes D) = AC \otimes BD $
    
- **Inverse Matrix**: $(A \otimes B)^{-1} = A^{-1} \otimes B^{-1}$
    
- **Transpose**: $(A \otimes B)^T = A^T \otimes B^T $ - **Orthogonality Preservation**: If $ A, B $ is an orthogonal matrix, then $ A \otimes B $ is also an orthogonal matrix.
    

---

## Algebraic Characteristics (Rank, Determinant, Eigenvalues)

1. **Rank**: $\text{rank}(A \otimes B) = \text{rank}(A) \cdot \text{rank}(B)$ 2. **Determinant**: Let $ A $ be a square matrix of order $ m $, and $ B $ be a square matrix of order $ n $, then $|A \otimes B| = |A|^n |B|^m $
    
3. **Eigenvalues**: 
    
    - Let the eigenvalues of $ A $ be $\lambda_i $, and the eigenvalues of $ B $ be $\mu_j $.
        
    - The eigenvalues of $ A \otimes B $ are $\lambda_i \mu_j $.
        
    - **Kronecker Product**: The eigenvalues of $ A \otimes I_n + I_m \otimes B $ are $\lambda_i + \mu_j $.

## Special Instance: Construction of Hadamard Matrices

Using the Kronecker product, we can recursively construct special orthogonal matrices (such as Hadamard matrices):

- $ A_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$-$ A_4 = A_2 \otimes A_2 = \frac{1}{2} \begin{pmatrix} A_2 & A_2 \\ A_2 & -A_2 \end{pmatrix}$-$ A_8 = A_2 \otimes A_4 $ ... and so on.

## Matrix Representation of Linear Transformation

Let $\mathcal{A}: X \mapsto AXB $ be a linear transformation on the matrix space $ M_{m,n}(K)$:

- In the standard basis $ E_{1,1}, E_{2,1}, \dots, E_{m,n}$ (i.e., ordered by column stacking), the matrix corresponding to this linear transformation is exactly **$ B^T \otimes A $**.
    
- **Intuitive Understanding**: 
    
    - $ A $ acting on $ X $ the **row transformation**.
        
    - $ B $ acting on $ X $ the **column transformation**.

---

## Vectorization of Linear Matrix Equations

Based on the column expansion property from the previous section, complex matrix equations can be transformed into the standard form of a linear system of equations.

- **Corollary**: $ X \in M_{m,n}(K)$ is a solution to the matrix equation $\sum_{j=1}^{s} A_j X B_j = C $ **if and only if** its column expansion $\text{cs}(X)$ satisfies:

$$
\left( \sum_{j=1}^{s} B_j^T \otimes A_j \right) \text{cs}(X) = \text{cs}(C)
$$

where $ A_j, B_j $ is a square matrix of order $ m, n $, $ C \in M_{m,n}(K)$.
    
- **The idea.** The Kronecker product turns the operator acting on matrices into an ordinary matrix. We can then solve the matrix equation using standard linear algebra tools, such as Gaussian elimination.

## Tensor Product Space

elevating the tensor product from matrix operations to the construction of linear spaces.

- **Definition**: Let $ U = R^m, V = R^n $ be two linear spaces, the set

$$
T = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \alpha_i \otimes \beta_i \mid \alpha_i \in U, \beta_i \in V, k \geq 1 \right\}
$$

forms a linear space, called the **tensor product space** of $ U $ and $ V $, denoted by $ U \otimes V $.
    
- **Basis and Dimension**: 
    
    - If $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m $ is a basis for $ U $, and $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ is a basis for $ V $.
        
    - then all possible combinations $\{\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j\}$ form a basis for $ U \otimes V $.
        
    - **Conclusion**: $ U \otimes V \cong R^{mn}$, that is, the dimension of the tensor product space is the product of the dimensions of the original spaces.
        

---

## Tensor Product of Linear Transformations

This is the generalization of the tensor product to the level of operators.

- **Definition**: Let $\mathcal{A} \in \text{Hom}(U), \mathcal{B} \in \text{Hom}(V)$, define the linear transformation $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}: U \otimes V \to U \otimes V $ as:

$$
\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B} (\alpha \otimes \beta) = (\mathcal{A}\alpha) \otimes (\mathcal{B}\beta)
$$

This transformation is called the tensor product of the transformations $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$.
    
- **Matrix Representation**: 
    
    If the matrix of $\mathcal{A}$ in the basis $\{\alpha_i\}$ is $ A $, and the matrix of $\mathcal{B}$ in the basis $\{\beta_j\}$ is $ B $, then the matrix of the linear transformation $\mathcal{A} \otimes \mathcal{B}$ in the basis $ U \otimes V $ of $\{\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j\}$ is exactly **$ A \otimes B $**.

These things can actually simplify the structure, allowing us to see the matrix more clearly. For example, we can look at some problems:

---

### Problem: Proof of the property of the solution space of a matrix equation

Let $ A, B $ and $\text{rank}(A) = r $ be square matrices of order $ m, n $, and $\text{rank}(B) = s $. Prove that:

$$
W = \{ X \in M_{m,n}(K) \mid AXB = 0 \}
$$

is a subspace of the matrix space $ M_{m,n}(K)$, and $\dim W = mn - rs $.

---

### 1. Prove that $ W $ is a subspace

According to the definition of a subspace, we need to verify closure under addition and scalar multiplication:

- **Closure under Addition**: Let $ X, Y \in W $, then $ AXB = 0 $ and $ AYB = 0 $.

$$
A(X + Y)B = AXB + AYB = 0 + 0 = 0
$$

Thus, $ X + Y \in W $.
    
- **Closure under Scalar Multiplication**: Let $ X \in W, k \in K $, then:

$$
A(kX)B = k(AXB) = k \cdot 0 = 0
$$

Thus, $ kX \in W $.
    
    From this, we know that $ W $ is a subspace of $ M_{m,n}(K)$.
    

---

### 2. Prove $\dim W = mn - rs $

Here, we used the properties of **Vectorization** and the **Kronecker Product** to transform the matrix problem into a linear system of equations.

#### (1) Equation Transformation

Using the identity $\text{cs}(AXB) = (B^T \otimes A) \text{cs}(X)$, the original matrix equation $ AXB = 0 $ is equivalent to the linear system:

$$
(B^T \otimes A) \cdot \text{cs}(X) = \mathbf{0}
$$

where $\text{cs}(X)$ is a column vector of dimension $ mn $, the coefficient matrix is $ M = B^T \otimes A $, with size $ mn \times mn $.

#### (2) Determining the Rank of the Coefficient Matrix

According to the property of the rank of the Kronecker product:

$$
\text{rank}(B^T \otimes A) = \text{rank}(B^T) \cdot \text{rank}(A)
$$

Since the transpose of a matrix does not change the rank, and given $\text{rank}(A) = r, \text{rank}(B) = s $, we have:

$$
\text{rank}(B^T \otimes A) = s \cdot r = rs
$$

#### (3) Using the Dimension Formula

The dimension of the subspace $ W $ is equivalent to the dimension of the solution space of the above homogeneous linear system. According to the dimension formula for the solution space of a linear system (Dimension of the $ n $ space minus the rank of the coefficient matrix):

$$
\dim W = \dim (\text{Solution Space}) = mn - \text{rank}(B^T \otimes A) = mn - rs
$$

**Done.**

### Problem: Eigenvalue and Diagonalization Proof of the Matrix Transformation $\mathcal{S}$

Let $ A, B $ be $ m, n $ complex matrices of order, whose eigenvalues are $\{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m\}$ and $\{\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n\}$.

define the mapping $\mathcal{S}: M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C}) \to M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})$:

$$
\mathcal{S}(X) = AX - XB + AXB^2
$$

---

### 1. Prove that $\mathcal{S}$ is a linear transformation

According to the definition of a linear transformation, it must satisfy homogeneity for addition and scalar multiplication:

- **Linearity Verification**: Using the distributive and associative laws of matrix multiplication:

$$
\mathcal{S}(k_1 X + k_2 Y) = A(k_1 X + k_2 Y) - (k_1 X + k_2 Y)B + A(k_1 X + k_2 Y)B^2
$$

$$
= k_1(AX - XB + AXB^2) + k_2(AY - YB + AYB^2) = k_1 \mathcal{S}(X) + k_2 \mathcal{S}(Y)
$$

Thus, $\mathcal{S}$ is a linear transformation on $ M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})$.
    

---

### 2. Find all eigenvalues of the transformation $\mathcal{S}$

Using **Vectorization**, we convert $\mathcal{S}(X)$ into vector form:

$$
\text{cs}(\mathcal{S}(X)) = \text{cs}(AXI_n - I_m XB + AXB^2)
$$

According to the formula $\text{cs}(AXB) = (B^T \otimes A) \text{cs}(X)$, we extract $\text{cs}(X)$ to get the transformation matrix $ M $:

$$
M = (I_n \otimes A) - (B^T \otimes I_m) + ((B^2)^T \otimes A)
$$

- **Eigenvalue Calculation**: 
    
    Given that the eigenvalues of $ A $ are $\lambda_i $, and the eigenvalues of $ B $ (and $ B^T $) are $\mu_j $.
    
Based on the properties of the Kronecker product, the corresponding eigenvalues are:
    
    - The eigenvalues of $ I_n \otimes A $, the eigenvalues of $ 1 \cdot \lambda_i = \lambda_i $-$ B^T \otimes I_m $, the eigenvalues of $\mu_j \cdot 1 = \mu_j $-$(B^T)^2 \otimes A $, and the eigenvalues of $\mu_j^2 \cdot \lambda_i $. Since these terms can all be simultaneously diagonalized in the same basis (the tensor product basis formed by the eigenvectors of $ A $ and $ B^T $), the eigenvalues of the transformation $\mathcal{S}$ are:

$$
\lambda'_{ij} = \lambda_i - \mu_j + \lambda_i \mu_j^2, \quad (i=1,\dots,m; \ j=1,\dots,n)
$$

---

### 3. Diagonalization Proof and Eigenvectors

**Proof:**

1. **Prerequisites**: It is known that $ A $ and $ B $ are diagonalizable. This means that $ A $ has $ m $ linearly independent eigenvectors $\alpha_i $, and $ B^T $ has $ n $ linearly independent eigenvectors $\gamma_j $ (note that if $ B $ is diagonalizable, then $ B^T $ is also diagonalizable, and they have the same eigenvalues).
    
2. **Constructing the Eigenvectors of the Transformation $\mathcal{S}$**:
    
    In the tensor product space $ M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})$ the "matrix form" corresponding to the eigenvector of the transformation is: $\mathcal{S}$ More rigorously, if

$$
X_{ij} = \alpha_i \beta_j^H \quad (\text{or more simply denoted as } \alpha_i \text{ with } B \text{ the product of the left eigenvector})
$$

,
and $ A\alpha_i = \lambda_i \alpha_i $is $\beta_j $ is $ B $ eigenvector of the matrix (satisfying $ B\beta_j = \mu_j \beta_j $ is not intuitive, here it is usually seen as $ X \beta_j $ the mapping relationship of).
    
3. **Conclusion**:
    
    Since we can construct $ m \times n $ a form of $ E_{ij} = \alpha_i \otimes \gamma_j $ linearly independent eigenvectors, and the space $ M_{m,n}(\mathbb{C})$ dimension is exactly $ mn $, the set of eigenvectors forms a basis for the space.
    
    **Therefore**, $\mathcal{S}$ is diagonalizable.**
