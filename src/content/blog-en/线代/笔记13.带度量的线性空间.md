## 1. Why extend to complex space? — Motivation and Intuition

  

### 1.1 A seemingly simple problem

  

Students, in the previous chapter we studied Euclidean space—a real linear space equipped with an inner product. The inner product gave us geometric concepts like length, angle, and orthogonality; everything was beautiful.

  

But now we enter the domain of complex numbers. The natural question is: **How do we define the length of a vector in a complex linear space?**

  

The most direct idea is: just copy the formula from the real space. For $\mathbf{z} = (z_1, \dots, z_n)^T \in \mathbb{C}^n $, define $||\mathbf{z}||^2 = z_1^2 + z_2^2 + \cdots + z_n^2 $.

  

**But this immediately runs into trouble.** Consider the vector $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{bmatrix}$; its "squared length" is $ 1^2 + i^2 = 1 - 1 = 0 $. A non-zero vector having a length of 0? This completely violates the basic intuition of length.

  

### 1.2 The correct direction

  

Where is the problem? It's because we forgot how the "modulus" of a complex number is defined. The squared modulus of a complex number $ z $ is $|z|^2 = z\bar{z}$, **not** $ z^2 $.

  

Therefore, the length of a complex vector should be defined as:

$$
||\mathbf{z}||^2 = z_1\bar{z}_1 + z_2\bar{z}_2 + \cdots + z_n\bar{z}_n = \mathbf{z}^T\bar{\mathbf{z}}
$$

> **Instructor's Comment**: This step seems trivial, but it is the starting point for the entire chapter. Starting from here, we will find that the "inner product" on a complex space cannot be bilinear, but must be **sesquilinear**. This is the standard form of the inner product in complex analysis and quantum mechanics. Understanding this will be very helpful for your subsequent study of quantum mechanics—the inner product of a quantum state $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle $ is sesquilinear.

## 2. Sesquilinear Forms and Metric Matrices

  

### 2.1 Definition

  

**Definition (Sesquilinear Form)**: On a complex linear space $ V $, a bilinear form $ f(\alpha, \beta)$ is called a **sesquilinear form** if it satisfies:

$$
\begin{aligned}

f(k\alpha + l\beta, \gamma) &= k f(\alpha, \gamma) + l f(\beta, \gamma) &\quad &\text{(linear in the first variable)} \\

f(\gamma, k\alpha + l\beta) &= \bar{k} f(\gamma, \alpha) + \bar{l} f(\gamma, \beta) &\quad &\text{(conjugate linear in the second variable)}

\end{aligned}
$$

For any $ k, l \in \mathbb{C}$.

  

> **Note**: There is a convention issue here. The math world and the physics world have opposite conventions regarding "which variable is conjugate linear." Mathematics usually assumes the second variable is conjugate linear (as above), while physics (Dirac notation) usually assumes the first variable is conjugate linear. Both conventions are essentially equivalent, but mixing them will lead to sign errors, so consistency is crucial.

  

### 2.2 Metric Tensor: Coordinate Representation

  

It is completely analogous to the symmetric bilinear form of the previous chapter; after choosing a basis, the sesquilinear form can also be represented by a matrix.

  

Let the coordinates of $\alpha, \beta $ in the basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ be $ X = (x_1, \dots, x_n)^T $, $ Y = (y_1, \dots, y_n)^T $. By expanding the sesquilinear form sequentially:

$$
\begin{aligned}

f(\alpha, \beta) &= f\left(\sum_i x_i \alpha_i,\ \sum_j y_j \alpha_j\right) \\

&= \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n x_i \bar{y}_j \cdot f(\alpha_i, \alpha_j) \\

&= X^T A \bar{Y}

\end{aligned}
$$

Where $ A = [f(\alpha_i, \alpha_j)]_{n \times n}$ is called the **metric tensor** of $ f $ in this basis.

  

> **Instructor's Explanation**: Note that the formula here is $ X^T A \bar{Y}$, not the one from the previous chapter, $ X^T A Y $. The difference lies in $\bar{Y}$—because the second variable is conjugate linear, the coordinates must be conjugated. This detail appears repeatedly in subsequent derivations; you must develop it into a reflex action.

  

**Conclusion**: After fixing a basis, the sesquilinear form corresponds one-to-one with a complex matrix (the metric tensor).

  

### 2.3 Basis Change Formula

  

**Theorem**: If the metric tensor of $ f $ in the basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ is $ A $, and the new basis is $(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n) = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)P $, then $ f $ in the new basis is given by:

$$
P^T A \bar{P}
$$

**Proof**: Let the coordinates of the vector in the basis $\{\beta\}$ be $ X_i, Y_i $, then in the basis $\{\alpha\}$, the coordinates are $ PX_i, PY_i $. Thus:

$$
f(\cdot, \cdot) = (PX_i)^T A \overline{(PY_i)} = X_i^T (P^T A \bar{P}) \bar{Y}_i
$$

The new metric tensor is $ P^T A \bar{P}$. $\square $> **Comparison**: The basis change formula for the real symmetric bilinear form from the previous chapter is $ P^T A P $. Here it becomes $ P^T A \bar{P}$, with an extra conjugate. If $ P $ happens to be a real matrix, the two formulas are the same.

## 3. Hermitian Inner Product and Hermitian Positive Definite Matrices

  

### 3.1 From Sesquilinear Form to Inner Product

  

Not every sesquilinear form can be used as an "inner product." We need two additional conditions:

  

**Definition (Hermitian Inner Product)**: If a sesquilinear form $(\cdot, \cdot)$ satisfies:

  

1. **Conjugate Symmetry**: $(\alpha, \beta) = \overline{(\beta, \alpha)}$, $\forall \alpha, \beta $

2. **Positive Definite**: $(\alpha, \alpha) > 0 $, $\forall \alpha \neq 0 $, then $(\cdot, \cdot)$ is called a **complex (Hermitian) inner product**.

  

> **Instructor's Comment**: Condition 1 looks a bit strange—why not require $(\alpha, \beta) = (\beta, \alpha)$? Because if it is sesquilinear, forcing symmetry would lead to $(\alpha, \alpha)$ not necessarily being a real number, and then we couldn't define a length. Conjugate symmetry precisely guarantees that $(\alpha, \alpha) = \overline{(\alpha, \alpha)}$, i.e., $(\alpha, \alpha)$, is a real number. Combined with positive definiteness, $||\alpha|| = \sqrt{(\alpha, \alpha)}$ makes sense.

  

### 3.2 Hermitian Matrix

  

**Definition**: A complex matrix $ A $ is called a **Hermitian matrix** if $ A^H := \bar{A}^T = A $ (which is $ a_{ij} = \bar{a}_{ji}$).

  

If the Hermitian matrix $ A $ also satisfies $\bar{X}^T A X > 0 $ for all $ X \neq 0 $, then $ A $ is called a **Hermitian positive definite matrix**.

  

> **Instructor's Explanation**: A Hermitian matrix is the natural generalization of a real symmetric matrix to the complex domain. A real symmetric matrix satisfies $ A^T = A $, and a Hermitian matrix satisfies $ A^H = A $. When you replace "transpose" with "conjugate transpose," most conclusions from the theory of real symmetric matrices can be directly extended. This is the most important **mindset** in this chapter.

  

### 3.3 Hermitian Notation

  

Introduce the notation $ A^H := \bar{A}^T $ (conjugate transpose, also called Hermitian transpose). Basic properties:

  

- $(A^H)^H = A $-$(AB)^H = B^H A^H $ (**Note the reversal of order**, and similarly for $(AB)^T = B^T A^T $)

  

### 3.4 Inner Product $\iff $ Positive Definite Matrix

  

**Proposition**: Conjugate bilinear function $ f $ is a complex inner product $\iff $ $ f $ the metric matrix in any basis $ A $ is a Hermitian positive definite matrix.

  

**Proof**: Let the coordinates of $\alpha, \beta $ be $ X, Y $.

  

**Conjugate Symmetric $\iff $ Hermitian**:

$$
(\alpha, \beta) = \overline{(\beta, \alpha)} \iff X^T A \bar{Y} = \overline{Y^T A \bar{X}} = \bar{Y}^T \bar{A} X = X^T \bar{A}^T \bar{Y} \iff A = \bar{A}^T = A^H
$$

**Positive Definiteness**: $(\alpha, \alpha) > 0 \iff \bar{X}^T A X > 0 $ for all $ X \neq 0 $. $\square $

  

> **Instructor's Comments**: This equivalence is extremely important. It means that: **studying the problem of complex inner products can be completely transformed into studying the problem of Hermitian positive definite matrices**. ## 4. Cholesky Decomposition: The "Square Root" of a Positive Definite Matrix

  

### 4.1 Equivalent Characterizations of Hermitian Positive Definite Matrices

  

**Theorem**: Let $ A $ be a Hermitian matrix, then the following are equivalent:

  

1. $ A $ Complex positive definite ($\bar{X}^T A X > 0 $ for all $ X \neq 0 $)

2. The eigenvalues of $ A $ are all $> 0 $ 3.$ A = P^T \bar{P}$, where $ P $ is an invertible upper triangular complex matrix

4. $ A $ The leading principal minors of $> 0 $ > **Instructor's Explanation**: We have already seen these four conditions for real symmetric positive definite matrices. The proof idea for the complex case is exactly the same, only replacing the transpose $ T $ with the conjugate transpose $ H $. Condition (2) allows you to judge positive definiteness using eigenvalues; condition (3) is the Cholesky decomposition, which has computational meaning; and condition (4) is Sylvester's criterion, which only requires checking the principal minors, without needing to calculate eigenvalues.

  

### 4.2 Cholesky Decomposition

  

**Theorem (Cholesky Decomposition)**:

$$
A \text{ is a positive definite matrix} \iff A = P^T \bar{P}
$$

where $ P $ is an upper triangular matrix with all diagonal entries being $> 0 $.

  

### 4.3 Constructive Proof

  

**Motivation**: We want to transform $ A $ into the identity matrix using a congruence transformation. The method is **conjugate pair of row and column transformations**—that is, applying the same elementary transformation to both the $ i $ row and the $ i $ column simultaneously. This is equivalent to right-multiplying by an upper triangular matrix $ P $ and left-multiplying by $ P^T $.

  

Taking $ 4 \times 4 $ as an example, the leading principal minors of $ A $ are all $> 0 $:

- $|A_1| = a_{11} > 0 $-$ b_{22} = |A_2|/|A_1| > 0 $-$ c_{33} = |A_3|/|A_2| > 0 $-$ d_{44} = |A_4|/|A_3| > 0 $

  

Through paired Gaussian elimination, $ A $ is gradually reduced to a diagonal matrix $\mathrm{diag}(a_{11}, b_{22}, c_{33}, d_{44})$, and then each diagonal element is normalized by taking its square root, finally yielding $ P^T \bar{P} = A $.

  

> **Instructor's Comments**: Cholesky decomposition is **extremely important** in numerical computation. When solving the positive definite linear system $ Ax = b $, using the Cholesky decomposition $ A = P^T \bar{P}$, and then solving $\bar{P}y = b $ and $ P^T x = y $ sequentially (two back substitutions), is twice as fast and has better numerical stability than Gaussian elimination. Cholesky decomposition is heavily used in Monte Carlo simulations in financial engineering and Gaussian processes in machine learning.

## 5. Unitary Space: The Complex Version of Euclidean Space

  

### 5.1 Definition

  

**Definition**: A complex linear space equipped with a Hermitian inner product is called a **Unitary Space**. 

  

In a unitary space:

- Length: $||\alpha|| := \sqrt{(\alpha, \alpha)}$

- Orthogonality: $\alpha \perp \beta \iff (\alpha, \beta) = 0 $

- Distance: $ d(\alpha, \beta) := ||\alpha - \beta||$

  

### 5.2 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

  

This is the cornerstone of the entire metric theory.

  

**Theorem**: $||\alpha|| \cdot ||\beta|| \geq |(\alpha, \beta)|$, equality holds $\iff \alpha, \beta $ are collinear.

  

**Proof**: Let $\beta \neq 0 $. By positive definiteness, for any complex number $ t $:

$$
(\alpha - t\beta, \alpha - t\beta) \geq 0
$$

Expand (note the conjugate linearity):

$$
(\alpha, \alpha) - t(\alpha, \beta) - \bar{t}(\beta, \alpha) + t\bar{t}(\beta, \beta) \geq 0
$$

**Key Step**: Take $ t = \dfrac{(\alpha, \beta)}{(\beta, \beta)}$. Why take this? Because this is the $ t $ that minimizes the expression above (analogous to the vertex of a quadratic function). Substituting gives:

$$
(\alpha, \alpha)(\beta, \beta) - |(\alpha, \beta)|^2 \geq 0
$$

Which is $||\alpha|| \cdot ||\beta|| \geq |(\alpha, \beta)|$. $\square $> **Instructor's Explanation**: Note the difference from the real space—the right side is $|(\alpha, \beta)|$ (absolute value), not the absolute value of $|(\alpha, \beta)|$, because $(\alpha, \beta)$ itself might be a complex number. Equality holds if and only if $\alpha = t\beta $, i.e., they are collinear.

  

### 5.3 Triangle Inequality

  

**Theorem**: $||\alpha + \beta|| \leq ||\alpha|| + ||\beta||$.

  

**Proof**:

$$
\begin{aligned}

||\alpha + \beta||^2 &= (\alpha+\beta, \alpha+\beta) \\

&= (\alpha,\alpha) + (\alpha,\beta) + (\beta,\alpha) + (\beta,\beta) \\

&= ||\alpha||^2 + 2\mathrm{Re}(\alpha,\beta) + ||\beta||^2 \\

&\leq ||\alpha||^2 + 2|(\alpha,\beta)| + ||\beta||^2 \\

&\leq ||\alpha||^2 + 2||\alpha||\cdot||\beta|| + ||\beta||^2 \quad \text{(C-S inequality)} \\

&= (||\alpha|| + ||\beta||)^2

\end{aligned}
$$

Taking the square root gives it. $\square $> **Instructor's Comments**: Note that the third line to the fourth line uses $\mathrm{Re}(z) \leq |z|$. This is a subtle point in the complex case—in real space, $(\alpha,\beta) + (\beta,\alpha) = 2(\alpha,\beta)$, but in complex space, $(\alpha,\beta) + (\beta,\alpha) = (\alpha,\beta) + \overline{(\alpha,\beta)} = 2\mathrm{Re}(\alpha,\beta)$.

  

### 5.4 Angle and Orthonormal Basis

  

Define the angle $\alpha, \beta $ between $\theta $ satisfying $\cos\theta = \dfrac{|(\alpha, \beta)|}{||\alpha|| \cdot ||\beta||}$.

  

> **Note**: In complex space, $\theta $ is actually the minimum angle between the real plane $\langle \alpha, i\alpha \rangle $ and $\langle \beta, i\beta \rangle $. This is slightly different from the intuition in real space.

  

**Orthonormal Basis**: A basis formed by mutually orthogonal unit vectors. $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ is the metric matrix of the standard orthonormal basis $\iff $ under the inner product $\{\beta_i\}$, which is $ I $.

  

### 5.5 Standard Unitary Space $\mathbb{C}^n $ $\mathbb{C}^n $ Equipped with a standard inner product $(X, Y) = \bar{X}^T Y = \sum x_i \bar{y}_i $, forms $ n $ a standard unitary space.$\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ is a standard orthonormal basis $\iff $ matrix $ U = [\beta_1 \cdots \beta_n]$ satisfies $ U^H U = I $.

## 6. Unitary Matrices and Unitary Group

  

### 6.1 Definition and Equivalent Conditions

  

**Definition**: A complex square matrix $ U $ is a **Unitary Matrix** if it satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions:

  

1. $ U^H U = I $ 2.$ U U^H = I $ 3.$ U^{-1} = U^H $ 4.$ U $ the column vectors forming $\mathbb{C}^n $ a standard orthonormal basis

  

> **Instructor's Explanation**: A unitary matrix is the "complex version of an orthogonal matrix". An orthogonal matrix $ Q $ satisfies $ Q^T Q = I $, and a unitary matrix $ U $ satisfies $ U^H U = I $. Replacing $ T $ with $ H $ leaves everything unchanged. The columns of a unitary matrix are orthonormal, and the rows are also orthonormal (because $ U U^H = I $ also holds).

  

### 6.2 Eigenvalue Properties ★

  

**Theorem**:

- The eigenvalues of a unitary matrix $\lambda $ satisfy $|\lambda| = 1 $ (all lie on the unit circle)

- The eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are all **real numbers**

- The eigenvalues of an anti-Hermitian matrix ($ A^H = -A $) are all **purely imaginary or 0**

  

**Proof** (Unitary Matrix): Let $ A\alpha = \lambda\alpha $, $\alpha \neq 0 $. Take the conjugate transpose: $\bar{\alpha}^T A^H = \bar{\lambda}\bar{\alpha}^T $.

$$
\bar{\alpha}^T \alpha = \bar{\alpha}^T A^H A \alpha = \bar{\lambda}\lambda \bar{\alpha}^T \alpha = |\lambda|^2 \bar{\alpha}^T \alpha
$$

From $\bar{\alpha}^T \alpha = ||\alpha||^2 \neq 0 $, we get $|\lambda| = 1 $. $\square $> **Teacher's Comments**: This proof is very elegant—using $ A^H A = I $ to directly combine $\lambda $ and $\bar{\lambda}$ to get $|\lambda|^2 $. The proof for the Hermitian matrix is similar: $ A\alpha = \lambda\alpha \Rightarrow \bar{\alpha}^T A \alpha = \lambda \bar{\alpha}^T \alpha $, the left side is $= \overline{\bar{\alpha}^T A \alpha} = \bar{\lambda} \bar{\alpha}^T \alpha $ (because $ A = A^H $), so $\lambda = \bar{\lambda}$, which is $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.

  

### 6.3 Unitary Group

$$
U(n) = \{U \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) \mid U^H U = I\}, \quad SU(n) = \{U \in U(n) \mid \det U = 1\}
$$

$ U(n)$ is a unitary group of $ n $ level, and $ SU(n)$ is the special unitary group.

  

> **Teacher's Comments**: The unitary group is **everywhere** in physics. $ SU(2)$ describes spin, $ SU(3)$ is the gauge group of Quantum Chromodynamics, and $ U(1)$ is the gauge group of Quantum Electrodynamics. The gauge group of the Standard Model is $ SU(3) \times SU(2) \times U(1)$. Mastering unitary matrices will be directly helpful in understanding modern physics.

## 7. Fourier Matrix and Fast Fourier Transform FFT

  

### 7.1 Fourier Matrix

$$
F_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \begin{bmatrix}

1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\

1 & \zeta & \zeta^2 & \cdots & \zeta^{n-1} \\

1 & \zeta^2 & \zeta^4 & \cdots & \zeta^{2(n-1)} \\

\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\

1 & \zeta^{n-1} & \zeta^{2(n-1)} & \cdots & \zeta^{(n-1)^2}

\end{bmatrix}, \quad \zeta = e^{2\pi i / n}
$$

$ F_n $ is a unitary matrix. The column vectors of $ F_n $, $\alpha_0, \dots, \alpha_{n-1}$, form an orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{C}^n $.

  

**Fourier Transform**: $ X = F_n Y $ transforms the coefficients $ Y = (y_0, \dots, y_{n-1})^T $ (Fourier coefficients) into the signal $ X $. Inverse transform: $ Y = F_n^H X $.

  

> **Teacher's Explanation**: FFT might be the content in this chapter with the **highest engineering application value**. Without FFT, there is no modern digital signal processing, no MP3 compression, no WiFi communication (OFDM), and no MRI imaging. FFT reduces the complexity of DFT from $ O(n^2)$ to $ O(n \log n)$, which is a milestone in computer science.

  

### 7.2 The Core Idea of FFT: Divide and Conquer

  

**Key Observation**: The elements of $ F_{2n}$ are periodic. Let $\varepsilon = e^{2\pi i / 2n}$, then $\varepsilon^2 = e^{2\pi i / n} = \zeta_n $, and $\varepsilon^{k+n} = -\varepsilon^k $.

  

Using this property, move the odd-indexed columns of $ F_{2n}$ to the front and the even-indexed columns to the back, resulting in a **block decomposition**:

$$
F_{2n} = \begin{bmatrix} I_n & D_n \\ I_n & -D_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} F_n & 0 \\ 0 & F_n \end{bmatrix} \Gamma_{2n}
$$

Where $ D_n = \mathrm{diag}(1, \varepsilon, \varepsilon^2, \dots, \varepsilon^{n-1})$ and $\Gamma_{2n}$ are the odd and even column permutations.

  

Thus, calculating $ F_{2n} X $ becomes:

$$
F_{2n} X = \begin{bmatrix} F_n X_1 + D_n F_n X_2 \\ F_n X_1 - D_n F_n X_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Where $ X_1 $ are the elements at odd positions of $ X $, and $ X_2 $ are the elements at even positions.

  

> **Teacher's Explanation**: This is the "butterfly operation." The DFT of a $ 2n $ point is decomposed into the sum of the DFTs of two $ n $ points plus a complex multiplication of $ n $. Recursively:

$$
N_{2n} = 2N_n + n
$$

Expansion ($ n = 2^k $):

$$
N_{2^k} = \frac{n}{2} \log_2 n = \frac{n}{2} k
$$

### 7.3 Complexity Comparison

  

| $ n $| Direct $ n^2 $| FFT $\frac{n}{2}\log_2 n $ | Speedup Ratio |

|---|---|---|---|

| $ 2^{12} = 4096 $ | 16384 | 192 | 85x |

| $ 2^{20} \approx 10^6 $|$ 10^{12}$| 5120 |$ 2 \times 10^8 $ x |

| $ 2^{40} \approx 10^{12}$|$ 10^{24}$ | 5242880 | Astronomical |

  

> **Teacher's Comments**: When $ n = 2^{40}$, direct calculation requires $ 10^{24}$ multiplications—even if calculated at $ 10^{18}$ per second (exascale supercomputer), it would take 30 years. However, FFT only requires 5 million multiplications, which is done in milliseconds. This is the power of algorithms.

## 8. Unitary Transformation and Hermitian Transformation

  

### 8.1 Unitary Transformation

  

**Definition**: A linear transformation $ A $ on a unitary space that preserves the inner product:

$$
(A\alpha, A\beta) = (\alpha, \beta), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta
$$

**Theorem**: The following are equivalent:

- $ A $ is a unitary transformation

- $ A $ maps the standard orthonormal basis to the standard orthonormal basis

- The matrix of $ A $ in the standard orthonormal basis is a unitary matrix

  

**Proof**: Let $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ be the standard orthonormal basis.$(A\beta_1, \dots, A\beta_n) = (\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n)A $The left side is the standard orthonormal basis $\iff $ $ A $ is a unitary matrix.$\square $

  

> **Teacher's Explanation**: A unitary transformation is a "complex version of an orthogonal transformation." An orthogonal transformation preserves the real inner product, while a unitary transformation preserves the complex inner product. Geometrically, a unitary transformation is a "rigid motion" in complex space—it does not change length or angle.

  

### 8.2 Hermitian Transformation

  

**Definition**: If $(A\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, A\beta)$ for all $\alpha, \beta $, then $ A $ is a **Hermitian (self-adjoint) transformation**.

  

**Theorem**: If $ A $ is a Hermitian transformation, then $\iff $ $ A $ in the standard orthonormal basis is a Hermitian matrix.

  

**Theorem**: The eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are all real numbers.

  

> **Teacher's Comments**: The Hermitian transformation corresponds to an **observable** in quantum mechanics. One of the postulates of quantum mechanics is that every physical observable corresponds to a Hermitian operator, and the measurement result is the eigenvalue of this operator (which must be real, hence the requirement for Hermitian). This is why the theorem that the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are all real numbers is so important—it guarantees that the results of quantum mechanical measurements are indeed real numbers.

## 9. Conjugate Transformation: The Complex Version of the Adjoint Operator

  

### 9.1 Definition

  

**Definition**: Let $ A $ be a linear transformation on a unitary space. If there exists $ A^*$ such that:

$$
(A\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, A^*\beta), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta
$$

Then $ A^*$ is called the **conjugate transformation** (adjoint operator) of $ A $.

  

**Example**:

- The conjugate transformation of the unitary transformation $ U $ is $ U^{-1}$ (because $(U\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, U^{-1}\beta)$)

- The conjugate transform of the Hermitian transform $ A $ is itself $ A $.

  

### 9.2 Existence and Uniqueness

  

**Theorem**: On a finite-dimensional unitary space, any linear transformation $ A $ has a unique conjugate transform $ A^*$. In the standard orthonormal basis, if the matrix of $ A $ is $ A $, then the matrix of $ A^*$ is $ A^H $.

  

**Proof**: Let the matrix of $ A, A^*$ in the standard orthonormal basis be $ A, B $, and the coordinates of $\alpha, \beta $ be $ X, Y $:

$$
(A\alpha, \beta) = \bar{X}^T A^H Y, \quad (\alpha, A^*\beta) = \bar{X}^T B Y
$$

Thus, $ A^*$ is the conjugate transform $\iff \bar{X}^T A^H Y = \bar{X}^T B Y $ for all $ X, Y $ $\iff B = A^H $. $\square $

  

> **Instructor's Explanation**: The core idea of this proof is that "the matrix is uniquely determined by its effect on all vectors." $\bar{X}^T M Y = 0 $ for all $ X, Y $ $\iff M = 0 $. This is a technique frequently used in linear algebra.

## 10. Normal Transformations and Spectral Decomposition of Normal Matrices ★Key Theorem★

  

### 10.1 What is a Normal Transformation?

  

**Definition**: If $ A A^* = A^* A $, then $ A $ is a **normal transformation**.

  

Correspondingly, the matrix of $ A A^H = A^H A $ is called a **normal matrix**.

  

> **Instructor's Explanation**: Why is it called "normal"? Because it is the "most generally well-behaved matrix." Unitary matrices, Hermitian matrices, anti-Hermitian matrices, and even real symmetric matrices are **all** special cases of normal matrices. Normal matrices are the unified generalization of these "well-behaved matrices."

  

Verification:

- Unitary matrix $ U $: $ U U^H = U U^{-1} = I = U^{-1} U = U^H U $ ✓

- Hermitian matrix $ A $: $ A A^H = A^2 = A^H A $ ✓

- Anti-Hermitian matrix $ A $: $ A A^H = A(-A) = -A^2 = (-A)A = A^H A $ ✓

  

### 10.2 ★Main Theorem★: Unitary Diagonalization of Normal Matrices

  

**Theorem**: If $ A $ is a normal matrix $\iff $, there exist a unitary matrix $ U $ and a complex diagonal matrix $ D $ such that:

$$
A = U D U^H = U D U^{-1}
$$

> **Instructor's Comment**: This is the **most important theorem** of this chapter, without exception. It tells us: any normal matrix can be diagonalized by a unitary similarity transformation. Compared to the orthogonal diagonalization of a real symmetric matrix $ A = Q D Q^T $, here we just replace $ Q $ with $ U $, and $ Q^T $ with $ U^H $. But the power of this generalization is huge—it covers unitary matrices, Hermitian matrices, and all their combinations.

  

### 10.3 Complete Proof

  

**Proof** (By Mathematical Induction):

  

**Base Case**: The matrix $ 1 \times 1 $ is clearly true.

  

**Inductive Step**: Assume it holds for normal matrices of degree $< n $. Let $ A $ be a normal matrix of degree $ n $.

  

**Step 1**: Any matrix on $\mathbb{C}$ has eigenvalues (over an algebraically closed field). Let $\lambda_1 $ be an eigenvalue of $ A $, and $\alpha_1 $ be the corresponding unit eigenvector: $ A\alpha_1 = \lambda_1 \alpha_1 $, $||\alpha_1|| = 1 $.

  

**Step 2**: Extend $\alpha_1 $ to a basis of $\mathbb{C}^n $, and use Schmidt orthogonalization + normalization to obtain the standard orthonormal basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $. Form the unitary matrix $ U_1 = [\alpha_1 \ \alpha_2 \ \cdots \ \alpha_n]$.

  

**Step 3**: Calculate $ U_1^H A U_1 $. Due to $ A\alpha_1 = \lambda_1 \alpha_1 $, the first column is $(\lambda_1, 0, \dots, 0)^T $:

$$
U_1^H A U_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & C \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $ C $ is a row vector of $ 1 \times (n-1)$, and $ B $ is a matrix of $(n-1) \times (n-1)$.

  

**Step 4 (Crucial)**: Take the conjugate transpose:

$$
U_1^H A^H U_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \bar{\lambda}_1 & 0 \\ \bar{C}^T & \bar{B}^T \end{bmatrix}
$$

Since $ A $ is normal ($ A A^H = A^H A $), applying the similarity transformation $ U_1^H (\cdot) U_1 $ to both sides preserves normality:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & C \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \bar{\lambda}_1 & 0 \\ \bar{C}^T & \bar{B}^T \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \bar{\lambda}_1 & 0 \\ \bar{C}^T & \bar{B}^T \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & C \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix}
$$

Comparing the top-left element:

$$
\lambda_1 \bar{\lambda}_1 + C \bar{C}^T = \lambda_1 \bar{\lambda}_1 \Rightarrow C \bar{C}^T = 0
$$

And $ C \bar{C}^T = |c_2|^2 + \cdots + |c_n|^2 = 0 \Rightarrow C = 0 $.

  

> **Instructor's Explanation**: This is the **soul step** of the proof. Why must $ C $ be 0? Because the normality of $ A $ forces this. If $ A $ were not normal, $ C $ would generally not be 0, and we would not obtain a block diagonal form, making the induction fail. This is why only normal matrices can be unitarily diagonalized.

  

**Step 5**: Now $ U_1^H A U_1 = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, B)$, and $ B $ is also normal (derived from $ A A^H = A^H A $ to $ B B^H = B^H B $). Apply the inductive hypothesis to $ B $, yielding $ B = U_1' D_1 (U_1')^H $.

  

**Step 6**: Let $ U = U_1 \cdot \mathrm{diag}(1, U_1')$, then $ U $ is a unitary matrix (the block diagonal of unitary matrices is unitary), and $ A = U D U^H $. $\square $

  

### 10.4 Consequences

  

**Consequence 1**: If $ A $ is normal, then $\iff A = U D U^H $ (where $ U $ is unitary and $ D $ is complex diagonal)

  

**Consequence 2**: If $ A $ is unitary, then $\iff A = U D U^H $, and the diagonal elements of $ D $ have modulus 1

  

**Consequence 3**: If $ A $ is Hermitian, then $\iff A = U D U^H $, and $ D $ is a **real** diagonal matrix

  

**Consequence 4**: If $ A $ is anti-Hermitian, then $\iff A = U D U^H $, and the diagonal elements of $ D $ are purely imaginary

  

> **Instructor's Explanation**: These four consequences are "a master key opening four locks." Within the same spectral decomposition framework, restricting the nature of the diagonal elements distinguishes these four important classes of matrices. In exams and research, when encountering any of these four types of matrices, the first reaction should be "it can be unitarily diagonalized."

  

### 10.5 Orthonormal Complement of an Invariant Subspace

  

**Consequence**: Let $ A $ be a normal transformation. If $ W $ is a $ A $-invariant subspace, then $ W^\perp $ is also a $ A $-invariant subspace.

  

**Proof**: Take the matrix with the standard orthonormal basis $ W = \langle \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k \rangle $.$ A $ as $\begin{bmatrix} B & C \\ 0 & D \end{bmatrix}$($ W $ invariant $\Rightarrow $ (the bottom left is 0).$ A^*$ as $\begin{bmatrix} B^H & 0 \\ C^H & D^H \end{bmatrix}$.

  

From $ A A^H = A^H A $, we compare to get $ C C^H = 0 $. Taking the trace: $\mathrm{tr}(C C^H) = \sum |c_{ij}|^2 = 0 \Rightarrow C = 0 $.

  

Thus, the matrix of $ A $ is block diagonal, and $ W^\perp $ is also an invariant subspace. $\square $

  

> **Teacher's Comments**: This conclusion also has a corresponding result for real symmetric matrices (the orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace under a symmetric transformation is still an invariant subspace), but it does not hold for general matrices. Regularity is the weakest condition that guarantees this point. This property is repeatedly used in the proofs of **simultaneous diagonalization** theory and the **spectral theorem**.

## 14. Matrix Norms and Eigenvalue Estimation

  

### 14.1 Induced Norms

  

The **Euclidean induced norm** of a real matrix: $ B $ The **Euclidean induced norm**:

$$
||B|| := \max_{||X||=1} ||BX|| = \sqrt{\lambda_1(B^T B)} = \sigma_1(B)
$$

is the largest singular value.

  

If $ A $ is real symmetric, $||A|| = \max_i |\lambda_i(A)|$ (the absolute value of the largest eigenvalue).

  

### 14.2 Norm Properties

  

1. $||A|| > 0 $($ A \neq 0 $)

2. $||kA|| = |k| \cdot ||A||$

3. Triangle Inequality: $||A+B|| \leq ||A|| + ||B||$

4. Submultiplicativity: $||AB|| \leq ||A|| \cdot ||B||$ 5.$||A^T|| = ||A||$

  

### 14.3 Range of Quadratic Forms

  

For real symmetric $ A $, $\lambda_n ||X||^2 \leq X^T A X \leq \lambda_1 ||X||^2 $.

  

Equality holds $\iff $ $ X $ in the corresponding eigenspace.

  

> **Teacher’s explanation.** This inequality is central to the geometry of eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix. The value of $ X^T A X $ lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues. In optimization, this is the basis of the Rayleigh quotient.

  

### 14.4 Example: Upper Bound of Eigenvalues for the Hilbert Matrix

  

**Example**: The eigenvalues of $ A = [\frac{1}{i+j-1}]_{n \times n}$ are all $> 0 $ and $\leq 3 + 2\sqrt{2}$.

  

**Solution**: Let $ B $ be the all-ones lower triangular matrix, $ D = I $. Then $ A = B^T B = B + B^T - D $.

  

$ B $ Invertible $\Rightarrow A $ Positive definite $\Rightarrow $ Eigenvalue $> 0 $.

$$
||A|| \leq ||B|| + ||B^T|| + ||D|| = 2||B|| + 1 = 2\sqrt{\lambda_1(B^T B)} + 1
$$

From $\lambda_1 \leq (2\sqrt{\lambda_1} + 1)^2 $, we get $\sqrt{\lambda_1} \leq 1 + \sqrt{2}$, so $\lambda_1 \leq 3 + 2\sqrt{2}$.

  

> **Teacher's Comment**: The Hilbert matrix is a famous **ill-conditioned matrix**—although positive definite, its condition number grows exponentially with $ n $. This example demonstrates a standard technique for estimating eigenvalue upper bounds using norm inequalities: matrix decomposition, individual estimation, and combination.

## 15. Penrose Pseudoinverse and SVD

  

### 15.1 Penrose Pseudoinverse

  

For any complex matrix $ A $, there exists a **unique** matrix $ X $ satisfying:

$$
AXA = A, \quad XAX = X, \quad (AX)^H = AX, \quad (XA)^H = XA
$$

$ X $ is denoted as $ A^+$, called the **Penrose Pseudoinverse**.

  

If $ A $ has full column rank, then $ A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T $ (this is the formula for the least squares solution!).

  

### 15.2 SVD and Pseudoinverse

  

If $ A = P S Q^H $ (Singular Value Decomposition), then:

$$
A^+ = Q \cdot \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1^{-1}, \dots, \sigma_r^{-1}, 0, \dots, 0) \cdot P^H
$$

> **Teacher's Explanation**: SVD is the **most powerful decomposition tool** in linear algebra. Eigenvalue decomposition only works for invertible/normal matrices, but SVD works for **any matrix**. Applications of SVD include: data compression (PCA), image denoising, recommendation systems (matrix completion), and solving overdetermined/underdetermined systems of equations. $ A^+$ is constructed via SVD, taking the reciprocal of non-zero singular values and keeping zero singular values as zero; this is the most natural definition of the "pseudoinverse".

  

### 15.3 MATLAB Numerical Computation

  

- `[U, D] = eig(A)`: Eigenvalue decomposition

- `[P, S, Q] = svd(A)`: Singular Value Decomposition

- `[Q, R] = qr(B)`: QR Decomposition

- `rref(B)`: Reduced Row Echelon Form

  

> **Caution**: The Jordan canonical form is **numerically unstable**; it must be converted to a symbolic matrix using `sym` before computation. This is because Jordan blocks are extremely sensitive to small perturbations in the matrix elements—any matrix can be approximated by a diagonalizable matrix arbitrarily closely, so Jordan blocks tend to "spread out" in numerical computations.

## 16. Cauchy Interlacing and Courant-Fischer Min-Max Theorem ★Advanced Tool★

  

### 16.1 Cauchy Interlacing Principle

  

**Theorem**:$ n $ Symmetric matrix $ A $ of the eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n $.$ A $ of the $ n-1 $ principal submatrix $ B $ of the eigenvalues $\mu_1 \geq \cdots \geq \mu_{n-1}$.
Then:

$$
\lambda_1 \geq \mu_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \mu_2 \geq \cdots \geq \mu_{n-1} \geq \lambda_n
$$

> **Teacher's Explanation**: "Interlacing" means "interspersing"—the eigenvalues of the principal submatrix "interlace" the eigenvalues of the original matrix. This theorem has important applications in graph theory (eigenvalues of subgraphs), random matrix theory, and numerical linear algebra.

  

### 16.2 Weyl's Theorem

  

$ A, B $ Symmetric, eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n $ and $\mu_1 \geq \cdots \geq \mu_n $.$ A+B $ of the eigenvalues $\tau_k $ satisfies:

$$
\lambda_k + \mu_1 \geq \tau_k \geq \lambda_k + \mu_n
$$

> **Teacher's Comment**: Weyl's theorem describes "how eigenvalues move under perturbation". If $ B $ is a small perturbation, $\tau_k $ will not deviate too much from $\lambda_k $—at most by $\mu_1 - \mu_n $ (the spectral width of $ B $). This is the theoretical basis for **eigenvalue stability** in numerical analysis.

  

### 16.3 ★Courant-Fischer Min-Max Theorem★

  

**Theorem**: For a real symmetric $ A $, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n $, and corresponding orthonormal eigenvectors $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $. Then:

$$
\lambda_k = \min_{\substack{V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \\ \dim V = n-k+1}} \max_{0 \neq \alpha \in V} \frac{\alpha^T A \alpha}{\alpha^T \alpha}
$$

> **Teacher's Comment**: This is an **advanced** tool. It provides a variational characterization of eigenvalues—$\lambda_k $ is not the Rayleigh quotient of some specific vector, but the **minimum** of the maximum Rayleigh quotient over all $ n-k+1 $-dimensional subspaces. This theorem is the common foundation for the finite element method, spectral graph theory, and the variational principle in quantum mechanics.

  

**Proof Idea**: Let $ U = \langle \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k \rangle $. For any $ n-k+1 $-dimensional subspace $ V $:

$$
\dim V + \dim U = n+1 > n = \dim(V + U)
$$

By the dimension formula, $\dim(V \cap U) \geq 1 $. Taking $ 0 \neq \gamma \in V \cap U $, we get $\gamma \in U \Rightarrow \frac{\gamma^T A \gamma}{\gamma^T \gamma} \geq \lambda_k $ (because the Rayleigh quotient of a vector in $ U $ is $\geq \lambda_k $).

  

Thus, $\max_{\alpha \in V} \frac{\alpha^T A \alpha}{\alpha^T \alpha} \geq \frac{\gamma^T A \gamma}{\gamma^T \gamma} \geq \lambda_k $. $\square $

  

> **Teacher's Explanation**: The core of the proof is the **dimension argument**—that two "large enough" subspaces must have a non-zero intersection. This is one of the most elegant arguments in linear algebra, and it appears repeatedly in functional analysis and algebraic topology.

  

### 16.4 Application: Eigenvalue Comparison for Positive Semidefinite Matrices

  

**Theorem**: If $ A, B $ is real symmetric and $ A - B $ is positive semidefinite, then $\lambda_k(A) \geq \lambda_k(B)$.

  

**Proof**: $ A-B $ is positive semidefinite $\Rightarrow \alpha^T A \alpha \geq \alpha^T B \alpha $. By Min-Max:

$$
\lambda_k(A) = \min_{\dim V=n-k+1} \max_{\alpha \in V} \frac{\alpha^T A \alpha}{\alpha^T \alpha} \geq \min_{\dim V=n-k+1} \max_{\alpha \in V} \frac{\alpha^T B \alpha}{\alpha^T \alpha} = \lambda_k(B)
$$

## 17. Matrix Representation of Three-Dimensional Rotation: Rodrigues' Formula

  

### 17.1 Problem Setup

  

Let $\alpha = (a_1, a_2, a_3)^T $ be a unit vector (the rotation axis), and $\theta $ be the rotation angle. Find the matrix $ A $ for a rotation of $\theta $ around $\alpha $.

  

### 17.2 Geometric Derivation

  

Decompose an arbitrary $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^3 $:

$$
\beta = \beta_1 + \beta_2, \quad \beta_1 = (\beta, \alpha)\alpha \in \langle\alpha\rangle, \quad \beta_2 = \beta - (\beta, \alpha)\alpha \in \langle\alpha\rangle^\perp
$$

Let $\gamma = \alpha \times \beta_2 = \alpha \times \beta $ be defined. Then $\alpha, \beta_2, \gamma $ forms a right-handed system, $||\gamma|| = ||\beta_2||$.

  

After rotation:

$$
A\beta = \beta_1 + \cos\theta \cdot \beta_2 + \sin\theta \cdot \gamma
$$

### 17.3 Matrix Form

  

Define the cross-product matrix $ C = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a_3 & -a_2 \\ -a_3 & 0 & a_1 \\ a_2 & -a_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, satisfying $\beta \times \alpha = C\beta $.

  

Substitute and simplify:

$$
\begin{aligned}

A\beta &= (\beta, \alpha)\alpha + \cos\theta(\beta - (\beta,\alpha)\alpha) + \sin\theta \cdot \alpha \times \beta \\

&= \cos\theta \cdot \beta + (1-\cos\theta)(\alpha\alpha^T)\beta - \sin\theta \cdot C\beta \\

&= (\cos\theta \cdot I + (1-\cos\theta)\alpha\alpha^T - \sin\theta \cdot C)\beta

\end{aligned}
$$

Using $\alpha\alpha^T - I = C^2 $ (verifiable), we finally obtain **Rodrigues' Formula**: 

$$
A = I - \sin\theta \cdot C + (1 - \cos\theta) C^2
$$

> **Teacher's Explanation**: Rodrigues' formula is a **fundamental tool** in computer graphics, robotics, and aerospace engineering. Any 3D rotation can be represented by "axis+angle", and Rodrigues' formula provides the explicit conversion from axis-angle to matrix.

  

### 17.4 Matrix Exponential Representation

  

More concisely: $ A = e^{\theta C}$.

  

**Why?** Because $ C $ is a skew-symmetric matrix and $||\alpha|| = 1 $, we have $ C^3 = -C $, $ C^4 = -C^2 $, $ C^5 = C $, ..., with a cycle period of 4. Expanding the power series of $ e^{\theta C}$:

$$
e^{\theta C} = I + \theta C + \frac{\theta^2}{2!} C^2 + \frac{\theta^3}{3!} C^3 + \cdots
$$

Using $ C^3 = -C $, $ C^4 = -C^2 $, etc., the odd powers combine to $\sin\theta \cdot C $, and the even powers combine to $(1-\cos\theta)C^2 $, which exactly yields Rodrigues' formula.

  

> **Teacher's Comment**: The notation $ e^{\theta C}$ is not formal—the matrix exponential $ e^A = \sum \frac{A^k}{k!}$ converges for any matrix. If $ A = UJU^{-1}$ (Jordan decomposition), then $ e^A = U e^J U^{-1}$. This is a core tool in the theory of differential equations: the solution to $\dot{x} = Ax $ is $ x(t) = e^{At} x(0)$.

  

### 17.5 Recovering Axis and Angle from Rotation Matrix

  

From $ A = I - \sin\theta \cdot C + (1-\cos\theta)C^2 $:

- $ I + (1-\cos\theta)C^2 $ is symmetric

- $-\sin\theta \cdot C $ is skew-symmetric

  

Thus, $ A^T - A = 2\sin\theta \cdot C $. From this, we can read:

- $\theta = \arccos\left(\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A) - 1}{2}\right)$-$\alpha $ recovered from the non-zero elements of $ C $

$$
X^T C X = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} b_{ij} x_i x_j = \sum_{i,j} \left(\sum_k \lambda_k p_{ik} p_{jk}\right) b_{ij} x_i x_j = \sum_k \lambda_k \left(\sum_{i,j} b_{ij} (p_{ik} x_i)(p_{jk} x_j)\right)
$$

For each fixed $ k $, let $ y_i = p_{ik} x_i $, then $\sum_{i,j} b_{ij} y_i y_j = Y^T B Y \geq 0 $($ B $ (positive semi-definite). Therefore, $ X^T C X \geq 0 $. $\square $> **Teacher's Comments**: This theorem is called the **Schur product theorem**. The essence of the proof is to use spectral decomposition to $ a_{ij}$"split up", and then interchange the order of summation, finding that the inner part is exactly $ B $ a quadratic form. This is the standard technique for handling Hadamard products.

  

### Exercise 4: Subspace Distance Formula

  

Let $ U, V $ be a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n $, $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^n $. Prove:

$$
\mathrm{dis}(\beta + U, V) = \mathrm{dis}(\beta, U + V)
$$

**Hint**: $||(\beta - \alpha) - \gamma|| = ||\beta - (\alpha + \gamma)||$, $\forall \alpha \in U, \gamma \in V $. Taking the minimum on both sides gives the result.

  

### Exercise 5: Spectral Projection of Normal Transformation

  

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a normal transformation on the unitary space $ V $, and $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_s $ be distinct eigenvalues. Let $\mathcal{P}_i = h_i(\mathcal{A})$, where $ h_i(x) = \prod_{j \neq i} \frac{x - \lambda_j}{\lambda_i - \lambda_j}$.

  

Prove:

1. $\mathcal{P}_i $ is the orthogonal projection onto $\mathrm{Ker}(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_i I)$

2. $ g(\mathcal{A}) = \sum_i g(\lambda_i) \mathcal{P}_i $ (for any power series $ g $)

3. There exists a polynomial $ f $ such that $ f(\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{A}^*$

  

> **Hint**: $ h_i(x)$ is the Lagrange interpolation polynomial. The eigenvalues of $\mathcal{P}_i $ are $\delta_{ij}$ (evaluated at $\lambda_j $). Normality guarantees that the eigenspaces corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, so $\mathcal{P}_i $ is an orthogonal projection.
