### On the existence of the (Jordan) canonical form

If the characteristic polynomial of $ A \in M_n(K)$ can be factored into a product of linear factors over the field $ K $, then there exists an invertible matrix $ U \in M_n(K)$ such that $ U^{-1}AU $ is a Jordan form matrix.

#### Proof process

From the above, for $ A \in M_n(K)$, there always exists an invertible $ x $-matrix $ P(x), Q(x)$ such that:

$$
(xI - A)Q(x) = P(x)^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} c_1(x) & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & c_n(x) \end{bmatrix} \quad (1)
$$

Let $(x - \lambda)^r $ be an elementary divisor of $ A $ from $ c_i(x)$. Let the $ i $ column of $ Q(x)$ be $ Q_i(x)$. Comparing with the $ i $ column of equation (1) gives:

$$
(xI - A)Q_i(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{(x - \lambda)^r} \quad (2)
$$

Expanding the components of $ Q_i(x)$ as a polynomial in $ x - \lambda $, we write it as:

$$
Q_i(x) \equiv \alpha_1 + (x - \lambda)\alpha_2 + \cdots + (x - \lambda)^{r-1}\alpha_r \pmod{(x - \lambda)^r} \quad (3)
$$

Here $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r $ is a column vector in $ K^n $. Note that substituting $ x=\lambda $ on both sides yields the term $\alpha_1 $, which is the $ i $ column $ Q_i(\lambda)$ of the invertible number matrix $ Q(\lambda)$, so $\alpha_1 \neq 0 $.

Applying the lemma to the above equations (2) and (3), we find:

$$
V_{i,\lambda} = \langle \alpha_r, \alpha_{r-1}, \dots, \alpha_1 \rangle \subseteq \text{Ker}(A - \lambda I)^r
$$

is $ A - \lambda I $ a generated $\alpha_r $ by $ r $ dimensional strong cyclic subspace, and $\alpha_1 = Q_i(\lambda)$ is $ V_{i,\lambda}$ the last term of a set of cyclic bases.

---

Now consider the elementary divisors that have the same eigenvalue $\lambda $ but come from different diagonal entries $ c_i(x)$ ($ 1 \le i \le n $): $(x - \lambda)^{r_1}, (x - \lambda)^{r_2}, \dots $. The subspaces given by them, $ V_{i,\lambda}$, are all $(A - \lambda I)$-strong cyclic subspaces. The **tails** of the cyclic bases of these spaces come from different columns $ Q_i(\lambda)$ of the invertible matrix $ Q(\lambda)$, so these **tails are linearly independent**. Therefore, when $\lambda $ is fixed, the subspaces $ V_{i,\lambda}$ ($ 1 \le i \le n $) are direct sums, and we denote this direct sum as $ V_\lambda $, which is $ V_\lambda = \bigoplus_{1 \le i \le n} V_{i,\lambda}$ (taking only the non-zero $ V_{i,\lambda}$).

Note that $ V_\lambda \subseteq \text{Ker}(A - \lambda I)^{r_\lambda}$, where $ r_\lambda $ represents the highest degree of all elementary divisors belonging to the eigenvalue $\lambda $.

When $\lambda $ takes all the different eigenvalues of $ A $, the generalized eigenspaces $\text{Ker}(A - \lambda I)^{r_\lambda}$ are direct sum relationships, so the subspaces $ V_\lambda $ are also direct sum relationships. Combining this with the definition of $ V_\lambda $, the sum of the subspaces $ V_{i,\lambda}$ ($\forall i, \lambda $) given by all elementary divisors of $ A $ is a direct sum.

Finally, we show that this direct sum has a dimension equal to the dimension of each subspace $ V_{i,\lambda}$ of $ K^n $, which equals the degree of its corresponding elementary divisor. Since the characteristic polynomial of $ A $ can be factored into a product of linear factors over the field $ K $, the sum of the degrees of all elementary divisors of $ A $ equals $ n $. Comparing the dimensions of the direct sum, we get:

$$
\bigoplus_{\lambda, i} V_{i,\lambda} = \bigoplus_\lambda V_\lambda = K^n
$$

(Similarly, taking only the non-zero $ V_{i,\lambda}$ as a direct sum).

$$

$$

This proves that the standard form $ F $ is similar to its transpose $ F^T $.

Using $ A = Q F Q^{-1}$, taking the transpose on both ends gives:

$$
A^T = (Q^{-1})^T F^T Q^T
$$

Substituting $ F^T = P F P^{-1}$ and $ F = Q^{-1} A Q $ into the equation above and expanding:

$$
\begin{aligned} A^T &= (Q^{-1})^T (P F P^{-1}) Q^T \\ &= (Q^{-1})^T P (Q^{-1} A Q) P^{-1} Q^T \\ &= \left( (Q^{-1})^T P Q^{-1} \right) A \left( Q P^{-1} Q^T \right) \end{aligned}
$$

Let $ R = (Q^{-1})^T P Q^{-1}$, and it is easy to show that its inverse matrix is $ R^{-1} = Q P^{-1} Q^T $.

The above equation can be simplified to:

$$
A^T = R A R^{-1}
$$

This shows that **$ A $ is similar to $ A^T $**. This conclusion holds over the complex numbers or any algebraically closed field. The next question is, can a square matrix be decomposed like that? In fact, such a decomposition is called the rational canonical form, and it can indeed be done.

# Rational Canonical Form

If the diagonal blocks $ B_i $ $(1 \le i \le s)$ of the block diagonal matrix $ B = \begin{bmatrix} B_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & B_s \end{bmatrix}$ are all **Companion Matrices** of certain monic polynomials $ d_i(x)$, and these polynomials satisfy $ d_1(x) \mid d_2(x) \mid \cdots \mid d_s(x)$, then the matrix $ B $ is called a **Rational Canonical Form Matrix**.

### Theorem:

Every matrix is similar to a **unique rational canonical form matrix**.

### Invariant Factor

At this point, we need to trace back to the elementary transformations of the x-matrix mentioned at the beginning. If we strictly follow step (4*), we will obtain what is called the Smith Normal Form.

If $ A \in M_n(K)$, then there exists an invertible $ x $-matrix $ P(x), Q(x)$ such that

$$
P(x)(xI - A)Q(x) = \text{Smith canonical form} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & & & & \\ & \ddots & & & \\ & & d_1(x) & & \\ & & & \ddots & \\ & & & & d_s(x) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Here, $ d_i(x)$ is a monic polynomial of degree $\ge 1 $, satisfying

$$
d_1(x) \mid d_2(x) \mid \cdots \mid d_s(x) .
$$

#### Invariant factors do not change with field extension

The invariant factor group of the matrix $ A \in M_n(K)$

$$
d_1(x), \cdots, d_s(x) \in K[x]
$$

is uniquely determined by $ A $ (the similarity equivalence class), and does not depend on the extension of $ K $.

**Proof:** Suppose $ A $ has another set of invariant factors $ c_1(x), \cdots, c_t(x)$. Due to the one-to-one correspondence between the above invariant factor group and the elementary factor group of $\mathbb{C}$, $ A $ will have two different sets of elementary factors in $\mathbb{C}$. However, according to what was stated before, the elementary factor group of $ A $ (because it is bound to the quasi-block of $ A $) is uniquely determined by the similarity class of $ A $, leading to a contradiction!

#### Invariant factors give a space decomposition

Let $ d_1(x), \cdots, d_s(x)$ be the invariant factor of $ A \in M_n(K)$, then there exists $\gamma_1, \cdots, \gamma_s \in K^n $, such that

$$
Z(\gamma_1, A) \oplus \cdots \oplus Z(\gamma_s, A) = K^n,
$$

And the minimal polynomial of $ A $ at $\gamma_i $ is $ d_i(x), \forall 1 \le i \le s $.

Here, $ Z(\gamma_i, A)$ is the **$ A $-cyclic subspace** generated by $\gamma_i $.
