From inner products, we abstract a bilinear map, which naturally leads us to wonder if there are multilinear maps, and what their simplest form is.

Multilinear maps can actually be defined intuitively:

$$
f(a_1, ...,ka_i + b,...a_n)=kf(a_1,...,a_i,...a_n)+f(a_1,...,b,...a_n)
$$

Such a multilinear map holds for every $ i $. So, what kind of object can satisfy such a property? In the notes on bilinear maps to tensor products, we pointed out that the tensor product is the cleanest form of a bilinear map. Specifically, let $\xi,\eta $ be two elements in the same dual space, then

$$
\xi\otimes\eta
$$

such a so-called tensor product, we simply let it be equivalent to:

$$
\xi\otimes\eta\ (\alpha,\beta)=\xi(\alpha)\eta(\beta)
$$

It is not difficult to verify that this is a bilinear map, and it is very simple: applying separately and then multiplying. So, how do we describe such a space? To describe a linear map, we naturally look for a basis and observe the image of the basis. Let $\{\alpha_i\},\{\beta_i\}$ be a basis for two spaces, then taking any pair and applying them separately gives

$$
\xi(\alpha_i)\eta(\beta_j)
$$

In fact, we find that such a pair $\alpha_i,\beta_j $ actually forms a basis for this tensor product space, which we denote as $\alpha_i\otimes\beta_j $, similar to a pair, so

$$
\xi\otimes\eta(\alpha_i\otimes\beta_j)=\xi(\alpha_i)\otimes\eta(\beta_j)
$$

This is natural, but these are all very intuitive feelings; how do we describe it rigorously using axioms?

# Tensor Product: The Purest Space of Multilinear Maps

---

## I. From Bilinear Maps to Multilinear Maps

Starting from the inner product, we are already familiar with symmetric bilinear functions. This naturally leads us to wonder: **Are there multilinear maps? What is their simplest form?**

A multilinear map can be defined very intuitively: Let $V_1, \dots, V_m, W$ be a linear space over the field $K$. A map $\varphi: V_1 \times \dots \times V_m \to W$ is called a **multilinear map** if it is linear in each component—that is, for any $i \in \{1, \dots, m\}$, any $v_1, v_2 \in V_i$, any $\lambda \in K$, and any other component fixed, we have:

$$\varphi(v_1, \dots, \lambda v_2, \dots, v_m) = \lambda \varphi(v_1, \dots, v_2, \dots, v_m)$$

And it also satisfies the distributive law for addition. In other words, if we view the input as a slot of $m$ positions, we only change things in one slot at a time, and the map is linear with respect to that slot.

When $m = 1$, it is a regular linear map; when $m = 2$, it is a bilinear map.

### 1.1 What can satisfy multilinearity?

So, what kind of object can satisfy such a property?

In the notes, we have already seen that the cleanest form of a bilinear map is the **tensor product**. Specifically, let $V, W$ be a linear space, and $\xi \in V^*, \eta \in W^*$ be two linear functionals, then:

$$\xi \otimes \eta$$

We simply let this symbol be equivalent to:

$$(\xi \otimes \eta)(v, w) = \xi(v) \cdot \eta(w), \quad \forall v \in V, w \in W$$

It is not difficult to verify that this is a bilinear map—applying separately and then multiplying. Very simple.

### 1.2 How to describe such a space?

So, how do we describe such a space? To describe a linear space, we naturally look for a basis and observe the image of the basis.

Let $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m\}$ be a basis for $V$, and $\{\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n\}$ be a basis for $W$. For any $v = \sum a_i \alpha_i, w = \sum b_j \beta_j$, by bilinearity:

$$(\xi \otimes \eta)(v, w) = \left(\sum_i a_i \xi(\alpha_i)\right) \cdot \left(\sum_j b_j \eta(\beta_j)\right) = \sum_{i,j} a_i b_j \xi(\alpha_i) \eta(\beta_j)$$

Now observe the action of the basis vectors on $\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j$—more accurately, observe the value of $\xi \otimes \eta$ on the basis vectors:

$$(\xi \otimes \eta)(\alpha_i, \beta_j) = \xi(\alpha_i) \cdot \eta(\beta_j)$$

This shows that: if we consider $\{\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j\}_{1 \leq i \leq m, 1 \leq j \leq n}$ as some "pair of basis elements", then the value of $\xi \otimes \eta$ on these pairs of basis elements is exactly the product of $\xi(\alpha_i)$ and $\eta(\beta_j)$.

Furthermore, any element of the form $\alpha_i^* \otimes \beta_j^*$ (where $\alpha_i^*, \beta_j^*$ is the dual basis) has the value on the pair of basis vectors $(\alpha_k, \beta_l)$:

$$(\alpha_i^* \otimes \beta_j^*)(\alpha_k, \beta_l) = \alpha_i^*(\alpha_k) \cdot \beta_j^*(\beta_l) = \delta_{ik} \delta_{jl}$$

This set of elements takes the value 1 at $(i,j) = (k,l)$ and 0 otherwise on the pair of basis vectors. They form a basis for the space of bilinear functions—completely analogous to the property of the basis matrix $E_{ij}$ in the matrix space $M_{m \times n}(K)$, which is 1 at $(i,j)$ and 0 otherwise.

Therefore, the set of all bilinear functions forms a linear space with dimension $mn$, and its basis is composed of $\alpha_i^* \otimes \beta_j^*$.

## II. From "Tensor Product of Functions" to "Tensor Product of Vectors"

Above, we constructed the tensor product of **dual space elements**—they are bilinear functions that output scalars. But the true power of the tensor product lies in: it can also incorporate **ordinary vectors**.

### 2.1 A natural idea

Since $\alpha_i^* \otimes \beta_j^*$ can be viewed as a "function of bases", then conversely, can $\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j$ be viewed as "the bases themselves"?

More generally, given $u \in U, v \in V$, can we define an object $u \otimes v$ such that its action on any functor $\xi \in U^*, \eta \in V^*$ is:

$$(u \otimes v)(\xi, \eta) = \xi(u) \cdot \eta(v)$$

Note the right side: $\xi(u)$ is a number, $\eta(v)$ is a number, and the product is also a number. Therefore, the left side $u \otimes v$ is actually a bilinear function defined on $U^* \times V^*$.

**This is the concrete construction of the tensor product of vector spaces**: defining $U \otimes V$ as the space of all bilinear functions defined on $U^* \times V^*$.

### 2.2 Verifying Bilinearity

Define the map $\otimes: U \times V \to U \otimes V$, $(u, v) \mapsto u \otimes v$. Verify that it is bilinear:

For any $u_1, u_2 \in U, v_1, v_2 \in V, \lambda, \mu \in K$:

$$((\lambda u_1 + \mu u_2) \otimes v)(\xi, \eta) = \xi(\lambda u_1 + \mu u_2) \cdot \eta(v) = (\lambda \xi(u_1) + \mu \xi(u_2)) \cdot \eta(v) = \lambda \xi(u_1)\eta(v) + \mu \xi(u_2)\eta(v) = \lambda (u_1 \otimes v)(\xi, \eta) + \mu (u_2 \otimes v)(\xi, \eta)$$

Thus $(\lambda u_1 + \mu u_2) \otimes v = \lambda(u_1 \otimes v) + \mu(u_2 \otimes v)$. Similarly, right linearity can be proven.

### 2.3 Construction of Bases

Now for the most crucial step: If $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m\}$ is a basis for $U$, and $\{\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n\}$ is a basis for $V$, does $\{\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j \mid 1 \leq i \leq m, 1 \leq j \leq n\}$ form a basis for $U \otimes V$?

**Proof**:

Take an arbitrary $T \in U \otimes V$ (i.e., $T: U^* \times V^* \to K$ is a bilinear function). For any $(\xi, \eta) \in U^* \times V^*$, let $\xi = \sum_i x_i \alpha_i^*, \eta = \sum_j y_j \beta_j^*$. By bilinearity:

$$T(\xi, \eta) = \sum_{i,j} x_i y_j T(\alpha_i^*, \beta_j^*)$$

Let $c_{ij} = T(\alpha_i^*, \beta_j^*)$, then:

$$T = \sum_{i,j} c_{ij} (\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j)$$

This is because for any $(\xi, \eta)$:

$$\left(\sum_{i,j} c_{ij} (\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j)\right)(\xi, \eta) = \sum_{i,j} c_{ij} \xi(\alpha_i) \eta(\beta_j) = \sum_{i,j} c_{i j} x_i y_j = T(\xi, \eta)$$

Thus, $\{\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j\}$ spans $U \otimes V$.

Next, proving linear independence: Assume $\sum_{i,j} d_{ij} (\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j) = 0$. Applying it to any $(\alpha_k^*, \beta_l^*)$:

$$\sum_{i,j} d_{ij} \alpha_k^*(\alpha_i) \beta_l^*(\beta_j) = \sum_{i,j} d_{ij} \delta_{ki} \delta_{lj} = d_{kl} = 0$$

Thus all coefficients are 0, so it is linearly independent.

**Conclusion**: $\{\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j\}$ forms a set of bases for $U \otimes V$, $\dim(U \otimes V) = mn$.

## III. Axiomatic Description: Universal Property

The construction above depends on the dual space, which encounters difficulties in the infinite-dimensional case. How can we characterize the tensor product directly from its "behavior" without relying on a specific construction?

### 3.1 Core Motivation: Linearizing Bilinearity

Traditional linear maps are the easiest to handle. However, the underlying space of a bilinear map $\psi: U \times V \to W$ is the Cartesian product $U \times V$, which is not linear (for example, $\psi(u_1 + u_2, v_1 + v_2)$ cannot be directly decomposed into simple linear terms).

We want an "intermediate" space that reduces all bilinear maps to ordinary linear maps on this intermediate space.

### 3.2 Universal Property

**Definition**: A linear space $T$ equipped with a bilinear map $\varphi: U \times V \to T$ is called the **tensor product** of $U, V$ if it satisfies:

For any linear space $W$ and any bilinear map $\psi: U \times V \to W$, there exists a **unique** linear map $\sigma: T \to W$ such that $\psi = \sigma \circ \varphi$.

Commutative Diagram:
```
        φ
U × V ──────→ T
  │            │
  │ ψ          │ σ (unique)
  ↓            ↓
  └──────→   W
```

Intuitive understanding: $(T, \varphi)$ is the "first stop" of all bilinear maps starting from $U \times V$. As long as we know the behavior at the first stop, we can recover the bilinear map $\psi$ to any destination space $W$ via the unique linear map $\sigma$.

**Theorem**: The tensor product is unique up to isomorphism.

**Proof**: Assume that both $(T_1, \varphi_1)$ and $(T_2, \varphi_2)$ satisfy the universal property.

Consider $(T_2, \varphi_2)$ as an arbitrary bilinear map, and $(T_1, \varphi_1)$ as the tensor product. By the universal property, there exists a unique linear map $\sigma: T_1 \to T_2$ such that $\varphi_2 = \sigma \circ \varphi_1$.

Conversely, there exists a unique linear map $\tau: T_2 \to T_1$ such that $\varphi_1 = \tau \circ \varphi_2$.

Substituting gives $\varphi_2 = \sigma \circ \tau \circ \varphi_2$ and $\varphi_1 = \tau \circ \sigma \circ \varphi_1$. By uniqueness, $\sigma \circ \tau = \mathrm{id}_{T_2}$ and $\tau \circ \sigma = \mathrm{id}_{T_1}$. Thus, $\sigma$ is isomorphic. $\square$

### 3.3 Existence Verification

In the finite-dimensional case, we have constructed the concrete model $U \otimes V = T_2(U^*, V^*)$ in §2. We verify that it satisfies the universal property:

Given a bilinear map $\psi: U \times V \to W$, take a basis $\{\alpha_i\}, \{\beta_j\}$ in $U, V$. Define $\sigma: U \otimes V \to W$ on the basis as:

$$\sigma(\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j) = \psi(\alpha_i, \beta_j)$$

By the theorem that a linear map is uniquely determined by its values on a basis, $\sigma$ exists and is unique. Verifying commutativity: $\sigma(\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j) = \psi(\alpha_i, \beta_j)$, so $\sigma \circ \varphi = \psi$ holds on the basis vectors, and by the linear extension principle, it holds on the entire space. $\square$

## IV. Tensor Product of Linear Transformations and the Kronecker Product

### 4.1 Definition of Operator Tensor Product

Let $A: U \to U', B: V \to V'$ be a linear transformation. Define their **tensor product map** $A \otimes B: U \otimes V \to U' \otimes V'$ as:

$$(A \otimes B)(u \otimes v) = Au \otimes Bv$$

and extend it to a linear map.

**Geometric Meaning**: $A \otimes B$ allows the two spaces to evolve independently—vectors in $U$ are acted upon by $A$, and vectors in $V$ are acted upon by $B$, with no interference between them.

### 4.2 Matrix Representation: Kronecker Product

After choosing a basis, the matrix of $A \otimes B$ is the **Kronecker product** of $A$ and $B$.

**Full Derivation**:

Let $A$ the matrix in the basis $\{\alpha_j\}$ and $\{\alpha'_p\}$ be $M$($A\alpha_j = \sum_p m_{pj} \alpha'_p$),$B$ the matrix in the basis $\{\beta_l\}$ and $\{\beta'_q\}$ be $N$($B\beta_l = \sum_q n_{ql} \beta'_q$).

Consider $U \otimes V$ with basis $\{\alpha_j \otimes \beta_l\}$ and $U' \otimes V'$ with basis $\{\alpha'_p \otimes \beta'_q\}$:

$$(A \otimes B)(\alpha_j \otimes \beta_l) = A\alpha_j \otimes B\beta_l = \left(\sum_p m_{pj} \alpha'_p\right) \otimes \left(\sum_q n_{ql} \beta'_q\right)$$

Using the bilinear expansion:

$$= \sum_{p=1}^{m'} \sum_{q=1}^{n'} (m_{pj} \cdot n_{ql}) (\alpha'_p \otimes \beta'_q)$$

The coefficient before the basis element $\alpha'_p \otimes \beta'_q$ is $m_{pj} \cdot n_{ql}$. After flattening the double index $(p, q)$ in lexicographical order, the matrix formed by these coefficients is exactly:

$$M \otimes N = \begin{pmatrix} m_{11}N & m_{12}N & \cdots & m_{1n}N \\ m_{21}N & m_{22}N & \cdots & m_{2n}N \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ m_{m1}N & m_{m2}N & \cdots & m_{mn}N \end{pmatrix}$$

where $m_{ij}N$ denotes the element-wise product of the scalar $m_{ij}$ and the matrix $N$. This is the **block definition of the Kronecker product**.

### 4.3 Core Properties of the Kronecker Product

**Property 1 (Factorability of Rank)**: $\mathrm{rank}(A \otimes B) = \mathrm{rank}(A) \cdot \mathrm{rank}(B)$.

**Proof**: Let $\mathrm{rank}(A) = r, \mathrm{rank}(B) = s$. There exists an invertible matrix $P, Q, S, T$ such that $PAQ = \begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $SBT = \begin{pmatrix} I_s & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$.

Using the associative property $(P \otimes S)(A \otimes B)(Q \otimes T) = (PAQ) \otimes (SBT)$. Since $P, Q, S, T$ is invertible, $P \otimes S$ and $Q \otimes T$ are also invertible, and the change of basis does not change the rank. After expanding the right side, there are exactly $r \cdot s$ diagonal elements equal to 1, and the rest are 0. Thus, $\mathrm{rank}(A \otimes B) = rs$. $\square$

**Property 2 (Complete Factorability of Eigenvalues)**: If the eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$, and the eigenvalues of $B$ are $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$, then the eigenvalues of $A \otimes B$ are $\{\lambda_i \mu_j \mid 1 \leq i \leq m, 1 \leq j \leq n\}$ (a total of $mn$, counted with multiplicity).

**Property 3 (Trace and Determinant)**: $\mathrm{tr}(A \otimes B) = \mathrm{tr}(A) \cdot \mathrm{tr}(B)$, $\det(A \otimes B) = (\det A)^n \cdot (\det B)^m$.

## V. Properties of Pure Tensors

### 5.1 Separable vs. Non-separable

A tensor of the form $u \otimes v$ is called a **separable tensor** (or pure tensor). However, a general tensor is a form of $\sum u_i \otimes v_i$.

**Proposition**: Let $\dim U = m, \dim V = n$. If $m, n > 1$, then not all vectors in $U \otimes V$ can be written in the form of a single $u \otimes v$.

**Derivation (Extension to a Basis)**:

Let $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ be linearly independent, and $\beta_1, \beta_2$ be linearly independent. Consider $T = \alpha_1 \otimes \beta_1 + \alpha_2 \otimes \beta_2$. Assume $T = u \otimes v$.

Extend $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ to a basis of $U$, and extend $\beta_1, \beta_2$ to a basis of $V$. Let $u = \sum u_i \alpha_i, v = \sum v_j \beta_j$. Then:

$$u \otimes v = \sum_{i,j} u_i v_j (\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j)$$

Comparing coefficients with $T$: $u_1 v_1 = 1$, $u_1 v_2 = 0$, $u_2 v_1 = 0$, $u_2 v_2 = 1$.

From $u_1 v_1 = 1$, we get $u_1 \neq 0, v_1 \neq 0$. From $u_1 v_2 = 0$, we get $v_2 = 0$. From $u_2 v_1 = 0$, we get $u_2 = 0$. But $u_2 v_2 = 0 \neq 1$, a contradiction.

**Understanding**: The degrees of freedom of the separable tensor $u \otimes v$ are $m + n - 1$, while the dimension of the full space is $mn$. When $m, n > 1$, we have $mn > m + n - 1$, so the separable tensor is just a low-dimensional subspace within the full space. In physics, separable tensors correspond to independent states, while non-separable tensors correspond to **quantum entangled states**.

### 5.2 Non-zero Divisor Property

**Proposition**: If $u \otimes v = 0$, then $u = 0$ or $v = 0$.

**Derivation**: Proof by contradiction. Assume $u \neq 0, v \neq 0$. Extend $u$ to the first basis element of $U$, and extend $v$ to the first basis element of $V$. Then $u \otimes v$ is an element of the full space basis and cannot be the zero vector. Contradiction. $\square$

### 5.3 Separability Criterion

**Proposition**: $\alpha_1 \otimes \beta_1 + \alpha_2 \otimes \beta_2$ can be written as a single tensor product $u \otimes v$ if and only if $\alpha_1, \alpha_2$ is linearly dependent or $\beta_1, \beta_2$ is linearly dependent.

## VI. Tensor Product of Subspaces

**Theorem**: Let $U_0 \subseteq U, V_0 \subseteq V$ be a subspace. Let $W_0 = \mathrm{span}\{u \otimes v \mid u \in U_0, v \in V_0\} \subseteq U \otimes V$, then $W_0 \cong U_0 \otimes V_0$, and $\dim W_0 = \dim U_0 \cdot \dim V_0$.

**Proof**:

Take a basis $\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$ in $U_0$, and a basis $\{v_1, \dots, v_s\}$ in $V_0$. Extend both separately to a basis of $U, V$.

From §2.3, $\{u_p \otimes v_q \mid 1 \leq p \leq m, 1 \leq q \leq n\}$ is a basis for $U \otimes V$. Since $\{u_i \otimes v_j \mid 1 \leq i \leq r, 1 \leq j \leq s\}$ is a subset of this basis, it is linearly independent.

And these $rs$ vectors span exactly $W_0$, so they form a basis for $W_0$, $\dim W_0 = rs$. $\square$

## VII. Application: Sylvester Transform and Matrix Equations

### 7.1 Flattening Transform

Let $A \in M_{m \times m}(K), B \in M_{n \times n}(K)$. Define the linear transformation $\mathcal{S}: M_{m \times n}(K) \to M_{m \times n}(K)$ as:

$$\mathcal{S}(X) = AX - XB$$

Using the **column flattening** $\mathrm{vec}(X)$ (flattening the matrix into a column vector), the classical formula is:

$$\mathrm{vec}(AX) = (I_n \otimes A)\mathrm{vec}(X), \quad \mathrm{vec}(XB) = (B^T \otimes I_m)\mathrm{vec}(X)$$

Thus, the matrix corresponding to $\mathcal{S}$ is:

$$\mathrm{Mat}(\mathcal{S}) = I_n \otimes A - B^T \otimes I_m$$

### 7.2 Eigenvalues and Existence of Solutions

Let the eigenvalues of $A$ be $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$, and the eigenvalues of $B$ be $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_n$ (with the same eigenvalues as $B^T$ and $B$).

By Schur decomposition, $A$ and $B^T$ can be separately similar to upper triangular matrices, whose diagonal elements are $\lambda_i$ and $\mu_j$, respectively. Then $I_n \otimes A - B^T \otimes I_m$ is similar to an upper triangular matrix with diagonal elements $\lambda_i - \mu_j$.

**Eigenvalues of $\mathcal{S}$**: $\lambda_i - \mu_j, \ 1 \leq i \leq m, \ 1 \leq j \leq n$ (a total of $mn$).

**Corollary**: The equation $AX = XB$ has a non-trivial solution if and only if $A, B$ has common eigenvalues.

**Proof**: The non-trivial solution $\Leftrightarrow \ker \mathcal{S} \neq \{0\} \Leftrightarrow 0$ is the eigenvalue $\Leftrightarrow \exists i, j$ of $\mathcal{S}$ that makes $\lambda_i = \mu_j$. $\square$
