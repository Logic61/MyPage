# Introduction to Rings
## Formal Definition

Let $ R $ be a non-empty set, and define two binary operations on it: **addition** (denoted as $+$) and **multiplication** (denoted as $\cdot $). If the following three conditions are met, then $(R, +, \cdot)$ is called a ring:

### I. Addition is a commutative group (Abelian group)

1. **Associativity**: $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$
    
2. **Commutativity**: $ a + b = b + a $ 3. **Identity element (zero element)**: There exists $ 0 \in R $, such that for any $ a \in R $, $ 0 + a = a $.
    
4. **Inverse element (negative element)**: For any $ a \in R $, there exists $-a \in R $, such that $ a + (-a) = 0 $.
    

### II. Multiplication is a semigroup

1. **Associativity**: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$_Note: The definition of a basic ring does not mandate that multiplication must be commutative, nor does it mandate a multiplicative identity element (identity element $ 1 $)._
    

### III. Distributive Law

Multiplication satisfies the left and right distributive laws over addition:

1. $ a \cdot (b + c) = (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c)$ 2.$(a + b) \cdot c = (a \cdot c) + (b \cdot c)$

What are we discussing this structure for? Mainly for abstraction. If the conclusions of integers can be applied to polynomials, we only need to discuss a certain ring, and then similar problems can be solved in other rings using similar methods, so overall it is much easier.

Similar to integers, we define polynomial divisibility, considering the number field $ K $ polynomials over $ K[x]$, and define **divisibility**$ f|g $, if there exists a polynomial $ q $ that satisfies $ g=qf $ . 

# Number Theoretic Properties of Polynomials
## Division with Remainder
Similarly to integers, we can also consider the division with remainder of polynomials. What we hope for is:

> For any polynomial $ f,g $, there exists a polynomial $ q,r $ such that $ g=qf+r $.

At this point, it is not convenient to say the so-called $ r < f $ like in the ring of integers. How can we determine the 'size' of a polynomial? We can make this 'size' more abstract, and let the degree of the polynomial be the definition. The degree refers to the highest power of the polynomial, and is abbreviated in English as $ deg(f)$ .

Then, completely speaking, the division with remainder should be:

> For any polynomials $ f,g $ and $ f\neq 0 $, there exists a polynomial $ q,r $ such that $ g=qf+r $, where $ deg(r) < deg(f)$ .

Does it exist? And is it unique? We can discuss similarly to integers:

>[!Existence Proof]
>We fix $ f $, and use the second mathematical induction on the degree of $ g $.
>
>1. If the degree of $ g $ is less than $ f $, then we can directly let $ q=0,r=g $ be it. Now consider the case where the degree is less than $ k $. Then we consider the case where the degree is $ k $.
>2. The degree of $ g $ is greater than or equal to the degree of $ f $. We can assume that the leading term of $ f $ is $ ax^n $, and the leading term of $ g $ is $ bx^m $. Then $ g-f\cdot \frac{b}{a}x^{m-n}$ is a polynomial of degree $ m-1 $. By the induction hypothesis, the division with remainder exists, $ g-f\cdot \frac{b}{a}x^{m-n} = qf+r $, and after some rearrangement, we get $ g=(\frac{b}{a}x^{m-n}+q)f+r $, which satisfies the form of the division with remainder, so it holds.
>Combining the two cases above proves the original proposition.

>[!Uniqueness Proof]
>Using proof by contradiction.
>Let's assume it is not unique, so there are $ q_1f+r_1=q_2f+r_2 $, which transforms to $(q_1-q_2)f=r_2-r_1 $. If $ q_1=q_2 $, then $ r_2=r_1 $ also holds, which proves uniqueness. Otherwise, consider the degrees of both sides of the equation. The degree of the left side is $ deg(f)+deg(q_2-q_1)\ge deg(f)$, and the degree of the right side is $ deg(r_2-r_1)\le deg(r_2)<deg(f)$. This leads to a contradiction.

## Factorization
In the ring of integers, we have unique prime factorization, and in the polynomial ring, there is a similar operation. We define an **irreducible polynomial** as a polynomial that can only be divided by a constant polynomial or its associate. This is similar to the definition of a prime number. So, is there a unique irreducible factorization?

First, we derive some properties of irreducible polynomials.

**One**   
Consider the irreducible polynomial $ p $, and an arbitrary polynomial $ f $, then $ p|f \text{or} (p,f)=1 $. This is easy to derive from the definition.

**Two**
If $ p|fg $, then $ p|f \text{or} p|g $. Proof: If $ p|f $, it is already established, otherwise $ gcd(p,f)=1 $, then $ up+vf=1 $, and multiplying by $ g $ gives $ upg+vfg=g $, so $ p|\text{left} $, and thus $ p|g $.

**Three**
$ p $ is irreducible if and only if $ p $ cannot be factored into the product of two non-zero polynomials of lower degree.
Necessity: If $ p $ is irreducible, it obviously cannot be factored.
Sufficiency: Assume that $ p $ is reducible, then let $ p=fg $, so $ deg(p)=deg(f)+deg(g)$. Since $ deg(f),deg(g)\neq 0 $, the degrees of the two are clearly smaller than $ p $, which contradicts the condition. Thus, $ p $ is irreducible.

With the above, it is easy to prove the unique factorization theorem:

> $ K[x]$ The polynomial $ 0 $ with degree greater than $ f $ can be uniquely factored into $ K $ a product of several irreducible polynomials.

Proof:
First, we prove existence using the second mathematical induction.
Base case: It is obvious that when the degree is $ 1 $, it can be factored into itself.
Inductive step: Assume that the case for degrees less than $ k $ holds, and consider the case for $ deg(f)=k $. If $ f $ is irreducible, it can be factored into itself, otherwise, using property **Three** above, we get $ f=gh $, where $ g,h $ are all polynomials with degrees less than $ f $. Using the inductive hypothesis, we get that $ g,h $ can be factored, so substituting gives the irreducible polynomial factorization of $ f $.

Then we prove uniqueness. We use proof by contradiction.
Assume there are two factorizations: $ f=p_1p_2...p_n=q_1q_2...q_m $. Then we have $ q_1|p_1p_2...p_n $, and thus $ q_1|p_i $. We can order $ p $ arbitrarily, so that $ q_1|p_1 $. Then because they are all irreducible, $ q_1,p_1 $, we consider them to be equal in the sense of associates, and for convenience, we write an equals sign instead of changing the symbols, so we cancel them to get: $ p_2...p_n=q_2...q_m $. Repeating this, let's assume $ n > m $ and $ q_m=p_m...p_n $, then $ q_m $ is reducible, a contradiction, which gives $ n\le m $, and similarly for the other side, so $ n=m $, and the irreducible polynomials on both sides are equal in the sense of associates.

In summary, we have completed the proof. The polynomial $ f $ can always be decomposed into:

$$
f=ap_1^{e_1}\cdot\cdot\cdot p_n^{e_n}
$$

## Chinese Remainder Theorem
Theorem:
Let $ P_1(t), P_2(t), \dots, P_k(t)$ is a field $ F $ two pairwise coprime polynomials over. For any given polynomial $ A_1(t), A_2(t), \dots, A_k(t)$, there must exist a unique polynomial $ f(t)$ satisfying:

$$
f(t) \equiv A_i(t) \pmod{P_i(t)}, \quad i=1, 2, \dots, k
$$

and under the condition of degree $\deg(f) < \sum \deg(P_i)$ the solution is unique.

A constructive proof can be written very briefly:

Let $ M(t) = \prod P_i(t)$,$ M_i(t) = \frac{M(t)}{P_i(t)}$.

Because $ M_i(t)$ and $ P_i(t)$ are coprime, using the **Extended Euclidean Algorithm** we can find $ N_i(t)$ such that:

$$
M_i(t) N_i(t) \equiv 1 \pmod{P_i(t)}
$$

The final solution is:

$$
f(t) = \sum_{i=1}^k A_i(t) M_i(t) N_i(t) \pmod{M(t)}
$$

$ Q.E.D $

# Polynomial Functions
## Lagrange Interpolation Formula and the Inverse of the Vandermonde Matrix

Let $ K $ is a number field,$ c_0, c_1, \dots, c_n \in K $ distinct. Then for any $ b_0, b_1, \dots, b_n \in K $, there exists a unique polynomial of degree $\le n $ the polynomial $ f \in K[x]$, such that:

$$
f(c_i) = b_i, \quad \forall \ 0 \le i \le n
$$

### 1. Derivation in Matrix Form

Let $ f(x) = a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n $. According to the interpolation conditions, we get the linear system of equations:

$$
\begin{cases} f(c_0) = a_0 + a_1c_0 + \dots + a_nc_0^n = b_0 \\ f(c_1) = a_0 + a_1c_1 + \dots + a_nc_1^n = b_1 \\ \vdots \\ f(c_n) = a_0 + a_1c_n + \dots + a_nc_n^n = b_n \end{cases}
$$

This system of equations is equivalent to the matrix multiplication:

$$
\underbrace{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & c_0 & \cdots & c_0^n \\ 1 & c_1 & \cdots & c_1^n \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & c_n & \cdots & c_n^n \end{bmatrix} }_{\text{Vandermonde matrix } A} \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_0 \\ b_1 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

by $ c_0, c_1, \dots, c_n $ distinct, we know that the Vandermonde matrix $ A $ invertible**.

### 2. Determination of Coefficients

Thus, the polynomial $ f(x) = a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n $ exists and is determined by $ b_0, \dots, b_n $ uniquely:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & c_0 & \cdots & c_0^n \\ 1 & c_1 & \cdots & c_1^n \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & c_n & \cdots & c_n^n \end{bmatrix}^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} b_0 \\ b_1 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

In particular, taking $ b_0 = \dots = b_n = 0 $by contradiction, we get:**$ n $ a polynomial (function) has at most $ n $ roots in any field.**

### 3. Using the Adjugate Matrix Expansion

Using the inverse matrix formula $ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|} A^*$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{|A|} \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{21} & \cdots & A_{n+1,1} \\ A_{12} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{n+1,2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ A_{1,n+1} & A_{2,n+1} & \cdots & A_{n+1,n+1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_0 \\ b_1 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

to $ f(x)$ Write as a product of row and column vectors:

$$
f(x) = [1 \ x \ \cdots \ x^n] \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

Substituting the coefficient vector:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{|A|} [1 \ x \ \cdots \ x^n] \begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{21} & \cdots & A_{n+1,1} \\ A_{12} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{n+1,2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ A_{1,n+1} & A_{2,n+1} & \cdots & A_{n+1,n+1} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_0 \\ b_1 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

Further simplification:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{i=0}^{n} b_i (A_{i+1,1} + A_{i+1,2}x + \dots + A_{i+1,n+1}x^n)
$$

Observe the part in the parentheses, it is exactly the form of the determinant expanded along the $ i+1 $ row:

$$
= \frac{1}{|A|} \sum_{i=0}^{n} b_i \begin{vmatrix} 1 & c_0 & \cdots & c_0^n \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x & \cdots & x^n \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & c_n & \cdots & c_n^n \end{vmatrix} \leftarrow \text{the } i+1 \text{ row expansion}
$$

### 4. Lagrange Interpolation Formula

Finally, using the Vandermonde determinant again, we get the classic Lagrange interpolation formula form:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} b_j \frac{\prod_{k \neq j} (x - c_k)}{\prod_{k \neq j} (c_j - c_k)}
$$

## Roots of a polynomial, polynomial function
What was discussed before, besides the Lagrange interpolation part, is not actually the **polynomial function** in the traditional sense, but rather a $ x->f(x)$ mapping, but a formal polynomial, which is just the form of coefficients and powers. The differentiation before was also just formal differentiation, of course, exactly the same as the differentiation of a function. Because everything is so intuitive, we just haven't discussed these things.

The polynomials before should be defined as follows, a sequence of symbols of the form $ a_0+a_1x+...+a_nx^n $, and two polynomials are equal if and only if every $ a $ is equal. The definition of the formal derivative is defined to look like the derivative of a function.

Why the distinction? In the number fields we are usually familiar with, there is not a big difference between the two, and it can also be verified that polynomial functions also form a unique factorization domain. But in a **finite field**, for example, in a field modulo $ 2 $, it is different. This field only has $\{0,1\}$, so $ x^2 $ and $ x $ are actually the same if we consider functions, as they are congruent modulo $ 2 $, and are equal in this field. But for formal polynomials, according to the definition, they are not the same.

In infinite fields, there exists a bijection between them, so we can safely treat them according to intuition.

- **Injectivity (Uniqueness)**:
    
If two polynomial functions $ P(x)$ and $ Q(x)$ are equal, then their difference $ D(x) = P(x) - Q(x)$ is equal to 0 at infinitely many points.
    
According to the algebraic principle (which will be explained shortly), a non-zero polynomial of degree $ n $ has at most $ n $ roots. If it has infinitely many roots, then it must be the **zero polynomial** (i.e., all coefficients are 0). Therefore, the coefficients of $ P(x)$ and $ Q(x)$ must be completely the same.
    
- **Surjectivity**:
    
    By definition, every polynomial function is induced by some formal polynomial.

## Primitive Polynomial

### 1. Definition and Properties

**Primitive Polynomial**: If the greatest common divisor of the coefficients of all terms of the integer coefficient polynomial $ f(x)$ is 1, then $ f(x)$ is called a primitive polynomial.

**Example**:

$$
3x^5 + \frac{15}{4}x^2 + \frac{9}{2}x + \frac{45}{8} \in \mathbb{Q}[x]
$$

It can be factored by extracting a common factor as:

$$
= \frac{3}{8}(8x^5 + 10x^2 + 12x + 15)
$$

The part in the parentheses is the primitive polynomial.

---

### 2. Related Lemmas and Theorems

- **Lemma**: In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, the primitive polynomial $ g(x), h(x)$**is associated** with $ g(x) = \pm h(x)$ if and only if.
    
- **Theorem**: In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, every non-zero polynomial $ f(x)$ can be uniquely written as:

$$
f(x) = k \cdot g(x)
$$

where $ k $ is a rational number, and $ g(x)$ is a primitive polynomial with leading coefficient $> 0 $.
    

---

### 3. Gauss's Lemma and its Proof

**Lemma (Gauss)**: **The product of primitive polynomials is still a primitive polynomial.**

**Proof**: Let $ f = a_0 + a_1x + \dots + a_nx^n $ and $ g = b_0 + b_1x + \dots + b_mx^m $.

Since $ f, g $ is a primitive polynomial, that is, $(a_0, \dots, a_n) = (b_0, \dots, b_m) = 1 $.

We want to prove that the greatest common divisor of the coefficients of $ fg $ is still 1.

Otherwise, there exists a prime number $ p $ that divides every coefficient of $ fg $.

Let $ a_s, b_t $ be the coefficient of the highest degree term in $ f, g $ that is **not divisible by $ p $**:

- $ f = a_0 + \dots + a_s x^s + \dots + a_n x^n $ (where $ a_{s+1} \dots a_n $ is a multiple of $ p $)
    
- $ g = b_0 + \dots + b_t x^t + \dots + b_m x^m $ (where $ b_{t+1} \dots b_m $ is a multiple of $ p $)
    

Consider the coefficient of the $ x^{s+t}$ term in $ fg $:

In the composition of this coefficient, $ p $ does not divide $ a_s b_t $, but $ p $ divides all other terms (such as $ a_{s+1}b_{t-1} + \dots $).

This leads to $ p $ not dividing the coefficient of the $ x^{s+t}$ term, which contradicts the assumption that “$ p $ divides every coefficient of $ fg $”!

---

### 4. Decomposition Relationship between $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[x]$

In $\mathbb{Q}[x]$, there is a polynomial (irreducible) decomposition:

$$
f(x) = g(x)h(x) \dots
$$

By extracting the coefficients, this is equivalent to a primitive (irreducible) decomposition of a primitive polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$:

$$
f_1(x) = g_1(x)h_1(x) \dots
$$

> **Core Logic**: When dealing with irreducibility, it is sufficient to treat the following two perspectives as equivalent.

---

### 5. Deduction

#### 1
Every primitive polynomial of degree $\ge 1 $ can be uniquely written as a product of **primitive irreducible polynomials**:

$$
f(x) = \pm g_1(x)^{e_1} g_2(x)^{e_2} \dots g_r(x)^{e_r}
$$

#### 2
If the primitive polynomial $ g(x) = b_m x^m + \dots + b_0 $ divides the integer coefficient polynomial $ f(x) = a_n x^n + \dots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, then:

$$
b_m \mid a_n, \quad b_0 \mid a_0
$$

**Derivation Idea**:

Let $ f(x) = g(x) \cdot q(x)$. Since $ g(x)$ is a primitive polynomial and $ f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, by the deduction of Gauss's Lemma, $ q(x)$ must also be an integer coefficient polynomial.

Let $ q(x) = c_t x^t + \dots + c_0 $, then:

$$
(b_m x^m + \dots + b_0)(c_t x^t + \dots + c_0) = a_n x^n + \dots + a_0
$$

By comparing the coefficients of the highest degree term and the constant term:

- Highest degree term: $ a_n = b_m \cdot c_t \implies b_m \mid a_n $
    
- Constant term: $ a_0 = b_0 \cdot c_0 \implies b_0 \mid a_0 $
    

#### 3
If $ c/d $ (coprime to $ c, d $) is an integer coefficient polynomial

$$
f(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_0
$$

with a rational root, i.e., $(dx - c) \mid f(x)$, then:

$$
d \mid a_n, \quad c \mid a_0
$$

**Core Conclusion**:

- **The denominator $ d $** must be a factor of the leading coefficient $ a_n $.
    
- **The numerator $ c $** must be a factor of the constant term coefficient $ a_0 $.

This can be used to test for rational roots; by using divisibility, we obtain a finite number of possible rational roots, which can then be tested.

# Test for Reducibility
## Eisenstein's Criterion (p-Eisenstein Polynomial)

### Definition and Theorem

Let $ p $ be a prime number. If the integer coefficient polynomial

$$
f(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0
$$

satisfies the following three conditions:

1. $ p \nmid a_n $ ($ p $ does not divide the leading coefficient)
    
2. $ p \mid a_i \quad (i = 0, 1, \dots, n-1)$ ($ p $ divides all coefficients except the leading one)
    
3. $ p^2 \nmid a_0 $ ($ p^2 $ does not divide the constant term)
    

then $ f(x)$ is **irreducible** in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

### Proof Idea (Proof by Contradiction)

#### Step 1: Reduce to the ring of integers

By Gauss's Lemma, a primitive polynomial is reducible over $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ if and only if it is reducible over $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

Suppose that $ f(x)$ is reducible, then we can write:

$$
f(x) = G(x)H(x)
$$

where $ G(x) = \sum_{i=0}^s b_i x^i $, $ H(x) = \sum_{j=0}^t c_j x^j $, and $ s, t \ge 1, s+t=n $.

#### Step 2: Using the constant term to lock the distribution of $ p $

Observing the constant term: $ a_0 = b_0 c_0 $.

- Condition (2) says that $ p \mid a_0 $.
    
- Condition (3) says that $ p^2 \nmid a_0 $.
    
    This means that among $ b_0 $ and $ c_0 $, **exactly one** is divisible by $ p $. Let's assume $ p \mid b_0 $ and $ p \nmid c_0 $.
    

#### Step 3: Obtaining a contradiction

Next, consider the source of $ a_1 $; it is not difficult to see that it is $ b_1c_0+b_0c_1 $. Thus, using what we obtained above, we get $ p \mid b_1 $, and by induction, we will derive $\forall i, p\mid b_i $, which leads to $ p\mid b_sc_t =a_n $, contradicting the condition.

Therefore, the initial assumption of factorization is false, and $ f(x)$ is irreducible.

---

### Example: Proof of irreducibility of the cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_p(x)$

For $ f(x) = x^{p-1} + x^{p-2} + \dots + 1 $, looking directly at the coefficients, they are all $ 1 $, which completely fails to meet Eisenstein's criteria. So we need to perform a **coordinate shift**.

#### Core technique: Let $ x = y + 1 $. We construct a new polynomial $ g(y) = f(y+1)$. The motivation for the shift is the form of a geometric series (geometric progression).

Using the formula for the sum of a geometric series: $ f(x) = \frac{x^p - 1}{x - 1}$, substituting gives:

$$
g(y) = \frac{(y+1)^p - 1}{(y+1) - 1} = \frac{(y+1)^p - 1}{y}
$$

#### Analysis of the expanded terms

Using the binomial theorem to expand $(y+1)^p $:

$$
(y+1)^p = y^p + \binom{p}{1}y^{p-1} + \dots + \binom{p}{p-1}y + 1
$$

Subtracting $ 1 $ and dividing by $ y $, we get:

$$
g(y) = y^{p-1} + \binom{p}{1}y^{p-2} + \binom{p}{2}y^{p-3} + \dots + \binom{p}{p-1}
$$

#### Verification of conditions

Now, we apply Eisenstein's criterion (for the prime $ p $) to $ g(y)$:

1. **Leading coefficient**: is $ 1 $, which is clearly $ p \nmid 1 $.
    
2. **Intermediate coefficients**: are $\binom{p}{k}$, $ 1 \le k < p $.
    
    According to the binomial coefficient formula $\binom{p}{k} = \frac{p!}{k!(p-k)!}$, since $ p $ is a prime number and the denominator does not contain the factor $ p $, all these coefficients are divisible by $ p $.
    
3. **Constant term**: is $\binom{p}{p-1} = p $. It is clearly $ p \mid p $ and $ p^2 \nmid p $.
    

**Conclusion**: $ g(y)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Because the shift transformation does not change the reducibility of the polynomial, the original polynomial $ f(x)$ is also irreducible.

## By determining reducibility modulo

**If it cannot be factored in an even simpler world, it is even less likely to be factored in a complex world.**

**Criterion Theorem:**

Let $ f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, whose leading coefficient is not divisible by the prime $ p $.

If the polynomial $\bar{f}(x)$, obtained by taking modulo in the residue class field $\mathbb{F}_p $ modulo $ p $, is **irreducible**, and the degree of $\bar{f}(x)$ is the same as $ f(x)$, then $ f(x)$ must also be **irreducible** over $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

> **Critical Evaluation:**
> 
> - **Advantage**: Extremely effective when coefficients are very large or when Eisenstein fails. In the world modulo $ 2 $, there are only $ 0 $ and $ 1 $, and factorization becomes a kind of 'puzzle game,' drastically reducing the computational load.
>     
> - **Risk**: This is only a **one-way street**. If $\bar{f}(x)$ is **reducible** modulo $ p $, you **cannot** conclude that $ f(x)$ is reducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. For example, $ x^2 + 1 $ is $(x+1)^2 $ modulo $\mathbb{F}_2 $, but it is clearly irreducible modulo $\mathbb{Q}$.
>     

The proof is actually very clear, we want the contrapositive: if $ f $ on $\mathbb{Z}$ is reducible$\implies $ $\bar{f}$ on $\mathbb{F}_p $ is reducible (the premise is that the degree does not drop after taking the modulus, i.e., $ p \nmid a_n $) ). This is obviously true, it's just taking the modulus on both sides, so the contrapositive also holds, which is quite useful, we can also write a slightly formalized proof:
### Core Proof

**1. Preparation: The escort of Gauss's Lemma**

Similarly, by **Gauss's Lemma**, we only need to prove that $ f(x)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.

Assume that $ f(x)$ is reducible over $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, then there exist two polynomials with integer coefficients, $ g(x), h(x)$, whose degrees are both greater than $ 0 $, such that:

$$
f(x) = g(x)h(x)
$$

Let $\deg(g) = r, \deg(h) = s $, then $ r+s = n $.

**2. Projection to a Finite Field**

Consider the homomorphism modulo $ p $, $\phi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{F}_p $. This map can naturally induce a map between polynomial rings $\bar{\phi}: \mathbb{Z}[x] \to \mathbb{F}_p[x]$.

Applying it to both sides of the equation:

$$
\bar{f}(x) = \bar{g}(x)\bar{h}(x)
$$

**3. Examination of Degree Preservation**

Observing the left side of the equation: Since we know $ p \nmid a_n $, the leading coefficient of $\bar{f}(x)$ is $\bar{a}_n \neq 0 $. This implies:

$$
\deg(\bar{f}) = n
$$

Observing the right side of the equation: Since $\deg(\bar{g}) \le r $ and $\deg(\bar{h}) \le s $, and their product's degree must equal $ n $ (i.e., $ r+s $), this forces:

$$
\deg(\bar{g}) = r > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \deg(\bar{h}) = s > 0
$$

**4. Deriving a Contradiction**

We find that $\bar{f}(x)$ is decomposed into two lower-degree polynomials, $\bar{g}(x)$ and $\bar{h}(x)$, in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$.

This directly contradicts the hypothesis of the theorem—that "$\bar{f}(x)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$".

**Conclusion:** The assumption is false, so $ f(x)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, and consequently, it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}[x]$.

Suppose we want to determine if $ f(x) = x^4 + x + 1 $ is irreducible.

This polynomial does not satisfy Eisenstein's criterion (there is no suitable $ p $). Let's try modulo $ 2 $:

In $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$, the coefficients of $ f(x)$ are only $ 0 $ and $ 1 $.

1. **Checking for linear factors (roots)**:
    
    - $\bar{f}(0) = 1 $-$\bar{f}(1) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 1 $
        
There are no roots, which means there are no linear factors.
        
2. **Checking for quadratic factors**:
    
The only quadratic irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ is $ x^2 + x + 1 $.
    
Let's calculate it: $(x^2 + x + 1)^2 = x^4 + x^2 + 1 \neq x^4 + x + 1 $.
    

Since there are no linear factors and no quadratic factors, $\bar{f}(x)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}_2 $.

**Conclusion:** $ x^4 + x + 1 $ is absolutely irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$.
