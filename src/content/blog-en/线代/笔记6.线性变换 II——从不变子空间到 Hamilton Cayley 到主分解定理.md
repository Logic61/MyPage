# Invariant Subspaces

For non-diagonalizable matrices, we also hope to have a dissection tool similar to diagonalization, to cut the matrix into non-interfering pieces, so that we can clearly see the structure. For example, the eigenspaces are mutually non-interfering and are closed as subspaces.

We hope to abstract a relatively general subspace from such spaces, which is the so-called invariant subspace:

Let $\mathscr{A} \in Hom(V)$ and let $ W $ be a subspace of $ V $. If $\forall \alpha \in W, \mathscr{A}\alpha \in W $, then $ W $ is invariant under $ A $. Examples include $ V $, $ \{0\} $, $ ImA,KerA $, and the generalized eigenspaces.

### Properties

Recall that we proved before that if two diagonalizable matrices commute, they are simultaneously diagonalizable, which actually implies something deeper.

#### If the linear transformations $ A,B $ commute, then $ KerB,ImB $ are invariant under $ A $.

Let

$$
AB=BA
$$

Consider $ B\alpha=0 $ then $ B(A\alpha)=A(B\alpha)=0 $, therefore $ A\alpha\in KerB $ .
Consider $\alpha=B\beta $then $ A(B\beta)=B(A\beta)\in ImB $ .

Furthermore, there are some simple linear relationships.

#### The intersection and sum of invariant subspaces are invariant subspaces

Let $ U,W $ be invariant subspaces of $ A $.

Take $\alpha\in U+W $,
then $\alpha=u+w $apply it $ A $ obtain $ A\alpha=Au+Aw $by the condition $ Au,Aw $ is still in the original space, so $ A\alpha\in U+W $ .
Take $\alpha\in U \cap W $,
then $\alpha\in U\implies A\alpha\in U $similarly $ A\alpha\in W $so $ A\alpha\in U\cap W $ .

# Cyclic Subspace

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a linear transformation on the $ K $-vector space $ V $, with $ 0 ≠ α \in V $. **Consider the vectors associated with $α$ under $\mathcal{A}$.**

there must exist an integer $ r ≥ 1 $, such that

$$
α, \mathcal{A}α, \dots, \mathcal{A}^{r-1}α
$$

linearly independent
and

$$
α, \mathcal{A}α, \dots, \mathcal{A}^{r-1}α, \mathcal{A}^rα
$$

are linearly dependent. Then notice this form; if we apply $\mathcal{A}$ again, it will naturally be a similar form. For higher powers, it can always be obtained from a linear dependence relation, so we construct an invariant subspace.

For the linear dependence above, we have $ b_1, \cdots, b_r \in K $, such that

$$
\mathcal{A}^r\alpha + b_1\mathcal{A}^{r-1}\alpha + \cdots + b_r\alpha = 0 .
$$

let $ g(x) = x^r + b_1x^{r-1} + \cdots + b_r $, then

$$
g(\mathcal{A})\alpha = 0 .
$$

$ W = \langle \alpha, \mathcal{A}\alpha, \cdots, \mathcal{A}^{r-1}\alpha \rangle $ is the smallest $\alpha $ subspace $\mathcal{A}$containing $\alpha $ the span of$\mathcal{A}$the sequence

If $\mathcal{A}^r\alpha = 0 $it is called $ W $ the **strong cyclic subspace**.
From here, we can actually easily prove the Hamilton Cayley Theorem. First, let's consider the following lemma.

# Hamilton Cayley Theorem

We have that the characteristic polynomial of the restricted transformation divides the original characteristic polynomial. Without loss of generality, let $ g(x)$ be the characteristic polynomial of the restricted transformation,$ f(x)$ be the original characteristic polynomial, then we have $ f(x)=h(x)g(x)$, $ A $(ring isomorphism) then we have $ f(A)\alpha=h(A)g(A)\alpha $, let this $ g $ corresponding $\alpha $ cyclic subspace, then we get the equality equals $ 0 $ .

In other words, $\forall \alpha \in V, f(A)\alpha=0 $which shows that $ Kerf(A)$ spans $ V $only possible $ f(A)=0 $ This is what is called the Hamilton Cayley theorem, the characteristic polynomial of a matrix is the annihilating polynomial of the matrix.

# Minimal Polynomial

### 1. Concept of the Minimal Polynomial

Among all annihilating polynomials of the linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$, the polynomial with the **lowest degree and leading coefficient of one** is called the minimal polynomial of $\mathcal{A}$, denoted by $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$ or $ m(x)$.

> Knowing $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$, one has mastered all annihilating polynomials of $\mathcal{A}$.

---

### 2. Properties of the Minimal Polynomial

**Proposition 1**: Let $ g(x) \in K[x]$. Then

$ g(\mathcal{A}) = 0 $ if and only if $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(x) \mid g(x)$- **Proof Idea**: Let $ g(x) = q(x)m_{\mathcal{A}}(x) + r(x)$, where $\deg r(x) < \deg m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$.
    
- Then $ r(\mathcal{A}) = 0 $. This implies $ r(x) = 0 $.
    
- **Corollary**: The minimal polynomial $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$ of $\mathcal{A}$ is unique; and $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$ divides the characteristic polynomial $ f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$ of $\mathcal{A}$.
    

**Proposition 2**: If $\lambda \in K $ is an eigenvalue of $\mathcal{A}$, then $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) = 0 $.

- **Proof**: If $\lambda \in K $ is an eigenvalue of $\mathcal{A}$, then there exists $\alpha \in V, \alpha \neq 0 $ such that $\mathcal{A}\alpha = \lambda\alpha $.
    
- Thus, $ 0 = m_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})\alpha = m_{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)\alpha \implies m_{\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) = 0 $.
    

---

### 3. Relationship between Minimal and Characteristic Polynomials

**Summary**: If the characteristic polynomial of $\mathbf{A}$ factors over $ K $

$$
f_{\mathbf{A}}(x) = (x - \lambda_1)^{n_1} \cdots (x - \lambda_s)^{n_s}
$$

then the minimal polynomial $\mathbf{A}$ factors as

$$
m_{\mathbf{A}}(x) = (x - \lambda_1)^{r_1} \cdots (x - \lambda_s)^{r_s}
$$

where $ 1 \le r_i \le n_i $.

---

### 4. Minimal Polynomial Determines Diagonalizability

**Criterion**: 

Let $ V $ be an $ K $-linear space, $\mathcal{A} \in \text{Hom}(V)$. Then $\mathcal{A}$ is diagonalizable if and only if the minimal polynomial of $\mathcal{A}$ can **completely factor and have no repeated roots** over $ K $, i.e.:

$$
m_{\mathcal{A}}(x) = (x - \lambda_1) \cdots (x - \lambda_s)
$$

where $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_s \in K $ are distinct eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$.

Proof:

$\mathcal{A}$ is diagonalizable over the field $ K $, which means:

$$
V = \text{Ker}(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_1 I) \oplus \dots \oplus \text{Ker}(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_s I)
$$

($\lambda_i \in K $ are distinct eigenvalues of $\mathcal{A}$)$\iff V = \text{Ker}[(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_1 I) \dots (\mathcal{A} - \lambda_s I)]$(coprimality)$\iff (\mathcal{A} - \lambda_1 I) \dots (\mathcal{A} - \lambda_s I) = 0 $

$\iff (x - \lambda_1) \dots (x - \lambda_s)$ is $\mathcal{A}$ the polynomial of the lowest degree that annihilates, that is, $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$.

---

### 5. Minimal Polynomial Determines $ K[\mathcal{A}]$ the structure $\mathcal{A}$ Minimal polynomial determines $ K[\mathcal{A}]$ the structure:

- First,$ I, \mathcal{A}, \cdots, \mathcal{A}^{d-1}$ form $ K $a basis for the linear space $ K[\mathcal{A}]$ here $ d = \deg m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$.
    
- Secondly, the map $\sigma : K[x] \to K[\mathcal{A}]$

$$
$ g(x) \mapsto g(\mathcal{A})
$$

is a surjective ring homomorphism,$\text{Ker } \sigma $ is $ m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)$ the set of all multiples.
    
- Thus, the quotient ring $ K[x] / (m_{\mathcal{A}}(x)) \cong K[\mathcal{A}]$.

### 6. The minimal polynomial does not change with the extension of the field

6. Minimal Polynomial Does Not Change with Field Extension

First, of course, it is an annihilating polynomial.

Let $ I, \mathcal{A}, \dots, \mathcal{A}^{d-1}$ form $ K[\mathcal{A}]$ a **basis** for $ d = \deg m_A(x)$.

- This shows that from the $ K $ perspective, this $ d $ The operators are linearly independent.
    
- Linear independence is a "hard" property. If a set of vectors is independent over a small field, they are **still independent** over a large field.
    
- Therefore, in $ F $ the $ d $ operators $\{I, \mathcal{A}, \dots, \mathcal{A}^{d-1}\}$ must also be linearly independent.
    
- Since they are independent, any polynomial of degree less than $ d $ the $ h(A)$(i.e., a linear combination of these operators)cannot equal $ 0 $.

# the primary decomposition theorem

Let $\mathcal{A}$ is $ K $ linear transformation on it, its **minimal polynomial**$ m(x)$ over the field $ K $ decomposes into:

$$
m(x) = (x - \lambda_1)^{r_1} \cdots (x - \lambda_s)^{r_s}
$$

the entire space $ V $ can be uniquely decomposed into $\mathcal{A}$ the **direct sum of root subspaces**:

$$
V = \text{Ker}(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_1 I)^{r_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus \text{Ker}(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_s I)^{r_s}
$$

Once the exponent reaches $ r_1 $, raising it further (for instance to $ r_1+1 $) no longer increases the dimension of $\text{Ker}$. This stable space is the **root subspace** associated with $\lambda_1 $.

This is why we study polynomial rings, **the factors of the polynomials determine the subspaces**.
At the same time, **this is exactly a projection**:

Let $ f_i(x) = (x - \lambda_i)^{r_i}, F_i(x) = \prod_{j \neq i} (x - \lambda_j)^{r_j};$ then $(f_i(x), F_i(x)) = 1,$ there exists $ u_i(x), v_i(x) \in \text{K}[x],$

such that

$$
u_i(x)f_i(x) + v_i(x)F_i(x) = 1.
$$

Let $ h_i(x) = v_i(x)F_i(x) = 1 - u_i(x)f_i(x).$
$ V_i = \text{Ker}(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_i I)^{r_i}$, then $\mathcal{P}_i = h_i(\mathcal{A})$ is a projection onto $ V_i $ the regular projection, keeping $ V_i $ the vectors in the space unchanged, and projecting the rest $ V_j \ (j \neq i)$ all become $ 0.$- **on $ V_i $ :**
    
    any $\alpha \in V_i $ satisfies $(\mathcal{A} - \lambda_i I)^{r_i} \alpha = 0 $that is $ f_i(\mathcal{A})\alpha = 0 $.
    
    going back to the definition:$\mathcal{P}_i = I - u_i(\mathcal{A})f_i(\mathcal{A})$.
    
    when acting on $\alpha $ :$\mathcal{P}_i \alpha = I\alpha - u_i(\mathcal{A})(0) = \alpha $.
    
    This explains why it "preserves" $ V_i $ The vectors remain unchanged at the point.”**.
    
- **In $ V_j (j \neq i)$ :**
    
Any $\beta \in V_j $ satisfying $ f_j(\mathcal{A})\beta = 0 $. Since $ F_i(x)$ contains all factors except $ f_i(x)$, $ f_j(x)$ must be a factor of $ F_i(x)$.
    
Therefore, $ F_i(\mathcal{A})\beta = 0 $.
    
Since $\mathcal{P}_i = v_i(\mathcal{A})F_i(\mathcal{A})$, when acting on $\beta $: $\mathcal{P}_i \beta = v_i(\mathcal{A})(0) = 0 $.
    
This explains why it **“turns all the remaining $ V_j $ into 0”**.

The decomposition given here also holds for invariant subspaces:

**Theorem:** Let $ V = V_1 \oplus \dots \oplus V_s $ be the root subspace decomposition of the linear transformation $\mathcal{A} \in \text{Hom}(V)$.

Then any $\mathcal{A}$-subspace $ W $ has an invariant subspace decomposition

$$
W = W_1 \oplus \dots \oplus W_s, \quad W_i = W \cap V_i
$$

---

Thus, the calculation of the $\mathcal{A}$-subspace $ W $ reduces to the calculation of the $\mathcal{A}$-subspace $ W_i $ within the root subspace $ V_i $.

---

**Proof:** Let $\mathcal{P}_i $ be the projection transformation onto $ V_i $, which turns all the remaining $ V_j \ (j \neq i)$ into $ 0 $. Then, as before, $\mathcal{P}_i $ can be written as a polynomial $ h_i(\mathcal{A})$ of $\mathcal{A}$.

**Thus, $ W $ is an invariant subspace of $\mathcal{P}_i = h_i(\mathcal{A})$, $\mathcal{P}_i W \subseteq W $.**

For any $\alpha \in W $, we have

$$
I\alpha = \mathcal{P}_1 \alpha + \mathcal{P}_2 \alpha + \dots + \mathcal{P}_s \alpha,
$$

where $\mathcal{P}_i \alpha = h_i(\mathcal{A})\alpha \in W \cap V_i $. Thus,

$$
W \subseteq (W \cap V_1) + \dots + (W \cap V_s).
$$

the reverse inclusion relation, and the direct sum relation is obvious.
