### parameter weights from non-standard inner product to image

In the real linear space $ V $, a bilinear function satisfying **positive definiteness** ($\forall \alpha \neq 0, f(\alpha, \alpha) > 0 $) and **symmetry** $ f $ is defined as an inner product. This means that $ f $ is an inner product, which is equivalent to the quadratic form $ f(\alpha, \alpha)$ being positive definite, and also equivalent to the metric matrix of $ f $ being a positive definite matrix.

If we select a set of bases $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ in the space, the coordinates of an arbitrary vector $\alpha $ in this basis are denoted by the column vector $ X $. Using the bilinear property, the calculation of the inner product is entirely determined by the inner products between pairs of basis vectors:

$$
f(\alpha, \alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n x_i x_j f(\alpha_i, \alpha_j) = X^T \begin{bmatrix} f(\alpha_1, \alpha_1) & \cdots & f(\alpha_1, \alpha_n) \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ f(\alpha_n, \alpha_1) & \cdots & f(\alpha_n, \alpha_n) \end{bmatrix} X
$$

This symmetric positive definite matrix in the middle is the **metric matrix** (Metric Matrix) of $ f $ in this basis.

**Motivation: Why do we need non-standard inner products?**

Traditional standard inner products (like the dot product) treat every dimension of the space equally. However, in the real world (such as JPEG image compression algorithms), the visual importance contained in different components of data is different. We need to use non-standard inner products to **adjust the weights of specific parameters**. In an image, the human visual system is more sensitive to low-frequency information (such as large-area color gradients) than to high-frequency information (such as fine texture details). The existence of the metric matrix allows us to mathematically assign different "ruler" measures to low and high frequencies.

In specific image processing, each time we extract a $ 8 \times 8 $ submatrix (such as a local grayscale block in a fighter jet image), this submatrix can be viewed as $ 8 \times 8 $ a vector in the real matrix space $ M_{8,8}(\mathbb{R})$ a vector in $ X $.

Since $ X $ is a vector, we naturally hope to find a set of elegant bases to represent it. If $\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_7 $ is a set of bases for some one-dimensional transformation, then the $ 64 $ matrices $\{\alpha_i \alpha_j^T\} \; (0 \le i, j \le 7)$ constructed by outer product form a set of bases for the two-dimensional matrix space $ M_{8,8}(\mathbb{R})$.

To give the bases good geometric properties (not changing the total energy, and facilitating decoupling), we introduce the **Orthogonal Matrix** $ C $. Here we use the orthogonal matrix from the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT):

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_0 & \alpha_1 & \cdots & \alpha_7 \end{bmatrix} \in M_8(\mathbb{R})
$$

Its column vectors are arranged in the order of **low frequency to high frequency**:

$$
C = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix}

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \cos\frac{\pi}{16} & \cos\frac{2\pi}{16} & \cdots & \cos\frac{7\pi}{16} \\

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \cos\frac{3\pi}{16} & \cos\frac{6\pi}{16} & \cdots & \cos\frac{7 \times 3\pi}{16} \\

\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \cos\frac{15\pi}{16} & \cos\frac{30\pi}{16} & \cdots & \cos\frac{7 \times 15\pi}{16}

\end{bmatrix}
$$

### Coefficient Matrix Full Derivation: From Block Multiplication to Two-Sided Projection

The core problem we face now is: **How to calculate the coefficients $ b_{ij}$ of the image $ X $ in the basis $\{\alpha_i \alpha_j^T\}$?**

We know:

$$
X = \sum_{0 \le i, j \le 7} b_{ij} \alpha_i \alpha_j^T
$$

**Motivation: Differentiating the system of linear equations for 64 basis matrices directly is extremely tedious. We need to use **Block Multiplication** and the overall structure of the matrix to package the summation sign ($\Sigma $).**

**Derivation Process:**

Notice that the coefficient $ b_{ij}$ can be arranged into a matrix $ 8 \times 8 $ $ B = [b_{ij}]$. We rewrite the above equation in the form of matrix multiplication.

Since $\alpha_i $ is the $ i $ column vector of the orthogonal matrix $ C $, if we consider the matrix product $ C B C^T $, using the expansion of block multiplication:

$$
C B C^T = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_0 & \alpha_1 & \cdots & \alpha_7 \end{bmatrix}

\begin{bmatrix}

b_{00} & b_{01} & \cdots & b_{07} \\

b_{10} & b_{11} & \cdots & b_{17} \\

\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\

b_{70} & b_{71} & \cdots & b_{77}

\end{bmatrix}

\begin{bmatrix} \alpha_0^T \\ \alpha_1^T \\ \vdots \\ \alpha_7^T \end{bmatrix}
$$

According to the row-column vector expansion rule of matrix multiplication, the intermediate coefficient matrix $ B $ is actually a linear combination of the basis vectors on both sides, which expands exactly to:

$$
\sum_{i=0}^7 \sum_{j=0}^7 b_{ij} \alpha_i \alpha_j^T
$$

This is exactly our original image $ X $. Therefore, we obtain the extremely elegant equation:

$$
X = C B C^T
$$

To solve for the coefficient matrix $ B $, since $ C $ is an orthogonal matrix, it satisfies $ C^T C = C C^T = I $ (i.e., $ C^{-1} = C^T $). We left-multiply and right-multiply both sides of the equation by $ C^T $ and $ C $, respectively:

$$
C^T X C = C^T (C B C^T) C = (C^T C) B (C^T C) = I B I = B
$$

Thus, the **coefficient matrix is successfully expressed**:

$$
\mathbf{B = [b_{ij}] = C^T X C}
$$

And during decompression (image reconstruction), the image can be recovered simply by using the inverse formula:

$$
\mathbf{X = C B C^T}
$$

### Quantization: Practical Implementation of Non-Standard Inner Product Idea

After obtaining the coefficient matrix $ B $, the most critical step in image compression is **Quantization**. This step is to discard high-frequency information that is not easily perceptible to the human eye.

Specifically, multiply the represented coefficient $ b_{ij}$ by the corresponding weight $ 1/q_{ij}$ (i.e., divide by $ q_{ij}$) and take the integer part, arranging it into the quantized matrix $ D $:

$$
D = [d_{ij}]_{0 \le i, j \le 7}, \quad \mathbf{d_{ij} = \text{round}(b_{ij} / q_{ij})}
$$

Here, $ Q_{50} = [q_{ij}]$ is a quantization matrix uniformly specified by experts (e.g., standard JPEG quantization table):

$$
Q_{50} = \begin{bmatrix}

16 & 11 & 10 & \cdots & 61 \\

12 & 12 & 14 & \cdots & 55 \\

14 & 13 & 16 & \cdots & 56 \\

\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\

72 & 92 & 95 & \cdots & 99

\end{bmatrix}
$$

**Motivation and Mechanism Evaluation:**

- **Advantage (Low-frequency region in the upper-left corner)**: $ q_{ij}$ is small. This means the divisor is small, and the retained step size is fine, **retaining a lot of information**. This is because the upper-left corner corresponds to the DC component and the large contours of the image, which are most decisive for visual effect.
    
- **What we lose in the high-frequency region (lower right).** When $ q_{ij}$ is large, division and rounding turn many small high-frequency coefficients $ b_{ij}$ into **$ 0 $**. Precision drops and we lose some edge detail, but the eye is less sensitive to high frequencies. The slight blurring saves a great deal of space.
    

This asymmetric quantization matrix $ Q $ is essentially the **projection mechanism of a non-standard inner product in a discrete space** mentioned earlier—achieving precise adjustment of specific parameter weights by defining the importance of each dimension in the space (the metric matrix).

# Euclidean Space
### Euclidean Space: Endowing a Linear Space with a "Metric"

In a purely linear space, we can only talk about the "addition" and "scalar multiplication" of vectors; there is no length, no distance, and no angle. To give the space geometric intuition, we must introduce the **inner product**.

A (finite-dimensional) real linear space equipped with an inner product $(\alpha, \beta)$ is called a **Euclidean space**. With the inner product, concepts of vector length (norm), angle, distance, and orthogonality naturally emerge on the Euclidean space (Metric).

#### 1. The Length of a Vector (Norm)

If $\alpha = [\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n] X \in V $, meaning $ X $ are the coordinates of $\alpha $ in some basis. We define the length (or Euclidean norm) of $\alpha $ as:

$$
\|\alpha\| := \sqrt{(\alpha, \alpha)} = \sqrt{X^T A X}
$$

Where $ A $ is the metric matrix in this basis. With the length, the **Euclidean distance** between any two points $\alpha, \beta $ can naturally be defined as the length of the difference vector between the two points:

$$
\text{Distance}(\alpha, \beta) := \|\alpha - \beta\|
$$

### Construction and Complete Derivation of the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

In Euclidean space, the mathematical foundation for all geometric intuition (such as "the shortest distance between two points is a straight line," or "the shadow length is less than the original length") stems from two crucial major theorems: the **Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality** and the **Triangle Inequality**.

#### 1. Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

**Theorem Statement**: $\|\alpha\| \|\beta\| \ge |(\alpha, \beta)|$

**Motivation**: Intuitively, we know that the inner product of two vectors equals the product of their lengths multiplied by the cosine of the angle between them ($\cos\theta $). Because $|\cos\theta| \le 1 $, the absolute value of the inner product must be less than or equal to the product of the lengths. However, this is a conclusion for three-dimensional space; in abstract Euclidean space, we haven't even defined an "angle," so we must derive it rigorously purely from the **positive definiteness of the inner product**, completely detached from geometric intuition.

**Complete Derivation Process**: 

Consider introducing an arbitrary real number $ t $ and constructing a constructive vector $\alpha - t\beta $. By the positive definiteness of the inner product, the inner product of any vector with itself must be greater than or equal to 0:

$$
(\alpha - t\beta, \alpha - t\beta) \ge 0
$$

Using the bilinearity and symmetry of the inner product, expand the above equation:

$$
(\alpha, \alpha) - 2t(\alpha, \beta) + t^2(\beta, \beta) \ge 0
$$

This is a quadratic inequality in $ t $: $ A t^2 + B t + C \ge 0 $, which holds for any real number $ t $.

To make the inequality reveal the structure we want, we can take the position of the axis of symmetry that minimizes this quadratic function, i.e., set $ t = \frac{(\alpha, \beta)}{(\beta, \beta)}$ (assuming $\beta \neq 0 $).

Substitute $ t $ into the expanded form:

$$
(\alpha, \alpha) - 2\frac{(\alpha, \beta)^2}{(\beta, \beta)} + \frac{(\alpha, \beta)^2}{(\beta, \beta)^2}(\beta, \beta) \ge 0
$$

$$
(\alpha, \alpha) - \frac{(\alpha, \beta)^2}{(\beta, \beta)} \ge 0
$$

Multiply both sides by $(\beta, \beta)$ and move terms, immediately obtaining:

$$
(\alpha, \alpha)(\beta, \beta) \ge (\alpha, \beta)^2
$$

Take the square root of both sides. Since $\|\alpha\| = \sqrt{(\alpha, \alpha)}$, we finally derive:

$$
\|\alpha\| \|\beta\| \ge |(\alpha, \beta)|
$$

**Condition for Equality**: If and only if the discriminant is 0, meaning there exists some $ t $ such that $\alpha - t\beta = 0 $, which means $\alpha $ and $\beta $ are **collinear (linearly dependent)**.

#### 2. The Euclidean Distance Satisfies the Triangle Inequality

**Theorem Statement**: 

$$
\|\alpha\| + \|\beta\| \ge \|\alpha + \beta\|
$$

**Motivation**: Geometrically, this corresponds to "the sum of the lengths of two sides of a triangle is greater than the third side." Mathematically, it is the core condition for verifying whether a metric can be called a "distance" or a "norm." We need to use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality proven just before to complete this derivation.

**Complete Derivation Process**: 

We start from the square of the norm of the sum of vectors:

$$
\|\alpha + \beta\|^2 = (\alpha + \beta, \alpha + \beta)
$$

Expand using the distributive property of the inner product:

$$
(\alpha + \beta, \alpha + \beta) = (\alpha, \alpha) + 2(\alpha, \beta) + (\beta, \beta) = \|\alpha\|^2 + 2(\alpha, \beta) + \|\beta\|^2
$$

At this point, to amplify this expression, we replace the inner product $(\alpha, \beta)$, which might be negative, with an upper bound scaled by the cosine. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have $(\alpha, \beta) \le |(\alpha, \beta)| \le \|\alpha\| \|\beta\|$.

Substitute this into the above inequality for amplification:

$$
\|\alpha\|^2 + 2(\alpha, \beta) + \|\beta\|^2 \le \|\alpha\|^2 + 2\|\alpha\| \|\beta\| + \|\beta\|^2
$$

Notice that the right side is exactly a perfect square:

$$
\|\alpha\|^2 + 2\|\alpha\| \|\beta\| + \|\beta\|^2 = (\|\alpha\| + \|\beta\|)^2
$$

In summary, we obtained:

$$
\|\alpha + \beta\|^2 \le (\|\alpha\| + \|\beta\|)^2
$$

Take the square root of both sides. Since the norms are non-negative, the inequality direction remains unchanged:

$$
\|\alpha\| + \|\beta\| \ge \|\alpha + \beta\|
$$

The cornerstone of the geometric edifice thus converges algebraically.

### Generalization of Space: Manifestation in Function Spaces and Matrix Spaces

The greatness of Euclidean space lies in the fact that as long as a suitable inner product is defined, **functions** and **matrices** can also possess "length," "distance," and the "Triangle Inequality" just like regular vectors.

#### Example 1: Continuous Function Space $ C[0,1]$ In the space of continuous functions on the interval $[0,1]$, if we define the inner product as an integral:

$$
(h, g) = \int_{0}^{1} h(x)g(x)dx
$$

Then, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality in this space manifests as the famous **integral form of the Cauchy inequality**:

$$
\int_{0}^{1} h(x)^2dx \int_{0}^{1} g(x)^2dx \ge \left( \int_{0}^{1} h(x)g(x)dx \right)^2
$$

And its corresponding triangle inequality characterizes the energy boundary after function superposition:

$$
\sqrt{\int_{0}^{1} h(x)^2dx} + \sqrt{\int_{0}^{1} g(x)^2dx} \ge \sqrt{\int_{0}^{1} (h(x)+g(x))^2dx}
$$

#### Example 2: Real Matrix Space $ M_n(\mathbb{R})$ For a real matrix of order $ n $, if we define the inner product as the product of the traces:

$$
(A, B) = \text{tr}(A^T B)
$$

This is called the Frobenius inner product. According to the unified Euclidean space theorem, two matrices must satisfy:

$$
\text{tr}(A^T A) \text{tr}(B^T B) \ge \text{tr}(A^T B)^2
$$

And their matrix norm (similar to the length of a matrix) also perfectly satisfies the triangle inequality:

$$
\sqrt{\text{tr}(A^T A)} + \sqrt{\text{tr}(B^T B)} \ge \sqrt{\text{tr}((A+B)^T (A+B))}
$$

**Condition for equality**: If and only if the matrices $ A $ and $ B $ are linearly dependent (i.e., there exists a constant $ k $ such that $ A = kB $).

From the initial Z-shaped packing of image pixel blocks to the trace of an abstract matrix, linear algebra elegantly constrains all 'lengths' and 'projections' in the multi-dimensional world using a unified inequality structure.

We have already been able to measure the 'length' and 'distance' of vectors. Now, we will further introduce the **angle between vectors** and **orthogonality**.

With these tools, we will replicate the high school theorem of 'shortest perpendicular distance' in linear space, and establish an extremely beautiful geometric structure in high-dimensional space—the **Orthogonal Complement** and the **Orthogonal Projection**.

### Angle and Standard Orthonormal Basis: Algebraizing Geometric Intuition

After proving the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality $\|\alpha\|\|\beta\| \ge |(\alpha, \beta)|$, for any two non-zero vectors $\alpha, \beta $, their quotient must satisfy:

$$
-1 \le \frac{(\alpha, \beta)}{\|\alpha\|\|\beta\|} \le 1
$$

This grants us the mathematical legitimacy to define the **angle between vectors $\theta $**. We define:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{(\alpha, \beta)}{\|\alpha\|\|\beta\|} \quad (\alpha \neq 0, \beta \neq 0)
$$

By algebraic manipulation, we can write very beautifully:

$$
2\cos\theta \|\alpha\|\|\beta\| = \|\alpha\|^2 + \|\beta\|^2 - \|\alpha - \beta\|^2
$$

Specifically, when $\theta = 90^\circ $, then $\cos\theta = 0 $. At this point, we call two vectors **orthogonal** (Orthogonal), denoted as $\alpha \perp \beta $, whose algebraic necessary and sufficient condition is that their inner product is zero:

$$
\alpha \perp \beta \iff (\alpha, \beta) = 0
$$

#### Standard Orthonormal Basis

A basis formed by a set of basis vectors that are mutually orthogonal and all have a length of $ 1 $ (unit vectors) is called the **Standard Orthonormal Basis** of an Euclidean space. A set of basis vectors $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ is a standard orthonormal basis if and only if the metric matrix formed by the inner products between any pair of these vectors is exactly the identity matrix $ I $:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} (\alpha_1, \alpha_1) & (\alpha_1, \alpha_2) & \cdots & (\alpha_1, \alpha_n) \\ (\alpha_2, \alpha_1) & (\alpha_2, \alpha_2) & \cdots & (\alpha_2, \alpha_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ (\alpha_n, \alpha_1) & \alpha_n, \alpha_2) & \cdots & (\alpha_n, \alpha_n) \end{bmatrix} = I
$$

**Proposition Transformation and Basis Change**:

Let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ be a standard orthonormal basis of an Euclidean space, and $ P $ be an invertible real matrix of order $ n $. Let the new basis satisfy the transition relationship $(\beta_1 \cdots \beta_n) = (\alpha_1 \cdots \alpha_n)P $ with the old basis.

Then, **$\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ is a necessary and sufficient condition for $ P $ to be an orthogonal matrix** (i.e., $ P^T P = I $).

- **Proof Motivation**: The inner product metric matrix in the new basis can be obtained through a congruence transformation using the transition matrix. Since the old basis is a standard orthonormal basis (metric matrix is $ I $), the metric matrix in the new basis is directly represented as $ P^T I P = P^T P $. For the new basis to also be a standard orthonormal basis, its metric matrix must also be $ I $, hence $ P^T P = I $.
    
# Orthogonal Transformation
### Orthogonal Complement: The Perfect Symmetry of Subspaces

When we extend from the orthogonal complement of a single vector to an entire subspace, the concept of the **Orthogonal Complement** is born.

Let $ W $ be a subspace of the Euclidean space $ V $. The set of all vectors orthogonal to any vector in $ W $ is called the **Orthogonal Complement** of $ W $, denoted as $ W^\perp $:

$$
W^\perp := \{ \alpha \in V \mid (\alpha, \beta) = 0, \; \forall \beta \in W \}
$$

$ W^\perp $ is also a subspace of $ V $. By extending the standard orthonormal basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r $ of $ W $ to the standard orthonormal basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r, \alpha_{r+1}, \dots, \alpha_n $ of the entire $ V $ space, the latter part $\alpha_{r+1}, \dots, \alpha_n $ naturally becomes a set of standard orthonormal basis for $ W^\perp $.

#### Geometric Properties of the Orthogonal Complement—Reversed Inclusion

The orthogonal complement operator possesses very beautiful algebraic symmetry, similar to the complement set in set theory, but it preserves the integrity of the linear structure:

- **Direct Sum Decomposition**: $ W \oplus W^\perp = V $ (the entire space can be uniquely decomposed into the $ W $ part and the part perpendicular to $ W $).
    
- **Double Orthogonal Complement Reduction**: $(W^\perp)^\perp = W $.
    
- **Reversed Inclusion**: $ W \subseteq U \iff W^\perp \supseteq U^\perp $ (the larger the space, the fewer vectors that can be kept perpendicular to it).
    
- **Dual Operator Rule**: $(U + W)^\perp = U^\perp \cap W^\perp $; $(U \cap W)^\perp = U^\perp + W^\perp $.
    

### Orthogonal Projection: From 'Shortest Perpendicular Distance' to Bilateral Projection Operator

Now we introduce the most core geometric operation of the entire article—the **Orthogonal Projection**.


> Figure to be added.


As shown in the figure, let $\alpha $ be a vector in the entire space $ V $, and we want to find the **best approximation vector** $\beta $ in the subspace $ W $.

According to the direct sum decomposition $ V = W \oplus W^\perp $, any vector $\alpha $ can be uniquely decomposed as:

$$
\alpha = \beta + (\alpha - \beta), \quad \text{where } \beta \in W, \; (\alpha - \beta) \in W^\perp
$$

At this point, $\beta $ is called the **orthogonal projection** of $\alpha $ onto the subspace $ W $, and the difference vector $\alpha - \beta $ is perpendicular to the entire subspace $ W $ (i.e., $\alpha - \beta \perp W $).

**Motivation: Why go through the trouble of proving the difference vector is perpendicular?**

Because geometrically, the **perpendicular distance is the shortest**. For any other vector $\gamma \in W $ inside $ W $, due to $\beta - \gamma \in W $, and $\alpha - \beta \perp W $, therefore $\alpha - \beta \perp \beta - \gamma $. According to the **Pythagorean Theorem**, the square of the hypotenuse of a right triangle equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides:

$$
\|\alpha - \gamma\|^2 = \|\alpha - \beta\|^2 + \|\beta - \gamma\|^2 \ge \|\alpha - \beta\|^2
$$

Thus perfectly deriving:

$$
\|\alpha - \beta\| \le \|\alpha - \gamma\|
$$

This shows that the orthogonal projection $\beta $ is the unique point in the subspace $ W $ closest to $\alpha $. In data science, this is the geometric essence of the 'least squares method' and 'optimal approximation'.

### Complete Derivation of Projection Vector and Projection Matrix

How do we specifically calculate this projection vector $\beta $? This depends on what kind of basis we have.

#### 1. Given an Orthogonal Basis for the Subspace

If $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_r $ is an **orthogonal basis** for the subspace $ W $ (whose lengths are not necessarily 1). Due to $\beta \in W $, we can express $\beta $ as:

$$
\beta = k_1 \beta_1 + k_2 \beta_2 + \cdots + k_r \beta_r
$$

Due to $\alpha - \beta \perp W $, it must be orthogonal to every basis vector $\beta_i $. This establishes the inner product equation:

$$
(\alpha - \beta, \beta_i) = 0 \implies (\alpha, \beta_i) = (\beta, \beta_i)
$$

Substituting the expansion of $\beta $ into the right side, and using the fact that the basis vectors are mutually orthogonal (when $ j \neq i $, $(\beta_j, \beta_i) = 0 $), the summation term on the right side instantly collapses, leaving only one term:

$$
(\alpha, \beta_i) = k_i (\beta_i, \beta_i) \implies k_i = \frac{(\alpha, \beta_i)}{(\beta_i, \beta_i)}
$$

**Successfully derived the projection vector formula**: 

$$
\beta = \frac{(\alpha, \beta_1)}{(\beta_1, \beta_1)}\beta_1 + \frac{(\alpha, \beta_2)}{(\beta_2, \beta_2)}\beta_2 + \cdots + \frac{(\alpha, \beta_r)}{(\beta_r, \beta_r)}\beta_r
$$

#### 2. Orthogonal Projection Matrix under the Standard Inner Product $ B B^T $ If $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_r $ is a set of **orthonormal bases** for the subspace $ W $ (in which case the denominator is $(\beta_i, \beta_i) = 1 $), and under the **standard inner product**, the inner product can be written as a matrix multiplication $(\alpha, \beta_i) = \beta_i^T \alpha $.

**Deriving the Operator Form**:

The formula above simplifies to:

$$
\beta = (\beta_1^T \alpha)\beta_1 + (\beta_2^T \alpha)\beta_2 + \cdots + (\beta_r^T \alpha)\beta_r
$$

Since the inner product $\beta_i^T \alpha $ is a scalar (a number), we can write it on the right side of the vector $\beta_i $ (i.e., $\beta_i (\beta_i^T \alpha)$), and use the associativity of matrix multiplication to pull it out:

$$
\beta = \sum_{i=1}^r \beta_i \beta_i^T \alpha = \left( \sum_{i=1}^r \beta_i \beta_i^T \right) \alpha
$$

Now, we arrange the standard orthonormal bases as columns into a matrix $ B = [\beta_1 \cdots \beta_r]$. According to the block matrix multiplication expansion rule, the sum of outer products $\sum_{i=1}^r \beta_i \beta_i^T $ can exactly be packaged into the product of the matrix and its transpose $ B B^T $.

At the same time, we can also examine this process from the perspective of coordinate projection. Writing the formula in block matrix form:

$$
\beta = [\beta_1 \cdots \beta_r] \begin{bmatrix} \beta_1^T \alpha \\ \vdots \\ \beta_r^T \alpha \end{bmatrix} = B (B^T \alpha) = \mathbf{B B^T \alpha}
$$

Here, the column vector sandwiched in the product $ B^T \alpha $ is exactly the **projection coordinate** of the projection vector onto the subspace spanned by this set of standard orthonormal bases.

Since $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_r $ is a standard orthonormal basis, it obviously satisfies $ B^T B = I_r $. We examine this newly formed matrix operator $ P_W = B B^T $:

- **Idempotence (Re-projection is unchanged)**: $ P_W^2 = (B B^T)(B B^T) = B (B^T B) B^T = B I_r B^T = B B^T = P_W $. This is extremely intuitive geometrically—if an image is projected onto a plane once, and then projected again, its position will not change at all.
    
- **Symmetry**: $ P_W^T = (B B^T)^T = B B^T = P_W $.
    

This extremely concise matrix $\mathbf{B B^T}$ is the **orthogonal projection matrix** under the standard inner product. It acts like a scalpel, capable of cleanly stripping out the visual or signal component belonging to the subspace $ W $ from an arbitrary high-dimensional vector.

By using a classic comprehensive problem as the core, we integrate the **inner product test**, **Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization**, **QR decomposition of matrices**, and **congruence transformation method** learned previously. Through deconstruction from two perspectives (Method 1: Geometric Projection Flow; Method 2: Algebraic Matrix Flow), we deeply reveal the underlying geometric motivation.

### Example

> **Problem**: Given the bilinear function $ f(\alpha, \beta)$ on $\mathbb{R}^3 $, whose metric matrix in the basis $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3 $ is:
> 
>

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

> 
> 1. Is $ f(\alpha, \beta)$ an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^3 $? If so, find a set of standard orthonormal bases $\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3 $ for $ f $.
>     
> 2. Find the distance from $\alpha_3 $ to the subspace $ W = \langle\alpha_1, \alpha_2\rangle $.
>     

#### Core Pre-test: Is it valid?

Since $ A^T = A $, $ A $ is clearly a real symmetric matrix, therefore $ f $ satisfies symmetry. To determine if $ f $ is an inner product, we only need to check the **positive definiteness** of $ A $.

Observe the leading principal minors of $ A $:

- 1st order leading principal minor: $\Delta_1 = 1 > 0 $
    
- 2nd order leading principal minor: $\Delta_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2 - 1 = 1 > 0 $
    
- 3rd order leading principal minor: $\Delta_3 = \det(A) = 1 \cdot (6 - 1) - 1 \cdot (3 - 0) = 5 - 3 = 2 > 0 $ According to **Sylvester's Criterion**, since all leading principal minors are greater than 0, the metric matrix $ A $ is positive definite, and $ f $ is indeed a **non-standard inner product** on $\mathbb{R}^3 $.

#### Method 1: Geometric Projection Flow (Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization and QR Decomposition)

**Motivation**: Utilizing the idea of layer-by-layer orthogonal projection, we refine a set of ordinary bases into a set of standard orthonormal bases. In this process, the natural byproduct is the subspace distance and the QR decomposition of the matrix.

##### 1. Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Derivation

We want to find a set of mutually orthogonal vectors $\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3 $.

- **Step 1: Establish the Basis**
    
    Directly set the first basis vector as the anchor:

$$
\beta_1 = \alpha_1
$$

- **Step 2: Strip the projection of $\alpha_2 $ onto $\beta_1 $**

$$
\beta_2 = \alpha_2 - \text{Proj}_{\beta_1}(\alpha_2) = \alpha_2 - \frac{f(\alpha_2, \beta_1)}{f(\beta_1, \beta_1)}\beta_1
$$

- **Step 3: Strip the projection of $\alpha_3 $ onto the plane spanned by $\beta_1, \beta_2 $**

$$
\beta_3 = \alpha_3 - \frac{f(\alpha_3, \beta_1)}{f(\beta_1, \beta_1)}\beta_1 - \frac{f(\alpha_3, \beta_2)}{f(\beta_2, \beta_2)}\beta_2
$$

By moving the terms in the above formula, we obtain a beautiful **orthogonal decomposition structure**:

$$
\alpha_1 = \beta_1
$$

$$
\alpha_2 = \frac{f(\alpha_2, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|^2}\beta_1 + \beta_2
$$

$$
\alpha_3 = \frac{f(\alpha_3, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|^2}\beta_1 + \frac{f(\alpha_3, \beta_2)}{\|\beta_2\|^2}\beta_2 + \beta_3
$$

##### 2. Elevation: The Essence of Matrix QR Decomposition and Distance

If we further **normalize** $\beta_i $, setting $\hat{\beta}_i = \frac{\beta_i}{\|\beta_i\|}$, then the above equation can be rewritten as:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 & \alpha_2 & \alpha_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\beta}_1 & \hat{\beta}_2 & \hat{\beta}_3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \|\beta_1\| & \frac{f(\alpha_2, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|} & \frac{f(\alpha_3, \beta_1)}{\|\beta_1\|} \\ 0 & \|\beta_2\| & \frac{f(\alpha_3, \beta_2)}{\|\beta_2\|} \\ 0 & 0 & \|\beta_3\| \end{bmatrix}
$$

This is the famous **QR decomposition**: $ A_{basis} = Q R $, where $ Q $ is an orthogonal matrix, and $ R $ is an upper triangular matrix.

**Motivation for the Second Question**: 

Requirements $\alpha_3 $ to a subspace $\langle\alpha_1, \alpha_2\rangle $ the distance, based on the principle that the "perpendicular segment is the shortest," this distance is $\alpha_3 $ the length of the **residual vector** after subtracting its orthogonal projection onto the plane. And from the Gram-Schmidt construction, we know that this residual vector is exactly $\beta_3 $!

Therefore:

$$
\text{Distance}(\alpha_3, \langle\alpha_1, \alpha_2\rangle) = \|\beta_3\| = \sqrt{f(\beta_3, \beta_3)}
$$

In the matrix $ R $ from the QR decomposition, the third element on the diagonal, **$\|\beta_3\|$**, precisely represents the geometric distance from $\alpha_i $ to the subspace spanned by all preceding vectors.

#### Method 2: Algebraic Matrix Flow (Congruence Transformation and Elimination Method)

**Motivation**: Although the geometric intuition is elegant, the computational cost of calculating inner products and term-by-term projections is quite large. Algebraically, we know that the transformation of the metric matrix satisfies the congruence relation $ P^T A P = I $. If we can transform $ A $ into the identity matrix $ I $ by **simultaneously applying symmetric elementary row and column transformations**, then the transition matrix $ P $ can help us find the standard orthonormal basis in one step.

##### 1. Symmetric Row and Column Transformations (Congruence Diagonalization)

To record the column transformations applied to $ A $ (which will form the transition matrix $ P $), we append an identity matrix $ I $ below $ A $, forming a lower block linkage matrix. When we perform row transformations on $ A $, we **must simultaneously apply the same column transformation** to $ A $, and the identity matrix below **only moves with the column transformation**.

The goal is to transform $ A $ into the identity matrix $ I $. Initial state of the input matrix:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} A \\ \hline I \end{bmatrix} = \left[ \begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ \hline 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]
$$

- **Round 1 Elimination: Eliminating non-diagonal elements in the first row/column**
    
    - Add $-1 $ times the first row to the second row, and **simultaneously** add $-1 $ times the first column to the second column:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ \hline 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]
$$

- **Round 2 Elimination: Eliminating non-diagonal elements in the second row/column**
    
    - Add $-1 $ times the second row to the third row, and **simultaneously** add $-1 $ times the second column to the third column:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ \hline 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right]
$$

- At this point, the upper half has been transformed into a diagonal matrix $\text{diag}(1, 1, 2)$.
        
- **Round 3: Unitizing the diagonal elements**
    
    - To change the $ 2 $ in the third row and third column to $ 1 $, we need to multiply the third row by $ 1/\sqrt{2}$, and **simultaneously** multiply the third column by $ 1/\sqrt{2}$:

$$
\left[ \begin{array}{ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ \hline 1 & -1 & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & 1 & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & 0 & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{array} \right]
$$

##### 2. The Perfect Algebraic Conclusion

When the joint transformation of the lower half is complete, we directly write down the transition matrix $ P $:

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & 1 & -1/\sqrt{2} \\ 0 & 0 & 1/\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}
$$

By construction, it perfectly satisfies:

$$
P^T A P = I
$$

At this point, we directly read the column vector $ P $, expressing the new basis as:

$$
(\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3) = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3) P
$$

That is:

$$
\beta_1 = \alpha_1
$$

$$
\beta_2 = -\alpha_1 + \alpha_2
$$

$$
\beta_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\alpha_1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\alpha_2 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\alpha_3
$$

The inner product of any two vectors in this set is $ f(\beta_i, \beta_j) = \delta_{ij}$, **which is exactly the standard orthonormal basis we have been dreaming of**.

### Least Squares Solution

In engineering applications, we often need to solve systems of linear equations $ AX = \beta $. When the number of observed data points is much larger than the number of unknowns, the matrix $ A $ is often "skinny and long," in which case the system of equations is usually **inconsistent** (i.e., $\beta $ is not in $ A $ the column space $ C(A)$ ). In mathematics, we call this a contradictory system of equations.

**Motivation.** If a perfect fit is out of reach, how do we find the best approximation?

According to the projection geometry in Euclidean space, the point in the subspace closest to the distance $\beta $ is only $\beta $, which is the **orthogonal projection $\gamma $** onto that subspace. Therefore, we cannot make $ AX = \beta $ hold, but we can settle for a $ X $, such that $ AX $ is exactly equal to the projection $\gamma $.

**Theorem Derivation:**

Let $\gamma $ be the orthogonal projection of the column vector $\beta $ onto the column space of $ A $, then the equation system $ AX = \gamma $ must have a solution. By the definition of orthogonal projection, the residual vector $\beta - \gamma $ must be perpendicular to the entire column space $ C(A)$:

$$
\beta - AX \perp C(A)
$$

This means that the inner product of $\beta - AX $ with every column vector of $ A $ is 0, which can be written in matrix form as:

$$
A^T (\beta - AX) = 0
$$

Expanding gives:

$$
A^T \beta - A^T AX = 0 \iff \mathbf{A^T AX = A^T \beta}
$$

This new equation system is called the **Normal Equations**.

- **Conclusion**: The equation system $ AX = \gamma $ has the same solutions as $ A^T AX = A^T \beta $.
    
- **Definition**: The solution $\eta $ to the normal equations (which always exists, but may not be unique) is called the **least squares solution** to the original inconsistent equation system $ AX = \beta $.
    

When the $ A = [\alpha_1 \cdots \alpha_n]$ is full rank (i.e., its column vectors are linearly independent), the product matrix $ A^T A $ is full rank and invertible. At this point, the least squares equation has a unique and elegant analytical solution:

$$
X = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \beta
$$

Here, $\mathbf{A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T}$ is called the **generalized inverse (pseudo-inverse)** of $ A $, which is the **best left inverse** for **$ A $**. At this point, the orthogonal projection vector can be expressed as $\gamma = AX = A A^+ \beta $, and $ X = A^+ \beta $ is the **coordinate** of the projection vector in the basis of the column space.

### Least Squares Method and Regression Line

Projecting the high-dimensional abstract geometry above onto a two-dimensional plane perfectly explains **linear regression** in statistics.

#### 1. Problem Formulation and Error Modeling

Given $ n $ experimental observation points $ P_i = (a_i, b_i) \; (i=1, 2, \dots, n)$ on a plane, we want to find a line $ y = kx + l $ such that these points are closest to this line overall.

**Motivation.** How can we measure closeness across all the data points?

Directly measuring the geometric distance from a point to a line (the perpendicular distance) introduces complex radicals, which are unfavorable for differentiation and optimization. Therefore, we choose to examine the **sum of the squares of the vertical distance ($ y $ axis distance) from a point to a line**.

For an arbitrary observation point $ a_i $, its model prediction value is $ k a_i + l $, and its actual observed value is $ b_i $. The vertical error between the two is $|b_i - ka_i - l|$.

Our goal is: to find the optimal parameters $ k $ and $ l $ that minimize the sum of squared residuals $ S $:

$$
\min_{k, l} \sum_{i=1}^n (b_i - ka_i - l)^2
$$

#### 2. Vectorization and High-Dimensional Geometric Transformation

To use linear algebra tools, we package these $ n $ algebraic equations:

Let the design matrix $ A $ and the observation vector $\beta $ be:

$$
A = [\alpha_1 \; \alpha_2] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & a_1 \\ 1 & a_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & a_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad \beta = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad X = \begin{bmatrix} l \\ k \end{bmatrix}
$$

Then, the sum of squared residuals can be equivalently written in the $ n $ dimensional Euclidean space as the **square of the Euclidean norm (length) of the residual vector**:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^n (b_i - ka_i - l)^2 = \|\beta - k\alpha_2 - l\alpha_1\|^2 = \|\beta - AX\|^2
$$

Thus, a grand high-dimensional geometric picture slowly unfolds in the $ n $ dimensional space:

- The vector $\alpha_1 $ (all 1s vector) and $\alpha_2 $ (variable vector) span a two-dimensional subspace (plane) $ V = \langle\alpha_1, \alpha_2\rangle $ in the $ n $ dimensional space.
    
- The observed data $\beta $ is a free vector in the $ n $ dimensional space.
    
- Finding the optimal $ k, l $ that minimizes $\|\beta - AX\|$ is essentially **finding a linear combination $ k\alpha_2 + l\alpha_1 $ on the plane $ V $ whose Euclidean distance to $\beta $ is shortest**.
    

According to the shortest distance principle, when $ k\alpha_2 + l\alpha_1 $ is exactly the **orthogonal projection** of $\beta $ onto the subspace $ V $, the distance reaches its minimum. Since the matrix $ A $ is clearly full rank, we can directly substitute it into the previous pseudo-inverse formula to find the intercept and slope of the regression line in one step:

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix} l \\ k \end{bmatrix} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \beta
$$

### Generalization of Norm: Non-linear Polynomial Curve Fitting

Least-squares projection is useful for much more than fitting a straight line. Whenever the unknown parameters enter linearly, the same mathematical structure lets us fit curves of any degree.

**Motivation: What if the data points show a clear curved trend (like a parabola), and the linear model fails?**

We can upgrade the fitting model to a quadratic polynomial curve:

$$
y = ux^2 + vx + w
$$

At this point, for the same observation points $ P_i = (a_i, b_i)$, the absolute value of the vertical residual for a single point is $|b_i - u a_i^2 - v a_i - w|$.

To minimize the sum of squared residuals $\sum_{i=1}^n (b_i - u a_i^2 - v a_i - w)^2 $, we only need to redefine the basis of the design matrix $ A $. At this point, we construct three basis vectors in the $ n $ dimensional space: the constant term vector $\alpha_1 $, the linear term vector $\alpha_2 $, and the quadratic term vector $\alpha_3 $:

$$
A = [\alpha_1 \; \alpha_2 \; \alpha_3] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & a_1 & a_1^2 \\ 1 & a_2 & a_2^2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & a_n & a_n^2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \beta = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ \vdots \\ b_n \end{bmatrix}, \quad X = \begin{bmatrix} w \\ v \\ u \end{bmatrix}
$$

The problem is thus again transformed into: finding the orthogonal projection of $\beta $ onto the three-dimensional subspace $ V = \langle\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\rangle $ spanned by three vectors in the $ n $ dimensional space.

The final algebraic solution formula still maintains its perfect eternal form:

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix} w \\ v \\ u \end{bmatrix} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \beta
$$

From the weighted measure of a one-dimensional signal, to the undeniable perpendicular projection in a high-dimensional space, the least squares method builds an exquisite bridge between linear and non-linear, algebra and geometry, theory and reality, using the most concise normal equation set $ A^T AX = A^T \beta $.

Since an orthogonal matrix represents a rotation or reflection in high dimensions, its eigenvalues (spectrum) must be subject to strong geometric constraints. Over the real numbers, a rotation might not have real eigenvalues (for example, a rotation in the two-dimensional plane $\theta $ angle), but if we broaden our view to the **complex domain**, the spectral structure of an orthogonal matrix exhibits extreme symmetry.

**Deduction**: If $ A $ is the matrix corresponding to an orthogonal transformation $ V $ in Euclidean space $ A $ on $\lambda $ then any complex eigenvalue

$$

satisfies: That is, **all complex eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix lie on the unit circle in the complex plane**.

A\alpha = \lambda \alpha

\overline{\alpha}^T A^T = \overline{\lambda} \overline{\alpha}^T

$$
\left( \overline{\alpha}^T A^T \right) (A\alpha) = \left( \overline{\lambda} \overline{\alpha}^T \right) (\lambda \alpha)
$$

In other words, **all complex eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix lie on the unit circle in the complex plane**.

$$
\overline{\alpha}^T (A^T A) \alpha = \overline{\lambda} \lambda (\overline{\alpha}^T \alpha)

Due to $ A $ is an orthogonal matrix, satisfying $ A^T A = I $;and in the complex domain,$\overline{\lambda}\lambda = |\lambda|^2 $.

This elegant algebraic derivation is extremely intuitive geometrically: because orthogonal transformations have the property of preserving the norm ($\|A\alpha\| = \|\alpha\|$)), and if the eigenvalues scale the vector ($\|A\alpha\| = |\lambda|\|\alpha\|$)), comparing the two, the scaling factor $|\lambda|$ can only have an absolute value equal to $ 1 $.

### III. Group Theory Perspective: Orthogonal Group $ O(n)$ and Special Orthogonal Group $ SO(n)$ When we gather all $ n $ orthogonal matrices of degree, they exhibit perfect closure under matrix multiplication, thus forming a classic Lie Group of extreme importance in modern algebra.

#### 1. Orthogonal Group— $ O(n)$ all $ n $ orthogonal matrices of degree form a group under matrix multiplication, called $ n $ the **Orthogonal Group**, denoted by $ O(n)$.

- **Motivation: Why does it form a group?**
    
    - **Closure**: If $ A, B \in O(n)$, then $(AB)^T(AB) = B^T (A^T A) B = B^T I B = B^T B = I $, therefore $ AB $ is also an orthogonal matrix.
        
    - **Identity Element**: The identity matrix $ I $ obviously satisfies $ I^T I = I $.
        
    - **Inverse Element**: If $ A \in O(n)$, due to $ A^{-1} = A^T $, then $(A^{-1})^T(A^{-1}) = (A^T)^T A^T = A A^T = I $, the inverse is still orthogonal.
        

#### 2. Special Orthogonal Group— $ SO(n)$ For any orthogonal matrix $ A \in O(n)$, we take the determinant of both sides: $ A^T A = I $ Take the determinant of both sides:

$$
\det(A^T A) = \det(A^T)\det(A) = \det(A)^2 = \det(I) = 1 \implies \det(A) = \pm 1
$$

Based on the sign of the determinant, the orthogonal group is perfectly divided into two parts:

- **$\det(A) = 1 $**: Represents **pure rotation transformations** (which do not change the orientation of space).
    
- **$\det(A) = -1 $**: Represents **transformations that include mirror reflection** (which invert the right-handed and left-handed systems of space).
    

The subgroup formed by all orthogonal matrices of order $ 1 $ with determinant $ n $ is called the **special orthogonal group** of order $ n $, denoted as $ SO(n)$, and is often referred to as the **rotation group** in physics and engineering (such as the $ SO(3)$ describing the rotation of rigid bodies in three-dimensional space).

## The geometric rigidity and the operator expression of rotation

### I. Orthogonal Basis and Rigid Basis in Two-Dimensional Euclidean Space $\mathbb{R}^2 $

When we talk about the rigidity of Euclidean space, the most intuitive language is "preserving the inner product." Whether it is a mirror reflection or a rotation around the origin, these transformations fundamentally maintain the length and the angle between vectors. In an algebraic framework, this operator that preserves geometric structure is materialized as an **orthogonal matrix**. We are accustomed to listing the properties of orthogonal matrices directly, but if we go back to the beginning, we find that an orthogonal matrix is not just a collection of standard orthonormal bases formed by its rows (or columns); it is the matrix embodiment of a rigid operator, an extension of the complex plane.

In Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^2 $, if we select a set of standard orthonormal bases $\{\alpha, \beta\}$, then any matrix $ A = [\alpha \quad \beta]$ formed by them as columns must satisfy the definition of an orthogonal matrix. Geometrically, this means that $\alpha $ and $\beta $ are both unit vectors and are mutually orthogonal ($\alpha \perp \beta $).

> **Motivation for Conception:** How to constrain two mutually perpendicular unit vectors using a single continuous parameter? The most natural algebraic description is the trigonometric function. Because any point on the unit circle can be parameterized by coordinates, this allows us to encompass the form of the entire set of bases with a single rotation angle.

Based on this, the two-dimensional orthogonal matrix naturally splits into two types of forms:

* **Type 1 (Determinant is 1)**:

$$
A_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

* **Type 2 (Determinant is -1)**:

$$
B_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & -\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

#### 1. Eigenstructure and Geometric Rotation of Type 1 Orthogonal Matrices $ A_\theta $

For the transformation $ X \mapsto A_\theta X $, its geometric effect is obvious: rotating a vector in the plane counterclockwise around the origin by an angle of $\theta $. However, when we try to find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors in the real number domain, unless $\theta $ is an integer multiple of $\pi $, we cannot find a vector whose "direction remains unchanged" on the real plane. This forces us to broaden our view to the complex domain.

To explore the intrinsic structure of the rotation transformation, we solve the characteristic equation $\det(\lambda I - A_\theta) = 0 $, which is:

$$
(\lambda - \cos\theta)^2 + \sin^2\theta = 0 \implies \lambda = \cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta = e^{\pm i\theta}
$$

Bringing the complex eigenvalues back, we can perform a complex similarity diagonalization on the matrix $ A_\theta $:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -i & i \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -i & i \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta + i\sin\theta & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta - i\sin\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

This reveals a profound essence: **a real rotation in the two-dimensional plane is merely a pure stretch along two conjugate complex vector directions in the complex space**. The complex domain here serves as the pupil that allows us to see the underlying logic of geometric rotation.

#### 2. Reflection Eigen-decomposition of Type 2 Orthogonal Matrices $ B_\theta $

When the determinant becomes $-1 $, the matrix $ B_\theta $ loses the ability to preserve the space's orientation (chirality) and degenerates into a mirror reflection. We can gain insight into its internal structure through a subtle algebraic rearrangement:

$$
B_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & -\cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) & -\sin(\theta/2) \\ \sin(\theta/2) & \cos(\theta/2) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) & \sin(\theta/2) \\ -\sin(\theta/2) & \cos(\theta/2) \end{bmatrix}
$$

> **Geometric Motivation for Rearrangement:** The structure of this three-factor product is $ P M P^{-1}$. Here, the middle matrix is the standard mirror reflection across the $ y $ axis, while the matrices on the left and right represent rotating the coordinate axes by $\theta/2 $. This shows that the so-called Type 2 orthogonal transformation is essentially a **mirror reflection across a line that makes an angle of $\theta/2 $ with the $ x $ axis**.

If we take a unit vector $ X = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\omega \\ \sin\omega \end{bmatrix}$ in the direction $\omega $, after the action of $ B_\theta $, we get:

$$
AX = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta\cos\omega + \sin\theta\sin\omega \\ \sin\theta\cos\omega - \cos\theta\sin\omega \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta - \omega) \\ \sin(\theta - \omega) \end{bmatrix}
$$

From a physical geometric perspective, the initial angle is $\omega $, and the transformed angle is $\theta - \omega $. The arithmetic mean of the two is exactly $(\omega + \theta - \omega)/2 = \theta/2 $. This extremely intuitively proves that the transformation is always symmetric with respect to the $\theta/2 $ ray; it is indeed a standard reflection operator.

### II. Group Composition and Algebraic Structure of 2D Transformations

The composition of rotation and reflection operators is not random; they form a rigorous algebraic closed loop (i.e., the orthogonal group $ O(2)$ and its subgroup $ SO(2)$). We have the following operational rules:

1. **Rotation and Rotation Composition**: $ A_\theta A_\omega = A_{\theta+\omega}$. This shows that continuous rotations add up directly in angle, satisfying the commutative law.
2. **Reflection and Reflection Composition**: $ B_\theta B_\omega = A_{\theta-\omega}$. The superposition of two reflections that change chirality is geometrically equivalent to a pure rotation, with an angle equal to twice the angle between the two reflection axes.
3. **Rotation and Reflection Composition**: $ A_\theta B_\omega = B_{\theta+\omega}$ and $ B_\omega A_{-\theta} = B_{\theta+\omega}$. This reflects the non-commutativity of the group, and also indicates that the interaction between rotation and reflection still results in a reflection.

### III. Derivation of Rotation and Reflection in Three-Dimensional Euclidean Space

When we elevate the dimension to three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3 $, the complexity of rigid transformations increases sharply due to the extra degree of freedom. However, by using first principles, we can still decompose the core of the high dimension using conclusions from lower dimensions.

#### 1. Type 3 Orthogonal Matrix ($\det(A) = 1 $) — The Essence of Spatial Rotation

**[Theorem Proof 1] Proving that $\lambda = 1 $ must be an eigenvalue of $ A $.**

> **Motivation:** If we can prove the existence of $\lambda = 1 $, it means that in three-dimensional space, there must exist a vector that remains unchanged after the transformation; the direction of this fixed vector is the **axis of rotation** of the space.

We need to examine the value of the characteristic polynomial at $\lambda = 1 $, i.e., determine if the matrix $ A - I $ is singular. Using the properties of orthogonal matrices $ A^T A = I $ and $\det(A) = 1 $:

$$
\det(A - I) = \det(A - A A^T) = \det(A (I - A^T)) = \det(A) \cdot \det(I - A^T) = 1 \cdot \det(I - A)^T = \det(I - A)
$$

Since $ A $ is a 3rd order matrix, for any 3rd order matrix, we have $\det(I - A) = (-1)^3 \det(A - I) = -\det(A - I)$.
Therefore, we get:

$$
\det(A - I) = -\det(A - I) \implies 2\det(A - I) = 0 \implies \det(A - I) = 0
$$

From this, we deduce that the matrix $ A - I $ must be singular, and the homogeneous linear system $(A - I)x = 0 $ has a non-zero solution. That is, **$\lambda = 1 $ must be an eigenvalue of the matrix $ A $**.

**[Standard Form Extension and Dimensionality Reduction 2]**

Since $\lambda = 1 $ is an eigenvalue, we can take its corresponding unit eigenvector and denote it as $\alpha_1 $, satisfying $ A\alpha_1 = \alpha_1 $. To study the dynamics of the entire space, we need to construct a coordinated coordinate system. According to the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization or basis extension theorem, $\alpha_1 $ can be extended to a set of standard orthonormal bases $\mathbb{R}^3 $ $\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\}$.

> **Proof: $\{\alpha_1, A\alpha_2, A\alpha_3\}$ is still an orthonormal basis, and the transformation has the property of invariance of the orthogonal subspace.**
> Because orthogonal transformations preserve the inner product, and $ A\alpha_1 = \alpha_1 $, we directly examine the inner product:
> 
>

$$
(\alpha_1, A\alpha_2) = (A\alpha_1, A\alpha_2) = (\alpha_1, \alpha_2) = 0
$$

> 
>

$$
(\alpha_1, A\alpha_3) = (A\alpha_1, A\alpha_3) = (\alpha_1, \alpha_3) = 0
$$

> 
>

> This shows that the vectors $ A\alpha_2 $ and $ A\alpha_3 $ are still orthogonal to $\alpha_1 $. In other words, the two-dimensional orthogonal complement space spanned by $\{\alpha_2, \alpha_3\}$ is **invariant** under the transformation $ A $ (i.e., $ A\alpha_2, A\alpha_3 \in \text{span}\{\alpha_2, \alpha_3\}$). Furthermore:

A\alpha_2 = \cos\theta \alpha_2 + \sin\theta \alpha_3

A\alpha_3 = -\sin\theta \alpha_2 + \cos\theta \alpha_3

A P = P \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} \implies \mathbf{P^{-1}AP = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}}

**Conclusion:** Algebraically, this provides the clearest geometric characterization—**any first-kind 3rd-order orthogonal transformation is essentially a rigid transformation that rotates by an angle of $\theta $ around a specific axis $\alpha_1 $**.

When the determinant is $-1 $, the space structure must necessarily include an odd number of mirror reflections. Its structural derivation forms a mirror symmetry with the first kind:

**[Theorem Proof 1] Proof that $\lambda = -1 $ must be an eigenvalue of $ A $.**

### Five. Space Conjugate Lemma for Rotation Operators: The Algebraic View of Basis Transformation

When studying three-dimensional rigid body rotations, we often encounter this problem: If we know a rotation is performed around a standard axis (such as the $ z $ axis), what happens to the matrix form of the operator when the rotation axis is transformed to an arbitrary direction?

> Let $ A, B \in SO(3)$, where the transformation corresponding to $ A $ is a right-hand rotation by an angle of $\theta $ around the unit vector $\alpha $ (denoted as $\rho(\alpha, \theta)$). Then, **the transformation corresponding to $ BAB^T $ is a right-hand rotation by an angle of $\theta $ around the unit vector $ B\alpha $**, which is:
> 
>

$$
\rho(B\alpha, \theta) = B \rho(\alpha, \theta) B^T \quad \iff \quad B \rho(\alpha, \theta) = \rho(B\alpha, \theta) B
$$

```
   α ───────── around α rotation θ (operator A) ─────────► Aα
   │                                           ▲
   │                                           │
applies a space transformation B                               applies a space transformation B
   │                                           │
   ▼                                           │
  Bα ──── around Bα rotation θ (operator B A Bᵀ) ──────────┘
```

> **Motivation and First Principles Decoding:** > The conjugate composite form of the matrix $ B A B^T $ (since $ B $ is an orthogonal matrix, i.e., $ B A B^{-1}$) geometrically represents the **“shifting of the coordinate system”**.
> 
> Its physical execution steps are read from right to left:
> 
> 1. $ B^T $ (i.e., $ B^{-1}$): **Reversing** the vector currently in the new space (after transformation) back to the initial reference frame;
>     
> 2. $\rho(\alpha, \theta)$: In the initial reference frame, around the original rotation axis $\alpha $ to rotate $\theta $ angle;
>     
> 3. $ B $: **Forward shifting** the rotated vector entirely back to the new space.
>     
> 
> The net effect of this series of movements is macroscopically equivalent to "rotating directly around the new axis in the new space", $ B\alpha $ angle". This is the underlying logic of the mutual conversion between "active transformation" and "passive transformation" in modern physics and computer graphics. $\theta $ II.

### Parameterization: $ SO(3)$ The algebraic composition mechanism of Euler Angles

To describe the arbitrary orientation of a rigid body (such as an airplane or spacecraft) in three-dimensional space, three degrees of freedom are usually required. The essence of Euler angles is to decompose a complex single spatial rotation into a composition of three classical two-dimensional rotations around specific axes.

In aerospace engineering, the most intuitive description of attitude is **Yaw ($\psi $)**, **Pitch ($\theta $)**, and **Roll ($\phi $)**. However, in algebraic derivations, crystallography and analytical mechanics favor **Euler angles ($ z\text{-}x\text{-}z $)**: that is, any first-class orthogonal transformation can be decomposed into a rotation about the $ z $ axis, followed by a rotation about the new $ x $ axis, and finally a rotation about the newest $ z $ axis.

For quantitative calculation, we first write down the two-block operators for rotation about the base axes:

$$
A_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{around } z \text{ axis}), \qquad B_\omega = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\omega & -\sin\omega \\ 0 & \sin\omega & \cos\omega \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{around } x \text{ axis})
$$

However, continuous rotation makes the order of multiplication of the composite matrix extremely subtle because "every rotation carries away the axis needed for the next rotation." Here, two completely opposite yet mathematically equivalent representations are separated:

#### 1. Intrinsic / Body-fixed Coordinate System Representation

If every rotation is performed about the coordinate axes **currently attached to and moving with the rigid body** (i.e., the body-fixed axes):

- First: A rotation of $\psi $ angle about the body's own $ z $ axis, at which point the body's $ x $ axis is moved to a new position $ x'$;
    
- Second: A rotation of $\theta $ angle about the body's **current new position** $ x'$ axis, at which point the body's $ z $ axis is moved to the newest position $ z''$;
    
- Third: A rotation of $\phi $ angle about the body's **newest position** $ z''$ axis.
    

> **Body-fixed Multiplication Rule:** The order of multiplication for the algebraic composite matrix of this intuitive body-fixed transformation is **exactly the same (from left to right)** as the intuitive execution order:
> 
>

$$
M_{\text{intrinsic}} = A_\psi B_\theta A_\phi
$$

#### 2. Extrinsic / Space-fixed Coordinate System Representation

If we stare fixedly at the **absolutely stationary space reference frame** throughout the process, requiring that every rotation axis must be an absolute axis that does not move in space:

> **Space-fixed Multiplication Rule:** If the composite is performed with respect to the space-fixed axes, the multiplication order must be **completely reversed (from right to left)**:
> 
>

$$
M_{\text{extrinsic}} = A_\phi B_\theta A_\psi
$$

#### 3. Core Theorem: Derivation of Equivalence between Body-fixed and Space-fixed Representations

When representing a body-fixed rotation using a space-fixed coordinate system, the form undergoes an inversion. Starting from first principles and the preceding "Conjugate Lemma," we provide its **complete dynamical proof**:

> **Proof:**
> 
> The initial rotation of the rigid body is by $\psi $ about the initial space-fixed system's $ z $ axis, with the operator being $ A_\psi $.
> 
> Step two, we need to rotate around the **moving axis** $ x'$ rotation $\theta $. How is this moving axis $ x'$ ? It is the result of the original stationary $ x $ axis after the action of the first step, $ A_\psi $ that is, $ x' = A_\psi(x)$.
> 
> According to our **Conjugate Transformation Lemma** $\rho(B\alpha, \theta) = B\rho(\alpha, \theta)B^T $, the matrix for moving the coordinate system here is $ A_\psi $, and the original rotation about the space-fixed axis is $ B_\theta $. Therefore, the actual operator for rotating by $\theta $ about the body-fixed axis $ x'$ is transformed to:
> 
>

$$
R_2 = A_\psi B_\theta A_\psi^T
$$

> 
> At this point, the cumulative total rotation operator for the first two steps is:
> 
>

$$
R_{\text{total, 2}} = R_2 \cdot A_\psi = (A_\psi B_\theta A_\psi^T) A_\psi = A_\psi B_\theta
$$

> 
> Step three, we need to rotate around the **newest moving axis**. $ z''$ rotation $\phi $This $ z''$ axis is the original stationary $ z $ axis that has undergone the cumulative transformation of the previous two steps $ R_{\text{total, 2}}$ the product after $ z'' = (A_\psi B_\theta)(z)$.
> 
> Applying the Conjugate Transformation Lemma again, the actual operator for rotating by $\phi $ about the body-fixed axis $ z''$ is:
> 
>

$$
R_3 = (A_\psi B_\theta) A_\phi (A_\psi B_\theta)^T = A_\psi B_\theta A_\phi B_\theta^T A_\psi^T
$$

> 
> Finally, multiplying the third-step operator from the left onto the total result of the first two steps yields the complete body-fixed composite operator:
> 
>

$$
M = R_3 \cdot R_{\text{total, 2}} = (A_\psi B_\theta A_\phi B_\theta^T A_\psi^T) \cdot (A_\psi B_\theta)
$$

> 
> Using the cancellation law for orthogonal matrices ($ A_\psi^T A_\psi = I $ and $ B_\theta^T B_\theta = I $), we see that the intermediate terms cancel out like falling dominoes:
> 
>

$$
M = A_\psi B_\theta A_\phi \underbrace{B_\theta^T A_\psi^T A_\psi B_\theta}_{I} = A_\psi B_\theta A_\phi
$$

> 
> **Identity Expansion:**
> 
> We can reverse-engineer this result to completely see its relationship with the space-fixed axes:
> 
>

$$
A_\psi B_\theta A_\phi = (A_\psi B_\theta A_\phi B_\theta^{-1} A_\psi^{-1}) \cdot (A_\psi B_\theta A_\psi^{-1}) \cdot A_\psi
$$

> 
> **Conclusion:** Every rotation performed in the body-fixed coordinate system has the algebraic essence of "first transforming the coordinate axis back to its original space-fixed state, performing a rotation in the space-fixed coordinates, and then returning to the original state." This flawlessly explains why the matrix for the body-fixed representation is multiplied from left to right, while the space-fixed representation is multiplied from right to left.

## Symmetric Transformations and Spectral Structure

Separated from orthogonal transformations (rigid motions that preserve handedness and length), another extremely noble class of operators in linear space is the symmetric transformation. They are not responsible for "rotating" the space, but for undergoing **pure stretching and deformation** along certain specific, interleaved directions.

### 1. Strict Definition

If a linear transformation $ A $ satisfies the following inner product symmetry:

$$
(A\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, A\beta), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in V
$$

Then $ A $ is called a **symmetric transformation**.

### 2. Coordinate Proof

> **Theorem: $ A $ is a symmetric transformation $\iff A $ whose matrix in the standard orthonormal basis is a real symmetric matrix ($ A^T = A $).**

**Proof:**

Select a set of standard orthonormal bases $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n\}$. By definition, the matrix elements of the transformation operator in this basis $ a_{ij}$ satisfy:

$$
A\alpha_j = a_{1j}\alpha_1 + \dots + a_{nj}\alpha_n = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{kj}\alpha_k
$$

Since the basis is orthonormal ($(\alpha_i, \alpha_k) = \delta_{ik}$), we use the inner product to extract the coefficients:

$$
(\alpha_i, A\alpha_j) = \Big(\alpha_i, \sum_{k=1}^n a_{kj}\alpha_k\Big) = a_{ij}
$$

Similarly, applying $ A $ from the left:

$$
(A\alpha_i, \alpha_j) = (\alpha_j, A\alpha_i) = a_{ji}
$$

If $ A $ is a symmetric transformation, then by definition, $(\alpha_i, A\alpha_j) = (A\alpha_i, \alpha_j)$ must hold, and substituting this into the equation above immediately yields:

$$
a_{ij} = a_{ji}
$$

That is, the element in the $ i $ row and $ j $ column of the matrix equals the element in the $ j $ row and $ i $ column, meaning the matrix $ A $ must be a real symmetric matrix. Q.E.D.

### 3. Spectral Theorem: The Ultimate Eigen-decomposition

One of the most core theorems in linear algebra:

$$
\text{symmetry transformation } A \iff \text{is a real symmetric matrix in any set of standard orthonormal bases} \iff \exists \text{ standard orthonormal basis } \{\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n\} \text{ such that the operator is completely diagonalizable}
$$

is that there exists a perfect spatial basis such that:

$$
A\beta_1 = \lambda_1 \beta_1, \quad A\beta_2 = \lambda_2 \beta_2, \quad \dots, \quad A\beta_n = \lambda_n \beta_n
$$

> **Critical Viewpoint and Geometric Summary:**
> 
> We can now contrast and critique **orthogonal transformations** and **symmetric transformations** from a unified macroscopic perspective:
> 
> - **Orthogonal transformation ($\det(A)=1 $)**: In the real domain, it stubbornly refuses to provide a sufficient set of real eigenvectors, tending to decouple the entire space into individual **two-dimensional rotation planes** (complex eigenvalues $ e^{\pm i\theta}$), forcing the space to undergo rigid rotation.
>     
> - **Symmetric transformation ($ A^T=A $)**: Extremely compliantly provides a framework in the entire space composed of a set of **mutually orthogonal real eigenvectors** $\{\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n\}$. In the space governed by it, there is no rotation, no change in chirality, only pure, clean linear stretching along these $ n $ orthogonal principal axes $\lambda_i $. This is the mathematical origin of quadratic form standardization, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) in image processing, and multivariate statistical physics.

# Matrix exponential mapping, the Lie algebra core of anti-symmetric operators, and high-dimensional orthogonal matrix decomposition
## I. From infinitesimal rotation to rigid rotation: The motivation of the matrix exponential map

In classical calculus, the exponential function of a constant $ a $ can be expanded as a Taylor series: $ e^a = 1 + a + \frac{1}{2!}a^2 + \frac{1}{3!}a^3 + \dots $. If we elevate the variable from a "number" to an "operator (matrix)", this algebraic form still possesses extremely powerful spatial topological force.

> **Motivation and First Principles:**
> 
> Imagine performing an infinitesimally small dynamic rotation in space. At an infinitesimal moment, the rate of change (velocity) of a vector $ X $ is perpendicular to itself, which can be characterized by an **anti-symmetric matrix $ C $** (i.e., $ C^T = -C $): $\frac{dX}{dt} = CX $.
> 
> This is a classic system of linear differential equations, whose formal solution is precisely $ X(t) = e^{Ct}X(0)$. Because the object's motion is rigid, it does not change the vector's length, meaning that **as time flows, the operator $ e^C $ must be able to perfectly preserve the inner product, meaning it must evolve into an orthogonal matrix**. The matrix exponential map is the natural bridge connecting the "infinitesimal rotation velocity (Lie algebra)" to the "macroscopic rotation state (Lie group)".

### 1. Proof of strict algebraic properties

**[Lemma Proof]If $ C $ is a real anti-symmetric matrix ($ C^T = -C $), prove that $ e^C $ must be a first-kind orthogonal matrix ($\det(e^C) = 1 $).**

- **Proof of Orthogonality**:
    
First, examine the transpose of $ e^C $ using the series expansion:

$$
\left(e^C\right)^T = \left(I + C + \frac{1}{2!}C^2 + \frac{1}{3!}C^3 + \dots\right)^T = I + C^T + \frac{1}{2!}(C^T)^2 + \frac{1}{3!}(C^T)^3 + \dots
$$

Substitute the anti-symmetric condition $ C^T = -C $:

$$
\left(e^C\right)^T = I + (-C) + \frac{1}{2!}(-C)^2 + \frac{1}{3!}(-C)^3 + \dots = I - C + \frac{1}{2!}C^2 - \frac{1}{3!}C^3 + \dots = e^{-C}
$$

Since $ C $ and $-C $ clearly satisfy the commutative law of multiplication ($ C(-C) = (-C)C $), by the property of the matrix exponential:

$$
\left(e^C\right)^T \cdot e^C = e^{-C} \cdot e^C = e^{-C + C} = e^0 = I
$$

It is thus concluded that, **$ e^C $ must be an orthogonal matrix**.
    
- **Proof that the determinant is 1**:
    
Using the important matrix trace and determinant identity $\det(e^A) = e^{\text{Tr}(A)}$:
    
For an anti-symmetric matrix $ C $, its main diagonal elements must all be $ 0 $ (because $ c_{ii} = -c_{ii} \implies c_{ii} = 0 $), so its trace is $ 0 $:

$$
\text{Tr}(C) = 0 \implies \det(e^C) = e^{\text{Tr}(C)} = e^0 = 1
$$

This proves that $ e^C $ is not only orthogonal but also preserves handedness, **it must belong to the first-kind orthogonal matrix $ SO(n)$**.
    

## II. Algebraic reorganization of 2D infinitesimal rotation

To see the operational details of the exponential map, we first perform a baseline calculation in the two-dimensional plane $\mathbb{R}^2 $. We define the most basic two-dimensional anti-symmetric matrix:

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\theta \\ \theta & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

To find $ e^C $, we need to calculate the higher powers of $ C $. Through simple matrix multiplication, we find that its powers exhibit an elegant periodicity:

$$
C^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\theta \\ \theta & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\theta \\ \theta & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\theta^2 & 0 \\ 0 & -\theta^2 \end{bmatrix} = -\theta^2 I
$$

$$
C^3 = C^2 \cdot C = -\theta^2 C, \qquad C^4 = (C^2)^2 = \theta^4 I, \qquad C^5 = \theta^4 C \dots
$$

Bringing this periodic law into the exponential Taylor series expansion, and regrouping by grouping according to $ I $ and $ C $:

$$
e^C = I + C + \frac{1}{2!}C^2 + \frac{1}{3!}C^3 + \frac{1}{4!}C^4 + \frac{1}{5!}C^5 + \dots
$$

$$
e^C = I + C + \frac{1}{2!}(-\theta^2 I) + \frac{1}{3!}(-\theta^2 C) + \frac{1}{4!}(\theta^4 I) + \frac{1}{5!}(\theta^4 C) + \dots
$$

$$
e^C = \left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \dots\right)I + \left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{3!} + \frac{\theta^4}{5!} - \dots\right)\frac{1}{\theta}C
$$

> **Convergence of Motivations:**
> 
> Observing the infinite series within these two sets of parentheses, they are exactly the Maclaurin series for the sine and cosine functions!
> 
>

$$
\left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \dots\right) = \cos\theta
$$

> 
>

$$
\left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \dots\right) = \sin\theta
$$

> 
> Therefore, substituting back into the matrix form:
> 
>

$$
e^C = \cos\theta \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -\theta \\ \theta & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

> 
> **Conclusion:** The final regrouping result from the pure algebraic expansion is **completely consistent** with the planar pure rotation matrix derived from geometry earlier. This indicates that the parameter $\theta $ in the anti-symmetric matrix perfectly translates into the macroscopic rotation angle in space after the exponential mapping.

When performing high-order power operations on it, due to the independence of the block diagonal, the squared term becomes:

$$
(P^T C P)^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\theta^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -\theta^2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

From this, we can obtain the zeroing elimination law of the three-dimensional characteristic polynomial: **$ C^3 = -\theta^2 C $** (This is the core simplification of the famous Rodrigues' operator).

Finally, utilizing the exponential property of similar matrices $ e^C = e^{P (P^T C P) P^T} = P e^{P^T C P} P^T $, we can perfectly write out the diagonalization block in the $\beta_1 $ basis:

$$
e^{P^T C P} = \begin{bmatrix} e^0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Final Qualitative Result:** The exponential of any 3rd-order real anti-symmetric matrix naturally corresponds to a rigid rotation of an angle of magnitude $\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}$ around a specific axis (the normal determined by the non-zero elements) in the space's topology.

## IV. Panorama of "Quasi-Diagonalization" for High-Dimensional Orthogonal Transformations

When we stand at the highest macro level, transcending the limitations of two and three dimensions, and look at orthogonal transformations in an arbitrary finite-dimensional Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n $, the last theorem presents us with an extremely stunning picture of space structure:

> **[Orthogonal Transformation Spectrum Theorem]**
> 
> If a linear transformation $ A $ is a generalized orthogonal transformation (satisfying $ A^T A = I $ in any standard orthonormal basis), then there must exist a set of standard orthonormal bases for the entire space such that the operator $ A $'s matrix in this basis can be perfectly **decomposed into the following non-interfering quasi-diagonal block form**:

$$
\mathbf{M_{\text{standard}} = \begin{bmatrix}

\pm 1 & & & & & \\

& \pm 1 & & & & \\

& & \ddots & & & \\

& & & \begin{matrix} \cos\theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 \\ \sin\theta_1 & \cos\theta_1 \end{matrix} & & \\

& & & & \ddots & \\

& & & & & \begin{matrix} \cos\theta_m & -\sin\theta_m \\ \sin\theta_m & \cos\theta_m \end{matrix}

\end{bmatrix}}
$$

### Critical Perspective and Summary

This theorem is the ultimate symphony of the theory of rigid geometry in linear algebra. It reveals the extremely simple underlying essence of high-dimensional rigid motion:

1. **Degeneration of One-Dimensional Invariant Subspaces**: The top row of the matrix $\pm 1 $, representing the existence of several one-dimensional lines in the space. If it is $+1 $, it represents that the vector on this line remains **unchanged** after the transformation (like the axis of rotation in three dimensions); if it is $-1 $, it represents that the vector on this line is **mirror-reflected** after the transformation.
    
2. **Decoupling of Two-Dimensional Independent Rotation Planes**: The individual $ 2 \times 2 $ triangular matrix blocks in the bottom right represent that the high-dimensional space can be perfectly sliced into **mutually orthogonal two-dimensional planes**. Within each independent plane, the space is quietly rotating at a different angle $\theta_j $ around the origin.
    
3. **Uncoupled Geometric Picture**: This means that, regardless of the complexity of the rigid motion of a high-dimensional object (such as the flipping of a high-dimensional hypersphere), in its mathematical essence, **it can be decoupled into a superposition of independent axial stretches/reflections and two-dimensional plane rotations, all mutually orthogonal**. The motion in the high-dimensional space loses its messiness at this moment, leaving only the ultimate symmetry and tranquility of algebraic block decomposition.

# Orthogonal Transformation Panorama, Extremal Estimation, PCA Geometric Optimization, and Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

## I. The Ultimate Puzzle of Rigid Operators: The Cartan-Dieudonné Theorem

Previously, we discussed orthogonal transformations in two and three dimensions, and found that they could be perfectly decoupled into combinations of "pure rotations" and "mirror reflections." So, what is the geometric boundary of rigid transformations in a general $ n $ dimensional Euclidean space?

Cartan-Dieudonné Theorem: 

> **Theorem Statement**: Any orthogonal transformation in a $ n $ dimensional Euclidean space can be geometrically represented as the **product (composition) of no more than $ n $ mirror reflection transformations**.

> **Motivation and First Principles of the Conception**: 
> 
> Mirror reflection is the most fundamental rigid action that changes the space's orientation (chirality). This theorem shows that **mirror reflection is the "geometric atom" that constitutes all high-dimensional rigid motion**. A pure rotation (determinant of 1) generated by continuous motion is, in its algebraic essence, merely a domino-like superposition of an even number of reflection atoms. This not only simplifies the generator structure of the high-dimensional orthogonal group $ O(n)$ but also provides the ultimate theoretical legitimacy for computer graphics to use reflection operators (like the Householder transformation) to approximate any rotation matrix.

## II. The Energy Bound of Operators: Rayleigh Quotient and Quadratic Form Estimation

When we shift from "orthogonal operators" that preserve length to "real symmetric operators (real symmetric matrices $ A $)" that stretch the space, a core question arises: What is the maximum possible "energy variation amplitude" (i.e., the quadratic form $ X^T AX $) of a vector $ X $ under the action of this operator? And what is the minimum possible?

**Theorem on the Extremum of Quadratic Forms for Real Symmetric Matrices**: 

> For any non-zero column vector $ X \in \mathbb{R}^n $, its quadratic form is absolutely controlled by the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of the operator:
> 
>

$$
\lambda_n \|X\|^2 \le X^T AX \le \lambda_1 \|X\|^2 \quad \left( \iff \lambda_n \le \frac{X^T AX}{X^T X} \le \lambda_1 \right)
$$

> 
> Where $\lambda_1, \lambda_n $ are the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of the real symmetric matrix $ A $. Equality holds if and only if $ X $ lies in the corresponding eigenspace.

> **Geometric Motivation for Steps and Derivation**: 
> 
> Why are its extrema exactly the eigenvalues? Because according to the spectral theorem, the real symmetric matrix $ A $ possesses $ n $ mutually orthogonal unit eigenvector scaffolds $ P = [\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n]$, satisfying $ P^T AP = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.
> 
> If we observe the vector $ X $ in this "principal axis coordinate system" formed by the eigenvectors (let the new coordinates be $ Y = P^T X $, and $\|Y\|^2 = \|X\|^2 $), the quadratic form is instantly stripped of cross-terms, becoming a pure sum of squares:
> 
>

$$
X^T AX = Y^T (P^T AP) Y = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \dots + \lambda_n y_n^2
$$

> 
> **Manifestation of Motivation**: We want to maximize this sum while keeping the total length $\sum y_i^2 = \|X\|^2 $ fixed. The most greedy strategy is clearly to concentrate all components (energy) onto the axis corresponding to the largest coefficient $\lambda_1 $ (i.e., setting $ y_1 = \|X\|, y_{2 \dots n} = 0 $); similarly, the most conservative strategy is to push everything to $\lambda_n $. This extremely intuitively proves the boundary of the quadratic form's extremum, and geometrically declares: **the most extreme and sensitive direction of energy stretching caused by a real symmetric operator is precisely the direction of the eigenvectors**.

## III. Principal Component Analysis (PCA): Finding the Optimal Subspace for Geometric Dimensionality Reduction

In data science and high-dimensional statistical physics, we often encounter a large number of high-dimensional samples with noise $ A = [\alpha_1 \quad \alpha_2 \quad \dots \quad \alpha_n]$. We hope to find a low-dimensional subspace $ V_k = \langle \beta_1, \dots, \beta_k \rangle $ (e.g., $ k $ dimensional) to project all sample points onto, achieving the goal of "compressing the dimension while losing as little original information as possible."

### 1. Maximizing Projection Sum of Squares vs. Minimizing Sum of Squares of Perpendicular Distance

Let $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_k $ be a set of standard orthonormal bases for the subspace we wish to find.

- The **projection vector** of a sample point $\alpha $ onto this subspace can be represented as: $ B_k B_k^T \alpha = \sum_{i=1}^k (\beta_i^T \alpha)\beta_i $.
    
- The **vertical distance vector** (i.e., the lost information) of the sample point to this subspace is: $(I - B_k B_k^T)\alpha $.
    

By the Pythagorean theorem, for any sample point:

$$
\|\text{original vector } \alpha\|^2 = \|\text{projection vector } B_k B_k^T \alpha\|^2 + \|\text{perpendicular distance } (I - B_k B_k^T)\alpha\|^2
$$

Since the total energy of all sample points $\sum \|\alpha_i\|^2 = \text{tr}(A A^T)$ is a constant, **to minimize the lost information (sum of squares of perpendicular distances) is algebraically equivalent to maximizing the retained information (sum of squares of projections)**.

### 2. The Ultimate Intervention of the Spectral Theorem

When we write the sum of the squared projections of all sample points in matrix form, it becomes the analytical expression: $\sum_{j=1}^k \beta_j^T (AA^T) \beta_j $.

Note! The $ AA^T $ here is naturally a **real symmetric matrix** (scatter matrix/covariance matrix), which is poised, waiting for decomposition by the spectral theorem.

To maximize this expression, according to the Rayleigh quotient extreme estimation, we should greedily select $ AA^T $ the $ k $ largest eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_k $ and the corresponding unit eigenvectors as the basis.

At this point, the remaining maximum projection energy is $\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i $. The corresponding **minimum sum of squared perpendicular distances** (error) is naturally the remaining discarded energy:

$$
\text{Min Error} = \text{tr}(AA^T) - \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i = \lambda_{k+1} + \dots + \lambda_m
$$

This perfectly derives the mathematical origin of PCA from first principles: **The so-called principal components are, geometrically, the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $ AA^T $; and the sum of the discarded small eigenvalues is exactly the geometric projection loss that cannot be recovered after dimensionality reduction.**

## Four. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD): A Panorama of Space Stretching by Linear Mapping

While the spectral theorem for the diagonalization of real symmetric matrices is perfect, it requires the matrix to be a "square matrix" and to satisfy symmetry. If we are faced with a general real matrix $ m \times n $ $ A $ (for example, a general linear transformation mapping from an $ n $ dimensional input space to an $ m $ dimensional output space), can we still find a similar tranquil geometric picture?

Finally, we present the most general eigen-reconstruction in linear algebra—the **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)**:

> **Theorem Statement**: Any real matrix $ m \times n $ $ A $ can be perfectly written in a block composite form:
> 
>

$$
A = P S Q^T
$$

> 
> Where $ P $ is an orthogonal matrix of order $ m $, and $ Q $ is an orthogonal matrix of order $ n $. $ S $ is a generalized diagonal matrix of shape $ m \times n $, whose diagonal elements $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0 $ are called **Singular Values**, which are the square roots of the positive eigenvalues of $ A A^T $ (or $ A^T A $).

### 1. Three-Stage Geometric Dynamics Decoding of SVD

Reading the operator composition $ A X = P (S (Q^T X))$ from right to left, any general linear mapping can be elegantly decomposed into three physical actions:

1. **$ Q^T $ (Rotation/Reflection)**: A rigid coordinate transformation in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n $. This set of new bases $\{ \gamma_1, \gamma_2 \}$ are the eigenvectors of $ A^T A $ (the standard orthonormal frame of the domain).
    
2. **$ S $ (Independent Axial Stretching)**: In the intermediate stage of the transformation, there is no tilting or rotation, only a simple, violent linear stretching of the vector components along the corresponding standard axes, with the stretching factor being the singular value $\sigma_i $.
    
3. **$ P $ (Rotation/Reflection)**: Finally, the stretched result is subjected to another rigid transformation in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m $, landing on the final standard orthonormal basis $\{ \beta_1, \beta_2 \}$ (this set of bases are the eigenvectors of $ A A^T $).
    

### 2. The Ultimate Geometric Picture: Transformation from Hypersphere to Hyperellipsoid

The physical essence of SVD:

> What exactly does the linear mapping $ X \mapsto AX $ do to the world?
> 
> It takes a **undirected unit hypersphere** ($\|X\| \le 1 $) in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n $, and after undergoing the space mapping, stretches and reconstructs it into a **hyperellipsoid** in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m $.

- The **geometric lengths** of the various semi-axes of this hyperellipsoid are exactly the non-zero **singular values $\sigma_i $**;
    
- The **spatial physical orientation** of the principal axes of this hyperellipsoid is uniquely determined by the orthonormal basis vectors **$\beta_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i}A\gamma_i $** of the output space.
    

### Conjugate Transformations and Normal Matrices

In Euclidean space, when we abstract geometric or algebraic operations into linear transformations, we often need to find some kind of "mirror" or "balancing" operation. This mapping, which achieves a symmetric balance with the original transformation under the inner product structure, is the conjugate transformation.

#### I. Definition and Inner Product Balance of Conjugate Transformations

**Motivation**

In linear algebra, the inner product $\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle $ endows the space with a metric structure. If a linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$ acts on one vector, we naturally ask: can this transformation be "transferred" to another vector while keeping the inner product result unchanged?

Since the inner product symbol used in the figure is $(\alpha, \beta)$, we will use this symbol in this note.

To achieve this transfer of transformation, we need to introduce a new transformation $\mathcal{A}^*$.

**Definition**

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a linear transformation on the Euclidean space $ V $. If there exists another linear transformation $\mathcal{A}^*$ such that:

$$
(\mathcal{A}\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, \mathcal{A}^*\beta), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in V
$$

Then $\mathcal{A}^*$ is called the **conjugate transformation (adjoint operator)** of $\mathcal{A}$.

**Typical Examples**

- **Orthogonal Transformation $ U $**: Preserves the inner product, i.e., $(U\alpha, U\beta) = (\alpha, \beta)$. By rearranging terms according to the definition, its conjugate transformation is the inverse transformation: $ U^* = U^{-1}$.
    
- **Symmetric Transformation $ A $**: It is symmetric in the inner product itself, so its conjugate transformation is itself: $ A^* = A $.
    

#### II. Matrix Representation and Uniqueness Proof of Conjugate Transformations in Finite-Dimensional Euclidean Space

**Motivation**

In abstract spaces, does a transformation $\mathcal{A}^*$ satisfying the inner product equality necessarily exist? If it exists, is it unique? In finite-dimensional spaces, linear transformations can be fully characterized by matrices. Therefore, by introducing a set of standard orthonormal bases, we can transfer the abstract transformation to concrete matrix operations. In matrix multiplication, the transpose operation precisely plays the role of "transferring the action of the left matrix to the right".

##### 1. Preparation of Coordinates and Inner Products in Matrix Form

Let $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ be a set of **standard orthonormal bases** for the finite-dimensional Euclidean space $ V $. Let the matrices of the linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$ and its candidate conjugate transformation $\mathcal{A}^*$ in this basis be denoted by $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$, respectively, such that:

$$
\mathcal{A}(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\beta_1 \dots \beta_n)\mathbf{A}
$$

$$
\mathcal{A}^*(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\beta_1 \dots \beta_n)\mathbf{B}
$$

The coordinate column vector of an arbitrary vector $\alpha, \beta $ in this basis is $\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}$. Since we are in a standard orthonormal basis, the inner product of the space can be directly converted to the inner product of the coordinates (i.e., the standard inner product):

$$
(\alpha, \beta) = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{I} \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{Y}
$$

At this point, applying the linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$ to $\alpha $ results in coordinates $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}$. We write the matrix form for both sides of the equation:

- Left-hand side: $(\mathcal{A}\alpha, \beta) = (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{X})^T \mathbf{I} \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{Y}$
    
- Right-hand side: $(\alpha, \mathcal{A}^*\beta) = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{I} (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{Y}) = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{Y}$
    

##### 2. Rigorous Two-Way Derivation

With the preparation of the above matrix forms, the existence, uniqueness, and matrix representation of the conjugate transformation can be derived seamlessly:

$$
\mathcal{A}^* \text{ is } \mathcal{A} \text{ the conjugate transformation}
$$

$$
\iff (\mathcal{A}\alpha, \beta) = (\alpha, \mathcal{A}^*\beta), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in V
$$

$$
\iff \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{B} \mathbf{Y}, \quad \forall \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

$$
\iff \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{A}^T
$$

**Conclusion**

Since a linear transformation and its corresponding matrix are in one-to-one correspondence under a fixed basis. And for any matrix $\mathbf{A}$, its transpose matrix $\mathbf{A}^T $ **exists and is unique**, which directly implies: **In a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, the conjugate transformation $\mathcal{A}^*$ of any linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$ exists and is unique**. In the same standard orthonormal basis, if the matrix of $\mathcal{A}$ is $\mathbf{A}$, then the matrix of $\mathcal{A}^*$ is $\mathbf{A}^T $.

#### III. Real Normal Transformations and Normal Matrices

**Motivation**

Since every linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$ is accompanied by a conjugate transformation $\mathcal{A}^*$, do these two satisfy the commutative law when composed? We know that matrix multiplication generally does not satisfy the commutative law ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T \neq \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}$). But if they happen to commute, such transformations exhibit excellent symmetry and spectral properties in structure (can be orthogonally diagonalized). We call such well-behaved transformations "normal" transformations.

**Definition: Real Normal Transformation**

If a linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$ and its conjugate transformation $\mathcal{A}^*$ commute, i.e.:

$$
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^* = \mathcal{A}^*\mathcal{A}
$$

Then $\mathcal{A}$ is called a **real normal transformation**.

- **Examples**: Orthogonal transformations $ U $ (because $ UU^{-1}=U^{-1}U $), symmetric transformations $ A $ (because $ AA=AA $), and anti-symmetric transformations $ A $ (whose conjugate is $-A $, satisfying $ A(-A)=(-A)A $); these transformations are special cases of real normal transformations.
    

**Definition: Normal Matrix**

If a real matrix $\mathbf{A}$ satisfies:

$$
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}
$$

Then $\mathbf{A}$ is called a **real normal matrix**.

**Proposition**

The linear transformation $\mathcal{A}$ is a real normal transformation **if and only if** the matrix of $\mathcal{A}$ in the standard orthonormal basis is a real normal matrix.

**Proof**

Based on the correspondence between operators and matrices, in the standard orthonormal basis, the composition of transformations corresponds to the multiplication of matrices.

$$
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^* = \mathcal{A}^*\mathcal{A} \iff \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}
$$

End of proof.

### Structure Theorem for Real Normal Matrices and Extension to Real Diagonalization

Over the complex field, by the spectral theorem, any normal matrix can be diagonalized by a unitary matrix. However, when we restrict to the **real field $\mathbb{R}$**, since the characteristic roots of real polynomials can be complex (appearing in conjugate pairs), real normal matrices often cannot be directly diagonalized into a real diagonal matrix.

To perfectly characterize the geometric structure of normal matrices within the real field, we need to use **invariant subspaces** to decompose them into a highly regular "block-diagonal" standard form.

#### I. Core Foundation: Invariant Subspaces and the Closedness of the Orthogonal Complement

**Motivation**

A common strategy for studying a complex linear transformation is "divide and conquer"—finding smaller subspaces within the space that remain closed under the action of the transformation. If the orthogonal complement of such a subspace also remains closed, we can decompose the entire space into an orthogonal direct sum, thereby decomposing the large matrix into independent blocks.

**Definition: Invariant Subspace**

Let $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbf{M}_n(\mathbb{R})$. If the subspace $ W $ of $\mathbb{R}^n $ satisfies:

$$
\mathbf{A}W \subseteq W \quad (\text{that for any } w \in W, \text{ have } \mathbf{A}w \in W)
$$

Then $ W $ is called an **invariant subspace** of $\mathbf{A}$.

##### Proof 1): Every real matrix has a 1-dimensional or 2-dimensional invariant subspace

**Steps and Geometric Motivation:**

The characteristic polynomial of a linear transformation $ f(\lambda) = \det(\lambda \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})$ is a polynomial with real coefficients. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, it must have solutions in the complex field.

- **Case 1: Existence of a real characteristic root $\lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}$**
    
    At this point, there exists a corresponding real eigenvector $ v \in \mathbb{R}^n $ such that $\mathbf{A}v = \lambda_0 v $.
Clearly, the 1-dimensional subspace spanned by $ v $ satisfies $ W = \text{span}\{v\}$ Satisfy $\mathbf{A}W \subseteq W $.
    
- **Case 2: No real characteristic roots, all roots are conjugate complex roots $\alpha \pm i\beta $ ($\beta \neq 0 $)**
    
    Let $\mathbf{A}(x + iy) = (\alpha + i\beta)(x + iy)$, where $ x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n $. Expanding the complex equation and separating the real and imaginary parts yields:

$$
\begin{cases} \mathbf{A}x = \alpha x - \beta y \\ \mathbf{A}y = \beta x + \alpha y \end{cases}
$$

This shows that the result of applying $\mathbf{A}$ to the real vectors $ x $ and $ y $ still lies within the space spanned by $\{x, y\}$. Therefore, $ W = \text{span}\{x, y\}$ forms a 2-dimensional invariant subspace.
    

##### Proof 2): If $ W $ is an invariant subspace of the real normal matrix $\mathbf{A}$, then $ W^\perp $ is also an invariant subspace of $\mathbf{A}$

**Steps and Algebraic Motivation:**

For a general matrix, the orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is not necessarily closed. But "normality" ($\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}$) provides a strong symmetry. We want to prove $\mathbf{A}(W^\perp) \subseteq W^\perp $, which is sufficient to prove that for any $ u \in W^\perp $ and $ w \in W $, $(\mathbf{A}u, w) = 0 $.

Using the inner product property of the conjugate transformation:

$$
(\mathbf{A}u, w) = (u, \mathbf{A}^T w)
$$

Since $ u \in W^\perp $, if we can prove $\mathbf{A}^T w \in W $, then the above inner product must be 0. Therefore, the problem reduces to proving: **"If $ W $ is an invariant subspace of $\mathbf{A}$, then $ W $ is also an invariant subspace of $\mathbf{A}^T $"**.

> **Lemma:** If $\mathbf{A}$ is a normal matrix, then for any vector $ x $, $\|\mathbf{A}x\| = \|\mathbf{A}^T x\|$.
> 
> _Proof:_ $\|\mathbf{A}x\|^2 = (\mathbf{A}x, \mathbf{A}x) = (x, \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}x) = (x, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T x) = (\mathbf{A}^T x, \mathbf{A}^T x) = \|\mathbf{A}^T x\|^2 $.

Using this lemma, it can be proven that $\mathbf{A}^T w \in W $ by constructing the trace of the operator or by restricting the characteristic polynomial of the operator. Since $\mathbf{A}^T w \in W $, for $ u \in W^\perp $, it must be that $(u, \mathbf{A}^T w) = 0 $.

Thus $(\mathbf{A}u, w) = 0 \implies \mathbf{A}u \in W^\perp $, which means $ W^\perp $ is also an invariant subspace of $\mathbf{A}$.

#### II. Spectral Decomposition Theorem for Real Normal Matrices (Block Diagonalization)

**Motivation**

Combining the properties 1) and 2) above, we can perform a recursive orthogonal direct sum decomposition on the entire space: $\mathbb{R}^n = W_1 \oplus W_2 \oplus \dots \oplus W_k $. On each 1-dimensional invariant subspace, the transformation acts as a real scaling; on each 2-dimensional invariant subspace, the transformation acts as an irreducible $ 2 \times 2 $ matrix. After orthonormalizing these bases, we obtain the standard form for a real normal matrix.

##### Theorem 3: Standard Form of a Real Normal Matrix

If $\mathbf{A}$ is a real normal matrix, then there exists an orthogonal matrix $\mathbf{P}$ such that:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{P} \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & & & & & \\ & \ddots & & & & \\ & & \lambda_s & & & \\ & & & \begin{matrix} a_1 & -b_1 \\ b_1 & a_1 \end{matrix} & & \\ & & & & \ddots & \\ & & & & & \begin{matrix} a_r & -b_r \\ b_r & a_r \end{matrix} \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{P}^T
$$

Here $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_s \in \mathbb{R}$ is a real eigenvalue of $\mathbf{A}$; $ a_i \pm ib_i $ ($ b_i \neq 0 $) are the complex conjugate eigenvalues of $\mathbf{A}$.

Where the block $ 2 \times 2 $ of $\begin{bmatrix} a_i & -b_i \\ b_i & a_i \end{bmatrix}$ geometrically corresponds to a rotation plus a uniform scaling.

#### III. Degenerate Standard Form for Special Normal Matrices

Due to the algebraic constraints of the matrix itself, the various blocks in the general formula are further restricted. The following three classic matrices are special cases of normal matrices:

##### Theorem 4: If $\mathbf{A}$ is an orthogonal matrix

**Motivation:** An orthogonal matrix satisfies $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T = \mathbf{I}$, which means it not only commutes but also preserves the length of all vectors (isometry).

- **Constraint on 1D Blocks:** Must satisfy $\lambda_i^2 = 1 \implies \lambda_i = \pm 1 $.
    
- **Constraint on 2D Blocks:** The eigenvalues must have a modulus of 1, i.e., $ a_i^2 + b_i^2 = 1 $. Therefore, we can set $ a_i = \cos\theta_i, b_i = \sin\theta_i $.
    

The standard form is:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{P} \begin{bmatrix} \pm 1 & & & & \\ & \ddots & & & \\ & & \pm 1 & & \\ & & & \begin{matrix} \cos\theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 \\ \sin\theta_1 & \cos\theta_1 \end{matrix} & \\ & & & & \ddots \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{P}^T
$$

**Geometric Interpretation:** Any real orthogonal transformation can be decomposed into an orthogonal combination of several one-dimensional axial reflections ($\pm 1 $) and several pure rotations in two-dimensional planes (rotation matrices).

##### Theorem 5: If $\mathbf{A}$ is a skew-symmetric real matrix

**Motivation:** A skew-symmetric matrix satisfies $\mathbf{A}^T = -\mathbf{A}$, and thus $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{A}^T = -\mathbf{A}^2 = \mathbf{A}^T\mathbf{A}$. Its eigenvalues must be purely imaginary.

- **Constraint on 1D Blocks:** The only real purely imaginary number is $ 0 $, so $\lambda_i = 0 $.
    
- **Constraint on 2D Blocks:** The eigenvalues $ a_i \pm ib_i $ must be purely imaginary $\implies a_i = 0 $.
    

The standard form is:

$$
\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{P} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & & & & \\ & \ddots & & & \\ & & 0 & & \\ & & & \begin{matrix} 0 & -b_1 \\ b_1 & 0 \end{matrix} & \\ & & & & \ddots \end{bmatrix} \mathbf{P}^T
$$

**Geometric Interpretation:** A skew-symmetric matrix is completely projected to 0 on a 1D invariant subspace, and in a 2D space, it manifests as pairs of mutually orthogonal 'vortex-like' skew-symmetric blocks.
