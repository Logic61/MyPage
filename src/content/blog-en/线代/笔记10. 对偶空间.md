Imagine you have an extremely precise measuring instrument that can only read the projection length of a vector in a specific direction within a certain space. If you consider this vector as an "object," then this instrument itself is a means of "observing" that object.

The essential motivation for the Dual Space is actually **objectifying the "observation" itself**.
We can consider the linearity of functions and the linearity of mappings in a completely analogous way.

### Linear Functional

In the linear space $ V $ over the field $ K $, if a mapping $ f: V \to K $ satisfies the following linear conditions:

- **Additivity:** $ f(\alpha + \beta) = f(\alpha) + f(\beta)$- **Homogeneity:** $ f(k\alpha) = kf(\alpha)$ For any $\alpha, \beta \in V $ and $ k \in K $, we call $ f $ a **linear functional** on $ V $.

What is meant by a functional is a function of a function.

- **Function:** The input is a number (or a set of numbers), and the output is a number. For example, $ f(x) = x^2 $.
    
- **Functional:** The input is a **function** (i.e., an object in some space), and the output is a number.

If we consider a "function" as a "point" (vector) in a vector space, then a "functional" is a "real-valued function" defined on that vector space.

Since we have what is called a linear functional, it naturally leads to thinking about whether we can extend the research logic of vectors to consider the linear space they form.

## Dual Space
### Definition of the Dual Space

- **Operational Definition:** Linear functionals can undergo addition and scalar multiplication:
    
    - $(f+g)(\alpha) := f(\alpha) + g(\alpha)$-$(kf)(\alpha) := kf(\alpha)$- **Space Composition:** All linear functionals defined on $ V $ form a new $ K $-linear space under the above operations, which is called the **dual space** of $ V $, denoted by $ V^*$ or $\text{Hom}(V, K)$.
    

#### Typical Examples

- **Matrix Space:** For the space of $ n $-order matrices $ M_n(K)$, the trace function is a classic linear functional: $\text{tr}: A \mapsto \text{tr}(A)$. Furthermore, the mapping that extracts matrix components $ t_{ij}: A \mapsto a_{ij}$ is also a linear functional.
    
- **Function Space:** For the space of continuous functions $ C[a, b]$, the integration operation provides a linear functional: $ f \mapsto \int_a^b f(x)g(x)dx $ (where $ g(x)$ is a fixed function).
    

Once we have a space, we naturally think about how to represent the entire space. Just like in vector spaces, we consider choosing a basis. How should we choose it? We should fully utilize linearity, while also ideally making the basis simple, like an identity matrix.

### Construction of the Dual Basis

The ideal situation is that, given a basis $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n\}$, any linear functional $ f $ can be uniquely expressed as a linear combination of the dual basis $\{\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*\}$:

$$
f = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \alpha_i^*
$$

If we apply $ f $ to a $\alpha=\sum c_i\alpha_i $, then using linearity:

$$
f(\alpha)=\sum c_if(\alpha_i)
$$

Ultimately, we only need $ f(\alpha_i)$, and these $ n $ values, combined with the coefficients of the original space, can yield the result of $ f $. In other words, the value of a linear functional is uniquely determined by its values on the basis vectors. But these are numbers; what we need are elements of the dual space—linear functionals. So the question is, how do we abstract the process of extracting $ c_i $ so that our $ f $ can be written generally, without depending on a specific vector $\alpha $? Recall vector spaces: if there is an orthonormal basis, we can get the corresponding coefficients by taking the inner product, but the dual space is not that complicated; we can just define such a functional: 

For a finite-dimensional linear space $ V $, after selecting a basis $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n\}$, a set of **dual basis** $\{\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*\}$ is correspondingly generated in $ V^*$.
    
- **Dual Basis Definition:** A dual basis satisfies the following Kronecker delta property:

$$
\alpha_i^*(\alpha_j) = \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1, & i=j \\ 0, & i \neq j \end{cases}
$$

This means the action of $\alpha_i^*$ acts like a "selector"; when the input is the $ j $-th basis vector, it outputs 1 only at $ i=j $, and 0 otherwise.

Selecting a basis automatically generates these $ n $ dual bases. So we might seemingly be redundant in writing:

$$
\alpha = \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j^*(\alpha) \alpha_j
$$

Here, the dual basis is equivalent to extracting coefficients, and this process can be generalized. We can discard the coefficients corresponding to $\alpha $ and write the expression for $ f $:

$$
f = \sum_{j=1}^n f(\alpha_j) \alpha_j^*
$$

#### Independence
The dual basis $\{\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*\}$ is not only a set of generators for $ V^*$, but it is also linearly independent:

- If $\sum k_i \alpha_i^* = 0 $, applying this equation to any basis vector $\alpha_i $:

$$
\left(\sum k_j \alpha_j^*\right)(\alpha_i) = \sum k_j \delta_{ji} = k_i = 0
$$

- Therefore, all coefficients $ k_i $ must be 0, which proves that they form a basis for $ V^*$. This also directly implies: **If $ V $ is $ n $ dimensional, then $ V^*$ is also $ n $ dimensional**.

#### The Subtlety of Isomorphism

- **Finite-Dimensional Case:** Due to $\dim(V) = \dim(V^*) = n $, the space $ V $ is isomorphic to $ V^*$ ($ V \cong V^*$).
    
- **Not Natural:** Although they are isomorphic, this isomorphism depends on the **basis** you choose. Once you change the basis, the original mapping relationship changes. Therefore, this isomorphism is not "natural" (i.e., there is no universal isomorphism map independent of the basis).
    
- **Infinite-Dimensional Case:** For infinite-dimensional spaces, $ V $ and $ V^*$ are usually **not isomorphic** (the dimension of the dual space is often larger than the original space).

Since we have coordinates, why do we need to go through $ V^*$?

**The motivation is: what we want to study are "transformations," not "positions."**

When we perform a linear transformation $ A $ in the original space $ V $ (such as rotation or stretching), the vector positions in the original space change. But this transformation also changes our "standard" for measuring things. When you change from basis $\{\alpha\}$ to basis $\{\beta\}$, the coordinates change. To preserve the physical meaning (for example, the total energy I measure cannot change just because I changed coordinate systems), my measuring tool $\alpha^*$ must be adjusted in a "inverse" compensation way (i.e., by the inverse or transpose matrix).

### Transformation of the Dual Basis

In a finite-dimensional linear space $ V $, how do the dual bases in the dual space $ V^*$ change when we change the basis of the original space?

**Theorem (Law of Change of Dual Basis)**

Let the transition matrix from a basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ to another basis $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ in a finite-dimensional linear space $ V $ be $ U $, that is:

$$
(\beta_1 \ \dots \ \beta_n) = (\alpha_1 \ \dots \ \alpha_n) U
$$

Then the dual bases $\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*$ and $\beta_1^*, \dots, \beta_n^*$ of these two sets of bases in $ V^*$ have the following contravariant relationship:

$$
(\beta_1^* \ \dots \ \beta_n^*) = (\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) (U^T)^{-1}
$$

#### Intuitive Analysis

To maintain this "selector function of precisely outputting 1 or 0 when inputting a specific basis vector," when the basis vectors of the original space undergo a linear combination through $ U $ (becoming denser or sparser), the functionals in the dual space must undergo a **compensatory evolution in the opposite direction**. This geometric "contravariant" property manifests algebraically as the inverse of the transpose.

#### Formal Proof

We will use the idea of applying it twice.

Let the transition matrix from $\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*$ to $\beta_1^*, \dots, \beta_n^*$ be $ B $, that is:

$$
(\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) = (\beta_1^* \ \dots \ \beta_n^*) B
$$

Our goal is to find the relationship between $ B $ and $ U $. Let $ B = [b_{ij}]$, $ U = [u_{ij}]$. According to the expansion of matrix multiplication:

1. Representation of the functional: $\alpha_j^* = \sum_{k=1}^n b_{kj} \beta_k^*$
    
2. Representation of the vector: $\beta_i = \sum_{m=1}^n u_{mi} \alpha_m $ Now, let the functional $\alpha_j^*$ act on the vector $\beta_i $ to get $ u_{ji}$. This is obvious using the properties of the dual basis, so let's see if this yields the property of $ u $:

- **Perspective One (Using the dual property of the $\beta $ set):**

$$
\alpha_j^*(\beta_i) = \left( \sum_{k=1}^n b_{kj} \beta_k^* \right) (\beta_i) = \sum_{k=1}^n b_{kj} \beta_k^*(\beta_i)
$$

Because $\beta_k^*(\beta_i) = \delta_{ki}$, only the term $ k=i $ survives in the above equation:

$$
\alpha_j^*(\beta_i) = b_{ij}
$$

- **Perspective Two (Using the dual property of the $\alpha $ set):**

$$
\alpha_j^*(\beta_i) = \alpha_j^* \left( \sum_{m=1}^n u_{mi} \alpha_m \right) = \sum_{m=1}^n u_{mi} \alpha_j^*(\alpha_m)
$$

Because $\alpha_j^*(\alpha_m) = \delta_{jm}$, only the term $ m=j $ survives in the above equation:

$$
\alpha_j^*(\beta_i) = u_{ji}
$$

Combining the results from the two perspectives, we get $ b_{ij} = u_{ji}$, and therefore $ B = U^T $.

Substitute this back into the original assumption equation:

$$
(\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) = (\beta_1^* \ \dots \ \beta_n^*) U^T
$$

Multiply both sides by $(U^T)^{-1}$ from the right, which gives:

$$
\mathbf{(\beta_1^* \ \dots \ \beta_n^*) = (\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) (U^T)^{-1}}
$$

Proof closed.

### Can the dual of the operator be preserved as is?

After establishing the concept of basis transformation, we can consider a very classical mathematical question:

> **Thought Question**: Let $ V $ be a linear space of dimension $ n $, and $ V^*$ be its dual space. Given a linear isomorphism $ A $ from $ V $ to $ V^*$, can we find a basis $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n $ for $ V $ such that the action of this operator on the basis vectors is exactly equal to the corresponding dual basis? That is:
> 
>

$$
A(\beta_1) = \beta_1^*, \ \dots \ , A(\beta_n) = \beta_n^*
$$

First, consider something simpler:
**Definition** of two linear mappings $\mathbb{A}, \mathbb{B}: V \to V^*$:

1. **Definition of Mapping $\mathbb{A}$**: It forces a binding between the old basis and the old dual basis.
    
    When $\mathbb{A}$ acts on the row vector $(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n)$, it maps each $\alpha_i $ to its corresponding $\alpha_i^*$:

$$
\mathbb{A}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) I_n = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*)
$$

2. **Definition of Mapping $\mathbb{B}$**: It forces a binding between the new basis and the new dual basis.
    
    When $\mathbb{B}$ acts on the new basis row vector, similarly:

$$
\mathbb{B}(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\beta_1^* \dots \beta_n^*) I_n = (\beta_1^* \dots \beta_n^*)
$$

If the original space basis has the following transformation:

$$
(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) U
$$

The contravariant law for the dual basis:

$$
(\beta_1^* \dots \beta_n^*) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) (U^T)^{-1}
$$

Now, substitute the **definition of Mapping $\mathbb{B}$** into the left side of the dual basis transformation formula:

$$
\mathbb{B}(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) (U^T)^{-1}
$$

Next, using the basis transformation $\beta = \alpha U $, replace $\beta $ inside the left parenthesis:

$$
\mathbb{B}\big( (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) U \big) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) (U^T)^{-1}
$$

Because $\mathbb{B}$ is a **linear mapping**, when it acts on a linear combination of vectors, the matrix $ U $ can be brought out directly according to linearity (Note: since the basis is a row vector, the matrix $ U $ is on the right, and even after being brought out, it remains on the right):

$$
\mathbb{B}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) \cdot U = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) (U^T)^{-1}
$$

Finally, to isolate $\mathbb{B}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n)$, we **right-multiply** both sides of the equation by $ U^{-1}$:

$$
\mathbb{B}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) (U^T)^{-1} U^{-1}
$$

Using the property of matrix inversion $(U^T)^{-1} U^{-1} = (U U^T)^{-1}$, we finally get:

$$
\mathbf{\mathbb{B}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) (U U^T)^{-1}}
$$

If we substitute the definition $\mathbb{A}(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*)$ of the old mapping $\mathbb{A}$ into the final result, we get:

$$
\mathbb{B}(\alpha) = \mathbb{A}(\alpha) (U U^T)^{-1}
$$

What does this imply?

1. **When the transition matrix $ U $ is an orthogonal matrix** (i.e., $ U U^T = I $), then $(U U^T)^{-1} = I $, and thus $\mathbb{B} = \mathbb{A}$. This means that if you perform a **rigid rotation (orthogonal transformation)** in the original space, this isomorphism mapping of "seamlessly emitting the basis to the dual basis" can be preserved unchanged after rotation!
    
2. **When the transition matrix $ U $ is not an orthogonal matrix**, there will be a measure correction term $(U U^T)^{-1}$ between the new and old mappings. This correction term is essentially the **inverse of the Metric Tensor of the new basis**.

So, given a linear isomorphism $ A: V \to V^*$ that is **fixed and cannot move**.

We want to find a basis $\beta = (\beta_1 \dots \beta_n)$ such that:

$$
A(\beta_1 \dots \beta_n) = (\beta_1^* \dots \beta_n^*)
$$

We arbitrarily borrow a set of known old bases $\alpha = (\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n)$ and their corresponding dual bases $\alpha^* = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*)$.

Since $ A $ is a known operator, it must have a fixed matrix representation in the old basis $\alpha $, let's denote it as $ M $ (which is an invertible matrix of $ n \times n $):

$$
A(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1^* \dots \alpha_n^*) M
$$

Now, the problem of finding the basis $\beta $ is essentially **finding a transition matrix $ U $** (such that $\beta = \alpha U $), which makes the new basis satisfy the requirements of the problem. Substitute $\beta = \alpha U $ into the equation:

$$
A(\alpha U) = (\beta_1^* \dots \beta_n^*)
$$

- **Expand the left side (using the linearity of $ A $ and the known matrix $ M $):**

$$
A(\alpha U) = A(\alpha) U = (\alpha^* M) U = \alpha^* (MU)
$$

- **Expand the right side (using the contravariant rule for the dual basis):**

$$
(\beta_1^* \dots \beta_n^*) = \alpha^* (U^T)^{-1}
$$

**Force the two sides to meet (extracting the consensus):**

For the left and right sides to be completely equal, since $\alpha^*$ is a set of bases for the dual space (linearly independent), the coefficient matrices in front of them must be completely equal:

$$
MU = (U^T)^{-1}
$$

Left-multiply both sides by $ U^T $:

$$
\mathbf{U^T M U = I_n}
$$

The problem is now transformed into: **Given an invertible matrix $ M $, can we find an invertible matrix $ U $ such that $ U^T M U = I_n $?**

In matrix algebra, the operation $ U^T M U $ is called **Congruence Transformation**.

We evaluate the type of the matrix $ M $ in cases:

#### Case one: If $ M $ is not a symmetric matrix ($ M \neq M^T $)

- **Risk/Fact**: If a matrix can be transformed into the identity matrix $ I_n $ via $ U^T M U $, then transpose both sides:

$$
(U^T M U)^T = I_n^T \implies U^T M^T U = I_n
$$

This implies $ U^T M U = U^T M^T U $, because $ U $ is invertible, eliminating both sides must inevitably lead to **$ M = M^T $**.
    
- **Conclusion**: If the given isomorphism $ A $ has a matrix $ M $ that is **non-symmetric** in some basis, then no matter how you change the basis, $ U^T M U = I_n $ can never hold. That is, **in this case, such a basis $\beta $ absolutely cannot be found**.
    

#### Case two: If $ M $ is a symmetric matrix ($ M = M^T $)

- **Fact**: According to the theory of congruence diagonalization of quadratic forms, a symmetric matrix $ M $ is congruent to $ I_n $ if and only if **$ M $ is a positive definite matrix** (in the complex domain, only an invertible symmetric matrix is needed; here we assume discussion in the real domain).
    
- **Conclusion**: Only when $ M $ is a positive definite symmetric matrix can we find this transition matrix $ U $ through Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization or eigenvalue decomposition.
    
The complete answer to this thought problem is:

**It depends.** Whether such a basis can be found entirely depends on the geometric properties of this linear isomorphism $ A $.

1. If the bilinear map $\Phi(u, v) = [A(u)](v)$ induced by $ A $ is a **positive definite inner product** (i.e., satisfies symmetry and positive definiteness), then we **must** be able to find such a basis $\beta $ (this basis is actually the standard orthonormal basis under this inner product).
    
2. If $ A $ does not even satisfy symmetry (e.g., $ A(u)(v) \neq A(v)(u)$), then in a finite-dimensional space, **even if you exhaust all combinations of bases, you absolutely cannot make the action of the operator on the basis vectors exactly equal to the dual basis.**

This problem profoundly reveals the lack of a "natural isomorphism." Although $ V $ and $ V^*$ have the same dimension and are necessarily isomorphic, this isomorphism $ A $ depends on the choice of basis. The problem above is essentially searching for an "invariant basis," one that achieves perfect mirror symmetry between the geometric operator and the algebraic dual.

### The Shadow of the Operator: Dual Transformation

When we do not manipulate the basis in the space itself, but rather when the vectors inside the space undergo a linear transformation, what happens to the functionals in the dual space? This is the origin of the **Dual Transformation** (or transpose transformation).

#### Motivation and Definition

Let $ A $ be a linear transformation on $ V $ ($ A \in \text{Hom}(V)$). For any linear functional $ f \in V^*$, since $ A $ transforms the vector $\alpha $ into $ A\alpha $, we can naturally define a new functional that first applies $ A $ to the vector, and then applies $ f $.

This constitutes the composition of functions: $ f \circ A $. Clearly, this new function is also linear, so $ f \circ A \in V^*$.

We define a mapping $ A^*$ that takes a functional $ f $ and outputs a new functional $ f \circ A $:

$$
A^*: V^* \to V^*, \quad f \mapsto f \circ A
$$

In other words:

$$
\mathbf{A^*(f)(\alpha) = f(A\alpha), \quad \forall \alpha \in V}
$$

This $ A^*$ is called the **Dual Transformation** of $ A $.

#### Matrix Representation of the Dual Transformation

In linear algebra, what we care about most is: if the operator $ A $ has the matrix $ M $ in a certain basis, what is the matrix of $ A^*$ in the corresponding dual basis?

**Theorem**

Let $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ be a basis for $ V $, and $\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*$ be its dual basis. $ A $ is a linear transformation on $ V $.

If the matrix representation of $ A $ in this basis is:

$$
A(\alpha_1 \ \dots \ \alpha_n) = (\alpha_1 \ \dots \ \alpha_n) A_M
$$

Then the matrix representation of the dual transformation $ A^*$ in the dual basis is its **transpose matrix**:

$$
\mathbf{A^*(\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) = (\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) A_M^T}
$$

#### Formal Proof

Let the transformation matrix of $ A^*$ in the dual basis be $ B $, i.e.:

$$
A^*(\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) = (\alpha_1^* \ \dots \ \alpha_n^*) B
$$

Let $ A_M = [a_{ij}]$ and $ B = [b_{ij}]$. By definition:

1. Expansion of the original space transformation: $ A\alpha_j = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{kj} \alpha_k $
    
2. Expansion of the dual space transformation: $ A^*\alpha_i^* = \sum_{m=1}^n b_{mi} \alpha_m^*$ To find the intrinsic relationship between $ a_{ij}$ and $ b_{ij}$, we examine the action of the functional $ A^*\alpha_i^*$ on the basis vector $\alpha_j $, and perform another dual-perspective deduction:

- **Perspective One (Direct expansion of the dual operator's matrix):**

$$
(A^*\alpha_i^*)(\alpha_j) = \left( \sum_{m=1}^n b_{mi} \alpha_m^* \right) (\alpha_j) = \sum_{m=1}^n b_{mi} \alpha_m^*(\alpha_j)
$$

Due to $\alpha_m^*(\alpha_j) = \delta_{mj}$, only the $ m=j $ term remains:

$$
(A^*\alpha_i^*)(\alpha_j) = b_{ji}
$$

- **Perspective Two (Returning to the original definition of the dual operator):**
    
According to the definition of the dual transformation, $(A^* \alpha_i^*)(\alpha_j) = \alpha_i^*(A\alpha_j)$. We substitute the expansion of $ A\alpha_j $:

$$
\alpha_i^*(A\alpha_j) = \alpha_i^* \left( \sum_{k=1}^n a_{kj} \alpha_k \right) = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{kj} \alpha_i^*(\alpha_k)
$$

Due to $\alpha_i^*(\alpha_k) = \delta_{ik}$, only the $ k=i $ term remains:

$$
\alpha_i^*(A\alpha_j) = a_{ij}
$$

**Consensus Extraction and Conclusion:**

Comparing the results from two perspectives, within the closed loop of mathematics, we must have:

$$
b_{ji} = a_{ij}
$$

This shows that the element in the $ j $ row and $ i $ column of matrix $ B $ is equal to the element in the $ i $ row and $ j $ column of matrix $ A_M $.

From this, it is concluded that, **$ B = A_M^T $**.

This means that, **the matrix of the dual operator in the dual basis is exactly the transpose of the matrix of the original operator in the original basis.** 

### The Battlefield Where Operators Die: The Annihilator Subspace

Before introducing geometric duality, we need a tool to establish a correspondence between subspaces. If some vectors in the original space are 'annihilated,' then who performs this killing in the dual space?

**Definition (Annihilator)**

Let $ V $ be a linear space of dimension $ n $, and let $ W $ be a subspace of $ V $ with dimension $ r $. The **annihilator subspace** of $ W $ in $ V^*$ (written as $ W^\perp $ in the figure) is defined as:

$$
W^\perp = \{ \beta^* \in V^* \mid \beta^*(\alpha) = 0, \ \forall \alpha \in W \}
$$

Then $ W^\perp $ is a **$ n-r $ dimensional subspace** of the dual space $ V^*$.

#### Structural Analysis

Why is the dimension exactly $ n-r $? It is obvious from the perspective of **extending a basis**:

We arbitrarily select a set of basis vectors $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r $ in $ W $, and extend it to a basis for the entire $ V $:

$$
V \text{ basis}: \quad \underbrace{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r}_{W \text{ basis}}, \alpha_{r+1}, \dots, \alpha_n
$$

Correspondingly, this set of basis vectors automatically generates a dual basis in the dual space $ V^*$:

$$
V^* \text{ the dual basis}: \quad \alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_r^*, \alpha_{r+1}^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*
$$

Now, what kind of functional $ f = \sum_{i=1}^n k_i \alpha_i^*$ can kill all the vectors in $ W $? Because the vectors in $ W $ are all linearly expressed by $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r $, according to the selector property of the dual basis $\alpha_i^*(\alpha_j) = \delta_{ij}$, the value of this functional on the first $ r $ basis vectors must be fixed at 0.

This means:

$$
k_1 = k_2 = \dots = k_r = 0
$$

Therefore, all such functionals can only be freely combined from the last $ n-r $ dual basis vectors:

$$
W^\perp = \text{span}\{\alpha_{r+1}^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*\}
$$

The dimension theorem $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = \dim V $ naturally closes the loop.

### Mirror Reversal of Subspaces: The Dual Principle

The annihilator is not just a subspace; it forms a **one-to-one correspondence**. It maps the set of subspaces of $ V $ to the set of subspaces of $ V^*$. Most fascinatingly, this mapping **reverses all the inclusion relationships** in the space.

#### 1. Core Theorem (Mirror Reversal of Inclusion Relations)

Let $ W, W_1, W_2 $ be a subspace of $ V $, then the following dual properties hold:

1. **Biconditional**:$(W^\perp)^\perp = W $ (in the sense of natural isomorphism)
    
2. **Inclusion Reversal**:If $ W_1 \subseteq W_2 $, then $ W_1^\perp \supseteq W_2^\perp $
    
3. **Intersection Union**:$(W_1 \cap W_2)^\perp = W_1^\perp + W_2^\perp $
    
4. **Union Intersection**:$(W_1 + W_2)^\perp = W_1^\perp \cap W_2^\perp $
    

#### 2. Multi-Perspective Deduction: Why does 'intersection' become 'union'?

We attempt to critically evaluate property 4 $(W_1 + W_2)^\perp = W_1^\perp \cap W_2^\perp $ from the first principles of the logical chain:

- **Perspective One (Intuitive Interception):** What capability must a functional have to turn all vectors in $ W_1 + W_2 $ (which are of the form $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 $) into 0? It must be able to **both** kill the entire $ W_1 $ (belonging to $ W_1^\perp $), **and** kill the entire $ W_2 $ (belonging to $ W_2^\perp $). Both tasks must be satisfied simultaneously, which naturally translates algebraically to the 'intersection' $\cap $.
    
- **Perspective Two (Dimension Check):** According to the left side: $\dim(W_1 + W_2)^\perp = n - \dim(W_1 + W_2)$.
    
    According to the right side: Using the subspace dimension formula, $\dim(W_1^\perp \cap W_2^\perp) = \dim W_1^\perp + \dim W_2^\perp - \dim(W_1^\perp + W_2^\perp)$.
    
    Substituting the annihilator dimension rule, the algebraic accounting on both sides perfectly matches.
    

### Natural Isomorphism: Escaping the Shackles of Basis (Second Dual Space $ V^{}$)

In our previous discussions, we repeatedly emphasized: the **$ V $ and $ V^*$ of finite dimensions are isomorphic, but their isomorphism heavily depends on the choice of basis.** Without a basis, you have no idea who to send a vector to.

However, if we take the dual of the dual space again, a magical thing happens—there exists a **'naturally natural' linear isomorphism** from $ V $ to the second dual space $ V^{}$ that **does not require any basis**.

#### 1. Motivation Analysis: Disguising a Vector as an 'Operator on Operators'

Originally, the functional $ f $ is an operator that consumes a vector $\alpha $ and outputs a number: $ f(\alpha)$.

Now let's change perspective: **Why can't we view the vector $\alpha $ as an operator that consumes the functional $ f $, also outputting a number $ f(\alpha)$?**

This is the subtle disguise of the second dual.

#### 2. Canonical Construction

For any $\alpha \in V $, we define a function $\alpha^{}$ acting on the functional (i.e., $\sigma(\alpha)$):

$$
\sigma: V \to V^{**}, \quad \alpha \mapsto \alpha^{**}
$$

Where, $\alpha^{}$, as a function taking a functional as input, is defined as:

$$
\mathbf{\alpha^{**}(f) = f(\alpha), \quad \forall f \in V^*}
$$

#### 3. Canonical Proof: Why is $\sigma $ a natural linear isomorphism?

We must critically evaluate this mapping in three steps to close the logical loop:

- **Step One: Proving that $\alpha^{**}$ is indeed a linear functional on $ V^*$ (i.e., $\alpha^{} \in V^{}$)**
    
    Take any $ f, g \in V^*, c \in \mathbb{R}$, using the definition of functional addition and scalar multiplication:

$$
\alpha^{**}(cf + g) = (cf + g)(\alpha) = c f(\alpha) + g(\alpha) = c \alpha^{**}(f) + \alpha^{**}(g)
$$

Verification passed.
    
- **Step 2: Proving that $\sigma $ itself is a linear map**
    
    We need to check the action of $\sigma(c\alpha + \beta)$ on an arbitrary functional $ f $:

$$
(c\alpha + \beta)^{**}(f) = f(c\alpha + \beta) = c f(\alpha) + f(\beta) = c \alpha^{**}(f) + \beta^{**}(f)
$$

This shows that $\sigma(c\alpha + \beta) = c\sigma(\alpha) + \sigma(\beta)$. The map is linear.
    
- **Step 3: Proving that $\sigma $ is injective (in finite dimensions, injective is equivalent to an isomorphism)**
    
    We only need to check its kernel $\text{Ker}(\sigma) = \{0\}$. Assume there exists a vector $\alpha $ such that $\sigma(\alpha) = 0 $ (i.e., the zero functional).
    
    This means that for **all** functionals $ f \in V^*$, we have $\alpha^{}(f) = f(\alpha) = 0 $.
    
    If $\alpha \neq 0 $, by the existence theorem for dual bases, we must be able to construct a dual basis functional $\alpha_1^*$ such that $\alpha_1^*(\alpha) = 1 \neq 0 $, which leads to a contradiction.
    
    Therefore, the only possibility is $\alpha = 0 $.
    
    __Conclusion: $\sigma $ is injective. In finite dimensional spaces, because $\dim V = \dim V^* = \dim V^{**}$, injectivity automatically implies surjectivity, and an isomorphism holds._*
    

### The Collapse in Infinite Dimensions: When the Mirror Cracks

All beautiful fairy tales come to an abrupt end in infinite-dimensional space. In the **infinite-dimensional polynomial space**, the isomorphism relationship between the original space and its dual space completely collapses.

#### 1. Counterexample Scenario Coupling: Polynomial space over the field of rational numbers

Let $ V = \mathbb{Q}[x]$ be the linear space formed by all polynomials with rational coefficients. Its standard basis is countable:

$$
\text{basis}: \{1, x, x^2, \dots, x^n, \dots\}
$$

This means that any element (polynomial) in $ V $ can only be a linear combination of a **finite number** of terms.

$$
V \cong \{ (a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots) \mid a_i \in \mathbb{Q}, \text{only a finite number of } a_i \neq 0 \}
$$

#### 2. The Dual Space Evolves into 'Formal Power Series'

Now let's look at the linear functional $ b^* \in V^*$ on $ V $. For a functional to be uniquely determined by its values on the basis, it must assign an output number $ b_i $ for every $ x^i $.

Since the basis is infinite, this functional can assign infinitely many numbers $(b_0, b_1, b_2, \dots)$ without constraint. When acting on a vector, because the vector has only a finite number of non-zero terms, the sum will never result in infinity:

$$
b^*(\alpha) = a_0b_0 + a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + \dots \quad (\text{is absolutely a finite sum})
$$

This means that the dual space $ V^*$ accommodates **all Formal Power Series**:

$$
V^* \cong \mathbb{Q}[[x]] = \{ (b_0, b_1, b_2, \dots) \mid b_i \in \mathbb{Q} \quad \text{can have infinitely many non-zero terms} \}
$$

#### 3. The Decisive Macro Conclusion (Confidence: High)

According to set theory (Cantor's Theorem and Cardinality Theory):

- The number of basis elements for the original space $ V = \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is **countably infinite** ($\aleph_0 $).
    
- The basis for the dual space $ V^*$, due to allowing arbitrary combinations of infinite sequences, has a cardinality that becomes **uncountably infinite** (the cardinality of the continuum).
    

**Final Critical Conclusion**: Because the cardinalities (dimensions) of the two spaces are fundamentally separated by an insurmountable chasm, in infinite-dimensional spaces, **$ V \not\cong V^*$**. This is also the fundamental reason why in functional analysis, we must additionally introduce topology and continuity (the topological dual space) to forcibly recover the dual property.
