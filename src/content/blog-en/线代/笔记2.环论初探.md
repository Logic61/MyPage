## Formal definition:

Let $ R $ be a non-empty set, on which two binary operations are defined: **addition** (denoted as $+$) and **multiplication** (denoted as $\cdot $). If the following three conditions are met, then $(R, +, \cdot)$ is called a ring:

### I. Addition forms a commutative group (Abelian group)

1. **Associativity**: $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$
    
2. **Commutativity**: $ a + b = b + a $ 3. **Identity element (Zero element)**: There exists $ 0 \in R $, such that for any $ a \in R $, $ 0 + a = a $.
    
4. **Inverse element (Negative element)**: For any $ a \in R $, there exists $-a \in R $, such that $ a + (-a) = 0 $.
    

### II. Multiplication forms a semigroup

1. **Associativity**: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$_Note: The definition of a basic ring does not mandate that multiplication must be commutative, nor does it mandate the existence of a multiplicative identity element (identity element $ 1 $)._
    

### III. Distributive Law

Multiplication satisfies the left and right distributive laws over addition:

1. $ a \cdot (b + c) = (a \cdot b) + (a \cdot c)$ 2.$(a + b) \cdot c = (a \cdot c) + (b \cdot c)$

It is not difficult to verify the above properties for integers and polynomials. However, we find that they have some more beautiful properties, such as the commutativity of multiplication, for which such a ring is called a **commutative ring**. Although it is not clear why, we see that addition has an **identity element**, but multiplication is not required to have one, yet both integers and polynomials have an identity element; such a ring is called a **unital ring**, because the identity element can also be called a **unit element**.

In the rings of integers and polynomials, we also have the **cancellation law**. If $ ab=ac $ and both are non-zero, then $ b=c $. But this is not always true, like with matrices, two non-zero matrices can multiply to zero. We define a **zero divisor**: if there exists a non-zero $ b $ such that $ ab=0 $, then $ a $ is called a **left zero divisor**, and similarly, there is a **right zero divisor**. In the **commutative rings** we usually discuss, left and right zero divisors are the same, and are collectively called **zero divisors**. According to the standard definition, the **zero element $ 0 $ itself is usually not discussed as a zero divisor** (or called the 'trivial zero divisor' in some systems). If there are no zero divisors, then we can safely use the cancellation law.

If a ring is simultaneously a commutative ring, a unital ring, and has no non-trivial zero divisors, we call it an **Integral Domain**, perhaps because they possess properties similar to integers.

What about the corresponding prime numbers? We define a **unit** as an element with an inverse, which is also called a **unit**.

**Irreducible (Irreducible element):** 

A non-zero, non-Unit element $ p $ is called an **irreducible element** if it satisfies:

**If $ p = ab $, then at least one of $ a $ and $ b $ must be a Unit.**

Look at the example of the **polynomial ring $ F[x]$**:

- **$ 5 $ (non-zero constant)**: is a **Unit** (because there is an inverse in the field $ 1/5 $).
    
- **$ x + 1 $** : is an **irreducible element**.
    
- **$ x^2 - 1 $** : is a **reducible element**, because it can be factored into $(x+1)(x-1)$, and neither of these factors is a Unit.

To deal with the interference caused by Units, we introduce the concept of "associated": If $ a = u \cdot b $($ u $ is a Unit), we call $ a $ and $ b $are **associated**. In the case of irreducible decomposition, we consider associated elements to be "the same kind of building block." For example, in $\mathbb{Z}$ ...,$ 2 $ and $-2 $ is one thing; in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ ...,$ x+1 $ and $ 2x+2 $ is also one thing.

Furthermore, we also have **prime elements**.

In an integral domain $ R $ an element that is non-zero and not a Unit $ p $ is called a **prime element**, if it satisfies the following condition: **If $ p $ divides the product of two elements $ ab $(that is, $ p \mid ab $), then it must have $ p \mid a $ or $ p \mid b $**

This is very similar to irreducible elements, what is the difference?

- **Irreducible elements** focus on **'whether it can be factored'** (the perspective of an atom).
    
- **Prime elements** focus on **'whether it can divide'** (the perspective of propagation).

- **In any integral domain: Prime elements $\implies $ are irreducible elements.**
    
    - _Proof idea_: If $ p=ab $ is a prime element, then by definition $ p \mid ab \implies p \mid a $ or $ p \mid b $. Assume $ p \mid a $. Since $ a $ is also a factor of $ p $ ($ a \mid p $), you will find that $ a $ and $ p $ can only be associates, which means $ b $ must be a Unit. Therefore, $ p $ is irreducible.

## Field

### A. Additive Group Structure

$(F, +)$ is an **Abelian group**. That is to say:

- The additive associative law and the commutative law hold.
    
- There exists an identity element $ 0 $.
    
- Every element $ a $ has an inverse element $-a $.
    

### B. Multiplicative Group Structure (Key Difference!)

The set $ F = F \setminus {0}$ after removing the zero element forms an Abelian group under multiplication.

This means:

- The multiplicative associative law and the commutative law hold.
    
- There exists an identity element $ 1 $ ($ 1 \neq 0 $).
    
- **Core:** Every non-zero element $ a $ has an inverse $ a^{-1}$, such that $ a \cdot a^{-1} = 1 $.
    

### C. Distributive Law

Multiplication satisfies the distributive law over addition: $ a(b+c) = ab + ac $.

> **Concise Definition**: A field is a **commutative ring where every non-zero element is invertible**. Or, a field is a **non-trivial integral domain where every non-zero element has an inverse**.

As can be seen, a field is very similar to the real numbers. For the rational numbers in between, we define the field of fractions.

To turn the integral domain $ D $ into a field $ F $, essentially it is to forcibly define an inverse $ a^{-1}$ for every non-zero element $ a \in D $.
## Field of Fractions

### Step 2: Define the Equivalence Relation

Why are $\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{2}{4}$ the same number? Because $ 1 \times 4 = 2 \times 2 $.

## Ring Extension

Let $ R $ be a ring. If there exists another ring $ S $ that satisfies the following two conditions, we call **$ S $ an extension ring of $ R $** (or that $ R $ is a **subring** of $ S $):

1. **Inclusion Relation**: $ R $ is a subset of $ S $ (i.e., $ R \subseteq S $).
    
2. **Operation Consistency**: The addition and multiplication operations in $ R $ must be completely consistent with the addition and multiplication operations in $ S $.
    
3. **Identity Element Consistency** (Usually Required): If $ R $ has an identity element $ 1_R $, then the identity element $ 1_S $ of $ S $ must satisfy $ 1_S = 1_R $.
    

We usually denote this type of extension relationship as **$ S/R $**. There are three common types of ring extensions:

##### Step 2: To satisfy "additive closure"

##### Step 3: Verifying completeness

You will find that if you add two such linear combinations, the result is still of this form; if you multiply an $ r \in R $ by such a linear combination, the result (by the distributive law) is still of this form.

> This shows that the set is **self-consistent**. It is the "smallest black hole" containing $ S $.

## Quotient Ring
Let $ R $ be a ring, and $ I $ be an **ideal (Ideal)** of $ R $. We define the **quotient ring** of $ R $ by $ I $, denoted as $ R/I $, which is an algebraic structure satisfying the following conditions:

### A. Set Composition

$ R/I $, as a set, is the set of all **cosets (Coset)** of the additive subgroup $ I $ in $ R $:

$$
R/I = \{ a + I \mid a \in R \}
$$

Here $ a+I $ denotes the set $\{ a + i \mid i \in I \}$. It can be understood as: in the world of $ R/I $, all numbers differing from $ a $ by an ideal element are considered to be **the same point**.

### B. Operation Definition

To make this set also a "ring," we must define its addition and multiplication:

1. **Addition**: $(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I $
    
2. **Multiplication**: $(a + I) \cdot (b + I) = (ab) + I $> **Core Logic**: The reason these two operations are "well-defined" is entirely because $ I $ is an ideal. No matter who you choose as the representative, the resulting residue class is unique.

# Unique Factorization Domain (UFD) — Factorization
## Definition of UFD

An integral domain $ R $ is called a **Unique Factorization Domain** if it satisfies the following two conditions:

1. **Existence of Factorization**: 
    
    Every non-zero, non-unit element $ a $ can be written as a finite product of irreducible elements:

$$
a = p_1 p_2 \dots p_n
$$

2. **Uniqueness of Factorization**: 
    
    If the same element has another factorization $ a = q_1 q_2 \dots q_m $, then there must be $ n = m $, and after appropriate reordering, every $ p_i $ must be **Associate** with the corresponding $ q_i $.
    

> What is meant by "Associate" is differing by a Unit (like 1 or -1). In a UFD, $ 6 = 2 \times 3 $ and $ 6 = (-2) \times (-3)$ are considered the same type of factorization.

## Properties
### Irreducible implies Prime (Irreducible $\implies $ Prime)

In a general integral domain, "prime element $\implies $ is equivalent to irreducible element," but the converse (irreducible element $\implies $ is prime element) is usually not true. However, in a **UFD**, these two are equivalent.

#### Proof Idea

Let $ p \in R $ be an irreducible element. We want to prove: if $ p \mid ab $, then $ p \mid a $ or $ p \mid b $.

1. Assume $ p \mid ab $, then there exists $ c \in R $ such that $ ab = pc $.
    
2. Since $ R $ is a UFD, we can write $ a, b, c $ as a product of irreducible elements:
    
    - $ a = a_1 a_2 \dots a_n $-$ b = b_1 b_2 \dots b_m $-$ c = c_1 c_2 \dots c_k $
        
3. Substituting into the equation gives: $(a_1 \dots a_n)(b_1 \dots b_m) = p(c_1 \dots c_k)$.
    
4. By the **uniqueness** in a UFD, all irreducible factors on the left side must be associates of the factors on the right side.
    
5. Since $ p $ is an irreducible factor on the right side, it must be an associate of some $ a_i $ or $ b_j $ on the left side.
    
6. If $ p \sim a_i $, then $ p \mid a $; if $ p \sim b_j $, then $ p \mid b $.
    
7. Therefore, $ p $ is a prime element.
    

> **Conclusion:** In a UFD, whether an element can be factored only depends on whether it is a prime element, which eliminates the distinction between irreducible elements and prime elements.

---

### 2. Existence of Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)

In a UFD, the **GCD always exists** for any two non-zero elements $ a, b $ (unique up to associates).

#### Construction Method

By utilizing the factorization property of UFDs, we can find the GCD using the method of "taking the minimum exponent":

1. List the set of all non-associate prime elements (irreducible elements) appearing in the factorizations of $ a $ and $ b $: $\{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_k\}$.
    
2. Write $ a $ and $ b $ in a unified form:

$$
a = u \cdot p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \dots p_k^{e_k}
$$

$$
b = v \cdot p_1^{f_1} p_2^{f_2} \dots p_k^{f_k}
$$

(where $ u, v $ is a unit, $ e_i, f_i \ge 0 $)
    
3. Define $ d = p_1^{\min(e_1, f_1)} p_2^{\min(e_2, f_2)} \dots p_k^{\min(e_k, f_k)}$.
    
4. This $ d $ is the greatest common divisor of $ a $ and $ b $, denoted as $\gcd(a, b)$.
    

#### Points to Note

- **Not always expressible as a linear combination:** Although the GCD exists in a UFD, the **Bézout's identity** (i.e., there exists $ x, y $ such that $ ax + by = d $) is **not necessarily true** in a UFD.
    
- The necessary and sufficient condition for Bézout's identity to hold is that the ring is a **PID (Principal Ideal Domain)**. For example, in the UFD $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, $\gcd(2, x) = 1 $, but you cannot find a polynomial $ f(x), g(x)$ satisfying $ 2f(x) + xg(x) = 1 $.

### 3. Gauss's Lemma
If $ R $ is a Unique Factorization Domain (UFD), then its polynomial ring $ R[x]$ is also a UFD.
This requires using the Gauss's Lemma proven at the end of Note 1: the product of two primitive polynomials is also a primitive polynomial.
#### 1. Intervention of the Field of Fractions $ F $

We want to prove that $ R[x]$ is a UFD, but it is difficult to do directly in $ R[x]$.

1. Let $ F $ be the **field of fractions** of $ R $ (for example, $\mathbb{Z}$ corresponding to $\mathbb{Q}$).
    
2. We know that $ F $ is a field, and thus $ F[x]$ must be a **UFD**.
    
3. **Core Idea**: Throw the polynomial in $ R[x]$ into $ F[x]$ to decompose it, and then use Gauss's Lemma to pull it back to $ R[x]$!
    

---

#### 2. Existence of Factorization

We want to prove that any non-zero, non-unit element $ f(x)$ in $ R[x]$ can be decomposed into a product of irreducible elements.

1. **Content**: $ f(x) = c \cdot f_1(x)$, where $ c \in R $ is the greatest common divisor of the coefficients, and $ f_1(x)$ is the primitive polynomial.
    
2. **Factorization of $ c $**: Because $ R $ is a UFD, the constant $ c $ can be uniquely factored into a product of irreducible elements in $ R $.
    
3. **Factorization of $ f_1(x)$**: 
    
    - Consider $ f_1(x)$ as an element in $ F[x]$. Because $ F[x]$ is a UFD, it can be factored into irreducible elements in $ F[x]$: $ f_1(x) = q_1(x) \dots q_k(x)$.
        
    - By clearing the denominators and extracting the content, we can turn each $ q_i(x)$ into a primitive polynomial $ p_i(x)$ in $ R[x]$.
        
    - At this point, $ f_1(x) = u \cdot p_1(x) \dots p_k(x)$, where $ u \in F $.
        
    - **Key Point**: Since $ f_1 $ and all $ p_i $ are primitive, by Gauss's Lemma, their product is also primitive. This means that the coefficient $ u $ must be a **Unit** in $ R $.
        
4. **Conclusion**: Existence is proven!
    

---

#### 3. Uniqueness of Factorization

This is where the value of "prime element" is best demonstrated. In a UFD, the equivalent condition to proving uniqueness is proving: **"An irreducible element must be a prime element"**.

We need to prove that the irreducible element $ p(x)$ in $ R[x]$ is a prime element. We consider two cases:

- **Case A**: $ p(x) = p $ is an irreducible element in $ R $.
    
    Using the quotient ring method: $ R[x] / \langle p \rangle \cong (R/\langle p \rangle)[x]$. Since $ R $ is a UFD, $ p $ is a prime element in $ R $, so $ R/\langle p \rangle $ is an integral domain, and further the polynomial ring is also an integral domain. Thus, $ p $ is a prime element in $ R[x]$.
    
- **Case B**: $ p(x)$ is a primitive irreducible polynomial of degree $\ge 1 $.
    
Using the Gauss Lemma: If $ p(x) \mid g(x)h(x)$, then by the Gauss Lemma, it can be deduced that $ p(x)$ is also irreducible (i.e., a prime element) in $ F[x]$. Then, in $ F[x]$, $ p(x)$ must divide $ g(x)$ or $ h(x)$. We can use the Gauss Lemma once more to bring back $ R[x]$, proving that it also divides one of them in $ R[x]$.

# Principal Ideal Domain (PID) — Bezout's Theorem
## Definition of a PID

An integral domain $ R $ is called a **Principal Ideal Domain** if it satisfies:

**Every ideal $ I $ in $ R $ is a principal ideal.**

This means that for any ideal $ I \subseteq R $, you can always find an element $ a \in I $ such that:

$$
I = \langle a \rangle = \{ ra \mid r \in R \}
$$

> AI's mysterious analogy: Imagine that an ideal could potentially contain a messy bunch of generators, but in a PID, this motley crew can always be represented by a single "leader." This extremely simple structure makes many complex propositions easy as pie, meow!

| **Ring R** | **Is it a PID** | **Reason** |
| ------------------------ | ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Integers $\mathbb{Z}$** | **Yes** | Every ideal is of the form $ n\mathbb{Z}$. |
| **Polynomial ring over a field $ F[x]$** | **Yes** | As long as the coefficients are in the field, you can find the unique generator using polynomial long division. |
| **Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$** | **Yes** | It is an ED, and every ED is a PID. |
| **Polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$** | **No!** | The ideal $\langle 2, x \rangle $ requires two generators; you cannot find a single polynomial that generates both 2 and $ x $ |
## Properties
### Bezout's Theorem
#### Proof Steps: Constructing an Ideal

We want to prove: For any non-zero element $ a, b $ in $ R \in \text{PID}$, there exists $ u, v \in R $ such that $ ua + vb = \gcd(a, b)$.

##### Step 1: Construct an ideal $ I $ by considering the set $ I = \{ ra + sb \mid r, s \in R \}$.

- **Verifying Ideal Properties**:
    
    1. **Closed under addition**: $(r_1 a + s_1 b) + (r_2 a + s_2 b) = (r_1+r_2)a + (s_1+s_2)b $, which is still in $ I $.
        
    2. **Absorbed under multiplication**: For any $ k \in R $ and $ k(ra + sb) = (kr)a + (ks)b $, it is also in $ I $.
        
- Thus, $ I $ is an ideal of the ring $ R $, meow.
    

##### Step 2: Using the definition of a PID

Since $ R $ is a **PID**, the ideal $ I $ must be generated by a **single element**.

This means there exists some $ d \in R $ such that:

$$
I = \langle d \rangle
$$

Since $ d \in I $, by the construction definition of $ I $, it **must** be written as some linear combination of $ a $ and $ b $:

$$
d = ua + vb \quad (\text{For some } u, v \in R)
$$

##### Step 3: Proving that this $ d $ is the GCD

1. **$ d $ is a common divisor**: 
    
    Since $ a = 1 \cdot a + 0 \cdot b \in I $ and $ b = 0 \cdot a + 1 \cdot b \in I $, and $ I = \langle d \rangle $, this means that $ a $ and $ b $ are both multiples of $ d $. That is, **$ d \mid a $** and **$ d \mid b $**.
    
2. **$ d $ is the "greatest"**: 
    
    Let $ c $ be any common divisor of $ a, b $ (i.e., $ c \mid a $ and $ c \mid b $).
    
    Then $ c $ must divide any linear combination of them. Since $ d = ua + vb $, then **$ c \mid d $**.
    
    In algebra, the common divisor that is divisible by all common divisors is the **Greatest Common Divisor (GCD)**!

## Definition of an ED

An integral domain $ R $ is called a **Euclidean Domain** if a "size function" $ d: R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{N}$ (usually called the Euclidean norm) is defined on it, and it satisfies the following two hard criteria:

1. **Monotonicity**: For any non-zero $ a, b \in R $, there exists $ d(a) \le d(ab)$.
    
2. **Division Algorithm**: For any $ a $ and non-zero $ b $ in $ R $, there exist $ q, r \in R $ (quotient and remainder) such that:

$$
a = bq + r
$$

where either $ r = 0 $ or **$ d(r) < d(b)$**.
    

> The soul of an ED lies in this $ d(r) < d(b)$. It guarantees that when you continuously perform division, the "size" of the remainder will continuously decrease, and eventually must stop at zero. This is why we can play the **Euclidean Algorithm** in an ED.

👉 This is the core of the entire proof: **Pinning down the ideal using "size."**

---

##### Note 2. Introduction to Ring Theory $ I = \langle a \rangle $

We want to prove two things:

###### (1) $\langle a \rangle \subseteq I $ Clearly true, because $ a \in I $, and ideals absorb multiplication.

---

###### (2) $ I \subseteq \langle a \rangle $ Take an arbitrary $ b \in I $, using the division algorithm with remainder for ED:

$ b=aq+r $, where $ r=0 $ or $ d(r)<d(a)b = aq + r $, 

Note:

- $ b \in I $-$ aq \in I $(ideal absorption)

Thus:

$ r=b−aq∈I $

---

##### Step 3: Eliminating the remainder using "minimality"

If $ r \neq 0 $, then:

$ d(r)<d(a)$ But this contradicts $ a $ the minimality.

Therefore, the only possibility is:

$ r=0 $ That is to say:$ b=aq∈⟨a⟩$

---

##### Conclusion

$ I=⟨a⟩$

---

#### In one sentence summary

> **The strength of ED is that it allows you to perform a "Euclidean algorithm" on an ideal, eventually compressing it into a single generator.**

## Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT)

Now we move to a very beautiful structural result:

> **CRT = Decomposing a ring into several "non-interfering" parts.**

---

### Basic version (two ideals)

Let $ R $ is a commutative ring,$ I, J $ are two ideals, and satisfy:$ I+J=R $

(Called **coprime ideals**)

Then:

$ R/(I∩J)≅R/I×R/J $

---

### The intuitive version in integers

Take $ R = \mathbb{Z}$,$ I = (m)$,$ J = (n)$.

If $\gcd(m,n)=1 $, then:

$ Z/mnZ≅Z/mZ×Z/nZ $

---

### Where does the map come from?

Definition:

$φ:R→R/I×R/J $

$φ(a)=(amodI, amodJ)$

👉 It is "looking at two moduli simultaneously."

---

### Core question: Why is it an isomorphism?

We look at it in three steps:

---

#### 1. Homomorphism (obvious)

Both addition and multiplication hold component-wise.

---

#### 2. Kernel

$ kerφ={a∣a∈I \text{and} a∈J}=I∩J $

---

#### 3. Surjectivity (most crucial)

We want to prove:

> Given $(a \bmod I,\ b \bmod J)$there exists $ x \in R $ simultaneously satisfying:
> 
>$ x≡a (modI),x≡b (modJ)$

---

##### Constructing the solution (the soul step)

Because $ I + J = R $, there exists:

$ u∈I, v∈J,u+v=1 $ Construction:$ x=av+bu $

Check:

- Module $ I $:  
    $ u \in I \Rightarrow bu \equiv 0 $,  
    $ v \equiv 1 $⇒$ x \equiv a $- Module $ J $:  
    Similarly $ x \equiv b $

---

#### Conclusion

$ R/(I∩J)≅R/I×R/J $

---

## Understanding the essence of CRT

> CRT is not about "solving equations," but rather:
> 
> **When two ideals are coprime, they are "invisible" to each other.**

In other words:

- in $ R/I $ it,$ J $ has already "collapsed to 0"
- in $ R/J $ it,$ I $ and "disappeared".

So the entire structure can be split into two **independent worlds**.

---

## Relationship with decomposition (Echoing UFD)

If you are $\mathbb{Z}$ in$ n=p_1^{e1​_​}⋯p_k^{e_k}​​$ then:$ Z/nZ≅∏Z/p_i^{e_i​​}Z $

👉 This is:

> **"Prime factorization of integers"  "Direct product decomposition of quotient rings"**

In a UFD, we decompose elements into prime factors; 
In CRT, we decompose the structure into independent components.

# Summary

- **Integral Domain → UFD**
    What is added:
    👉 **Order of Decomposition (Unique Factorization)**
- **UFD → PID**  
    What is added:
    👉 **Minimal Structure of Ideals (Enough with one generator)**
- **PID → ED**  
    What is added:
    👉 **Algorithmic Nature (Division with remainder → Computable)**
- **ED → Field**  
    What is added:
    👉 **All non-zero elements are invertible (Completely eliminates the fraction problem)**

- From **Field → ED**:
    You start losing the property of "all elements being invertible," but you still retain "the ability to divide (with remainder)".
- From **ED → PID**:
    You might lose the "division algorithm," but the ideals remain very clean.
- From **PID → UFD**:
    You lose the "single-generator property of ideals," but the factorization is still unique.
- From **UFD → Integral Domain**:
    Even unique factorization is lost, and the world starts getting messy (e.g., $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$).

|Level|Control Method|
|---|---|
|Integral Domain|No zero divisors (Most basic order)|
|UFD|Controlled by "irreducible factorization"|
|PID|Controlled by "ideal generators"|
|ED|Controlled by "algorithm (division)"|
|Field|Everything is directly invertible (Complete control)|
> **The "factorization problem" and the "ideal problem" are two languages describing the same problem.**

Specific correspondence:

- Element factorization → Irreducible / Prime element
- Ideal structure → Principal ideal / Maximal ideal / Prime ideal
- Divisibility relation → Ideal inclusion relation

For example:

- $ p $ is a prime element
    ⇔ $\langle p \rangle $ is a **prime ideal**
- $ R $ is a PID
    ⇔ All ideals are "controllable" (by one generator)

What this is actually saying is:

> **The "factorization" in the world of elements becomes "structural decomposition" in the world of ideals.**
