# The Structure of a Linear Space
## Definition and Axiomatic System of a Linear Space

A linear space is a non-empty set $ V $ (whose elements are called **abstract vectors**), built over a field $ K $, and is equipped with two operations: **addition** and **scalar multiplication**.

### Core Operations and Closure

1. **Addition (Vector Addition)**:
    
    - Definition: $ V \times V \to V $
        
    - Mapping: $(\alpha, \beta) \mapsto \alpha + \beta $ - Essence: The set $ V $ is closed under the addition operation.
        
2. **Scalar Multiplication (Scalar Multiplication)**:
    
    - Definition: $ K \times V \to V $
        
    - Mapping: $(k, \alpha) \mapsto k\alpha $ - Essence: The result of combining a scalar from the field $ K $ with a vector remains in $ V $.
        

---

### The 8 Axioms

These eight axioms jointly constitute the framework of a linear space. The first four establish the status of $ V $ as a **commutative group** (Abelian group) under addition, and the last four specify the compatibility between scalar multiplication and addition.

#### 1. Addition Properties (Commutative Group)

- **(1) Commutative Law:** $\alpha + \beta = \beta + \alpha $ - **(2) Associative Law:** $\alpha + (\beta + \gamma) = (\alpha + \beta) + \gamma $ - **(3) Existence of Identity Element (Zero Element):** There exists an element $ 0 \in V $ such that $ 0 + \alpha = \alpha + 0 = \alpha, \forall \alpha \in V $ - **(4) Existence of Inverse Element (Negative Element):** For any $\alpha $, there exists $-\alpha \in V $ such that $\alpha + (-\alpha) = (-\alpha) + \alpha = 0 $
    
    > **Corollary:** The zero element and the inverse element are **unique** in a given space.
    

#### 2. Scalar Multiplication Properties

For $\forall \alpha, \beta \in V $ and $\forall k, l \in K $:

- **(5) Scalar Identity Element:** $ 1 \cdot \alpha = \alpha $ - **(6) Scalar Associative Law:** $ k(l\alpha) = (kl)\alpha $ - **(7) Left Distributive Law:** $(k + l)\alpha = k\alpha + l\alpha $ - **(8) Right Distributive Law:** $ k(\alpha + \beta) = k\alpha + k\beta $
    

---

**$ V $ is a commutative group that can be multiplied by $ K $.**

### Comparison with Rings

If we compare algebraic structures to some construction rules, linear spaces emphasize the **interaction between levels**, while rings emphasize **internal self-evolution**.

- **Ring** is a **'self-contained'** single set structure. It defines two binary operations: addition and multiplication, on a set $ R $. All operations ($ a+b $ or $ a \cdot b $) occur within $ R $. You can imagine it as a closed black box, where elements collide with each other through two rules.
    
- **Linear Space (Vector Space)** is a **'dual-set linkage'** structure. It involves two sets: a vector set $ V $ and a field $ K $ (scalars). Addition is an interaction within $ V $, but multiplication (scalar multiplication) is **cross-boundary**—acted upon by an 'external force' from the field $ K $ on elements in $ V $.

This part of the lecture shifts the focus from the 'definition' of linear spaces to their **structural measure**. If axioms are the 'constitution' of the space, then **Basis** and **Dimension** are its 'skeleton' and 'scale'.

---

Similar to the discussion of vector sets that we are familiar with, linear spaces can also define basis, dimension, etc., in a completely analogous way. In fact, we will see that linear mappings and matrices are isomorphic.

## Basis, Dimension, and Isomorphism

### Rank of a Vector Set and Maximal Linearly Independent Set

- **Maximal Linearly Independent Set:** A vector set can have many different maximal linearly independent sets, but the number of vectors they contain must be the same.
    
- **Rank:** The number of vectors contained in a maximal linearly independent set is called the rank of that vector set.
    

### Basis and Dimension

For a subset $ S $ of a linear space $ V $:

1. **Linear Expression:** If every vector in $ V $ can be expressed as a linear combination of a finite number of vectors in $ S $, then $ S $ is said to be linearly expressed by $ V $.
    
2. **Linear Independence:** If every finite subset of $ S $ is linearly independent, then $ S $ is said to be linearly independent.
    

**Definition of a Basis:**

If a subset $ S $ simultaneously satisfies:

1. $ S $ is linearly independent;
    
2. $ S $ is linearly expressed by $ V $;
    
Then $ S $ is called a **basis** for $ V $.
    

- **Dimension:**
    
    - If $ S $ is a finite set, then $ V $ is called a **finite-dimensional linear space**.
        
    - Any two bases of $ V $ contain the same number of vectors; this constant is called the dimension of $ V $, denoted by $\dim V $.
        
    - The dimension of the zero space $\{0\}$ is defined as $ 0 $.
        

---

### Lemma and Corollary

- **Lemma:** If $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s $ is linearly independent, then $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s, \beta $ is linearly dependent and $\iff \beta $ can be linearly expressed by $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s $.
    
- **Augmentation Theorem (Corollary)**: In a finite-dimensional linear space $ V $, any set of linearly independent vectors $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s $ can be augmented to form a basis for $ V $.
    

---

### Basis Determination Theorem

Let $ S $ be a subset of a finite-dimensional linear space $ V $. If any **two** of the following three conditions hold, then all three conditions hold (i.e., $ S $ forms a basis for $ V $):

1. $ S $ is linearly independent;
    
2. $ S $ can be linearly expressed by $ V $;
    
3. $|S| = \dim V $ (the number of vectors equals the dimension of the space).
    

---

### Coordinate Isomorphism $ V \cong K^n $ Once a basis $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n $ is chosen in a linear space $ V $, every element $\alpha $ in $ V $ can be uniquely represented as:

$$
\alpha = k_1\alpha_1 + k_2\alpha_2 + \dots + k_n\alpha_n
$$

- **Coordinates**: The column vector $[k_1, k_2, \dots, k_n]^T \in K^n $ is called the coordinates of $\alpha $ with respect to this basis.
    
- **Essence**: This one-to-one correspondence preserves addition and scalar multiplication. This means that, **any $ n $-dimensional linear space is algebraically identical (isomorphic) to $ K^n $**.

For operations concerning bases, changing bases, etc., please read in Mathematical Miscellany 3. Basis Idea.

# Operations on Subspaces

### Definition of a Linear Subspace

If a subset $ W $ of a linear space $ V $ satisfies the following three properties, then $ W $ is called a **subspace** of $ V $:

1. **Non-empty/Contains Zero Vector**: $ 0 \in W $ 2. **Closure under Addition**: If $\alpha, \beta \in W $, then $\alpha + \beta \in W $ 3. **Closure under Scalar Multiplication**: If $ k \in K, \beta \in W $, then $ k\beta \in W $
    

> **Essence**: $ W $ also forms a complete linear space under the original operations of $ V $, automatically satisfying all eight axioms.

---

### Properties and Construction of Subspaces

- **Theorem (Basis Extension and Dimension)**:
    
    - The basis of a subspace $ W $ can be extended to form a basis for the entire space $ V $.
        
    - **Dimension Inequality**: $\dim W \le \dim V $.
        
    - **Condition for Equality**: $\dim W = \dim V \iff W = V $.
        
- **Several Ways to Give a Subspace**:
    
    1. **Generator Method**: The span generated by a set of vectors $ W = \langle \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s \rangle $.
        
    2. **Solution Space Method**: The solution set of a homogeneous linear equation $ Ax = 0 $.
        
    3. **Operation Construction Method**: Obtained through operations such as intersection, sum, orthogonal complement (in an inner product space), etc.
        

---

### Operations on Subspaces: Intersection and Sum

#### 1. Intersection of Subspaces

- **Theorem**: If $ V_1, V_2 $ is a subspace of $ V $, then its intersection $ V_1 \cap V_2 $ is also a subspace of $ V $.
    
- **Note**: The union $ V_1 \cup V_2 $ **is generally not** a subspace, unless one contains the other (i.e., $ V_1 \supseteq V_2 $ or $ V_1 \subseteq V_2 $).
    

#### 2. Sum of Subspaces

- **Definition**: If $ V_1, V_2 $ is a subspace of $ V $, the set $\{ \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 \mid \alpha_1 \in V_1, \alpha_2 \in V_2 \}$ is called the sum of $ V_1 $ and $ V_2 $, denoted as **$ V_1 + V_2 $**.
    
- **Property**: $ V_1 + V_2 $ is also a subspace of $ V $.
    
- **Generator Representation**:
    
    If $ V_1 = \langle \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r \rangle $, $ V_2 = \langle \beta_1, \dots, \beta_s \rangle $,
    
    then $ V_1 + V_2 = \langle \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_s \rangle $.

Compared to the union, the sum better reflects the nature of a linear space. For example, the union of two lines is two lines as a whole, but the sum is spread out into a plane, carrying linear operations. Moreover, the sum can directly construct a subspace, whereas the union is not so easy.

### Dimension Formula

#### I. Theorem Content

If $ V_1, V_2 $ is a subspace of the linear space $ V $, then the dimensions of their sum space and intersection space satisfy the following relationship:

$$
\dim(V_1 + V_2) + \dim(V_1 \cap V_2) = \dim V_1 + \dim V_2
$$

> **Intuitive Understanding**: The dimension of the combined space of two spaces is equal to the sum of their individual dimensions minus the dimension of the intersection part that was counted twice. This is extremely similar to the Principle of Inclusion-Exclusion in set theory.

---

#### II. Proof Outline

The core of the proof lies in constructing a set of linearly independent vectors that span $ V_1 + V_2 $ through **basis augmentation**. In fact, the exact same proof was already done in Mathematical Miscellany 3 on the Basis Idea.

1. **Basis for the Intersection**: Let one basis for $ V_1 \cap V_2 $ be $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r $. Then $\dim(V_1 \cap V_2) = r $.
    
2. **Augmenting in Both Directions**:
    
    - Augmenting it to form a basis for $ V_1 $: $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_m $. Then $\dim V_1 = r + m $.
        
    - Augmenting it to form a basis for $ V_2 $: $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r, \gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_s $. Then $\dim V_2 = r + s $.
        
3. **Constructing the Basis for the Sum Space**: Prove that $\beta_1, \dots, \beta_m, \alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r, \gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_s $ is linearly independent and spans $ V_1 + V_2 $.
    
    - If the linear combination is zero: $ k_1\beta_1 + \dots + l_1\gamma_1 + \dots = 0 $.
        
    - This can be transformed to: The combination of $ k_i\beta_i $ belongs to $ V_2 $, and therefore must fall into the intersection $ V_1 \cap V_2 $.
        
    - By using the basis of the intersection space and combining it with linear independence, we deduce that all coefficients must be $ 0 $.
        
4. **Conclusion**: $\dim(V_1 + V_2) = m + r + s $.
    
    - Substituting into the equation: $(m + r + s) + r = (r + m) + (r + s)$, the formula holds.
        

---

### Linear Independence, Direct Sum, and Unique Representation

#### I. Linear Independence $\iff $ representation is unique

For the set of vectors $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s $:

- **Core Logic**: If the representation of the zero vector by this set of vectors is unique, then its representation of any representable vector is unique.

If the representation of the zero vector is unique, suppose there are two ways to represent $\beta $; subtracting them implies that the coefficients of the two ways must be the same, meaning there is actually only one way.

---

#### II. Direct Sum of Subspaces

When we elevate the object of discussion from "individual vectors" to "subspaces," uniqueness remains the core criterion for determining the structure.

##### 1. Definition of Representation

Let $ V_1, \dots, V_s $ be a subspace of $ V $. For an arbitrary vector $\beta $ in the sum space $ V_1 + \dots + V_s $, its representation is:

$$
\beta = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \dots + \alpha_s, \quad (\alpha_i \in V_i)
$$

The ordered set $(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s)$ is called one representation of $\beta $.

##### 2. Criterion for Direct Sum: Uniqueness of the Zero Vector

- **Theorem**: If the representation of the zero vector is unique $\iff $, then the representation of any vector is unique.
    
- **Definition of Direct Sum**: If the representation of any element in $ V_1 + \dots + V_s $ is unique, then this sum is called a **direct sum**, denoted as:

$$
V_1 \oplus V_2 \oplus \dots \oplus V_s
$$

- **Derivation Process**:
    
    - If the representation of the zero vector is not unique (there exists a non-zero vector sum equal to $ 0 $), then by adding these up, any vector's representation will have infinitely many combinations.
        
    - Conversely, if the representation of $ 0 $ is unique (i.e., $\sum \alpha_i = 0 \implies \alpha_i = 0 $), then by taking the difference of two representations of $\beta $, we find that the two representations are completely identical.
        

The essence of a direct sum is that the subspaces "do not overlap" (except for the zero vector).

- **Non-direct Sum Case**: If $ V_1 \cap V_2 \neq \{0\}$, then there exists a non-zero vector $\alpha \in V_1 \cap V_2 $. In this case, the zero vector can have a non-trivial representation: $ 0 = \alpha + (-\alpha)$. Geometrically, this is represented by two planes (or lines) intersecting at a line.
    
- **Direct Sum Case**: If $ V_1 \cap V_2 = \{0\}$, then $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 0 \implies \alpha_1 = -\alpha_2 \in V_1 \cap V_2 = \{0\}$. This means the decomposition is unique. Geometrically, this is represented by two spaces intersecting only at the origin.

When dealing with multiple subspaces $ V_1, \dots, V_s $, pairwise intersection is not enough; a stronger condition must be met.

**Theorem:**

$ V_1 + \dots + V_s $ is the direct sum if and only if for every $ k $ ($ 1 \le k \le s $) the $ k $ intersection of the subspace with the sum of all preceding subspaces contains only the zero vector:

$$
V_k \cap (V_1 + \dots + V_{k-1}) = \{0\}
$$

- **Proof Key Points**:
    
    - **Sufficiency**: Assume $\sum \alpha_i = 0 $. Take the last non-zero vector $\alpha_k $, then $\alpha_k = -(\alpha_1 + \dots + \alpha_{k-1})$. This implies that $\alpha_k $ lies in the intersection of $ V_k $ and the sum of the preceding subspaces, which contradicts the condition.
        
    - **Necessity**: If the intersection is not zero, then there exists a non-zero vector that can be expressed by the preceding set of vectors, leading to a non-unique representation of the zero vector.

---

#### III. Equivalent Proposition Theorems

The following propositions are equivalent for determining whether a subspace forms a direct sum:

- **Uniqueness of Representation**: $ V_1 + \dots + V_s $ is a direct sum (i.e., the representation of $ 0 $ is unique).
    
- **Basis Preservation**: If we take the basis for each subspace $ V_1, \dots, V_s $ and combine these basis vectors, the resulting set of vectors is **still linearly independent** in the entire space.
    
    - In other words, these combined basis vectors form a basis for the sum space $ V_1 \oplus \dots \oplus V_s $.
- **Dimension Sum**: $ dim(V_1+...+V_s)=dim(V_1)+...+dim(V_s)$ When $\dim(V_1 \cap V_2) = 0 $ (i.e., $ V_1 \cap V_2 = \{0\}$), the dimension formula simplifies to:

$$
\dim(V_1 + V_2) = \dim V_1 + \dim V_2
$$

The sum at this point is called a **direct sum**, denoted as $ V_1 \oplus V_2 $. This means every vector in the sum space can be **uniquely** represented as the sum of vectors from $ V_1 $ and $ V_2 $.

Let's look at a theorem; this was actually mentioned in the unexpected appearance of the Vandermonde determinant in mathematical notes, discussing the direct sum.

**Theorem:**

Let $ V_1, V_2, \dots, V_s $ be the **eigenspace** corresponding to distinct eigenvalues $ A $ of the square matrix $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_s $, then their sum is a **direct sum**.

- **Proof Idea (Vandermonde Method)**:
    
    1. Let $\alpha_1 + \alpha_2 + \dots + \alpha_s = 0 $, where $\alpha_i \in V_i $.
        
    2. By multiplying the equation by $ A $ on the left $ s-1 $ times, and according to $ A\alpha_i = \lambda_i \alpha_i $, we obtain a series of linear equations:

$$
\begin{cases} \alpha_1 + \dots + \alpha_s = 0 \\ \lambda_1 \alpha_1 + \dots + \lambda_s \alpha_s = 0 \\ \vdots \\ \lambda_1^{s-1} \alpha_1 + \dots + \lambda_s^{s-1} \alpha_s = 0 \end{cases}
$$

3. Since the eigenvalues $\lambda_i $ are distinct pairwise, the corresponding **Vandermonde determinant** is non-zero, which implies that every component $\alpha_i $ must be all $ 0 $.

We can go one step further and consider so-called generalized eigenspaces.

### Direct Sum of Generalized Eigenspaces and Polynomial Decomposition

#### I. Direct Sum Property of Generalized Eigenspaces

**Theorem**: 

Let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_s \in K $ be the distinct eigenvalues of the matrix $ A \in M_n(K)$, and let $ r_1, \dots, r_s > 0 $ be the corresponding powers, then the sum of their generalized eigenspaces is a direct sum:

$$
\text{Ker}(A - \lambda_1 I)^{r_1} \oplus \dots \oplus \text{Ker}(A - \lambda_s I)^{r_s}
$$

- **Proof Logic (Constructive Contradiction Method)**:
    
    1. Assume there exists a non-zero vector sum equal to zero: $\alpha_1 + \dots + \alpha_s = 0 $.
        
    2. For $\alpha_1 $, find the minimum index $ t $ that makes it "degenerate" to the eigenvector level, and define $\beta_1 = (A - \lambda_1 I)^{t-1} \alpha_1 \neq 0 $. At this point, $\beta_1 $ is an eigenvector belonging to $\lambda_1 $ (the action of $ A-\lambda I $ on $\beta $ is $ 0 $ ).
        
    3. Multiply the original equation by the operator $(A - \lambda_1 I)^{t-1} \prod_{j \neq 1} (A - \lambda_j I)^{r_j}$ on the left.
        
    4. Except for the first term, the remaining terms will cancel out because they lie in $\text{Ker}$ } $ 0 $.
        
    5. Finally, we derive $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)^{r_2} \dots (\lambda_1 - \lambda_s)^{r_s} \beta_1 = 0 $. Since the eigenvalues are distinct and $\beta_1 \neq 0 $, a contradiction arises, thus proving that each component must be zero.

So what use are all those polynomial concepts I studied? In fact, by using polynomials, we can give a decomposition of the space:

## Detailed proof of the Primary Decomposition Theorem

### I. Theorem

Let $ f_1(x), \dots, f_s(x) \in K[x]$ be pairwise coprime. Denote $ f(x) = \prod_{i=1}^s f_i(x)$. For $ A \in M_n(K)$, we have:

$$
\text{Ker } f(A) = \text{Ker } f_1(A) \oplus \text{Ker } f_2(A) \oplus \dots \oplus \text{Ker } f_s(A)
$$

---

### II. Deconstruction of Proof Steps

#### 1. Proving the sum is a "direct sum"

To prove it is a direct sum, it suffices to prove $ V_i \cap (\sum_{j \neq i} V_j) = \{0\}$, where $ V_i = \text{Ker } f_i(A)$.

- **Constructing an Auxiliary Polynomial**: Let $ F_i(x) = \frac{f(x)}{f_i(x)}$, which is the product of all polynomials other than $ f_i $.
    
- **Using Coprimality**: Since $(f_i(x), f_j(x)) = 1 $ (for all $ j \neq i $), we deduce that $ f_i(x)$ and $ F_i(x)$ are coprime, i.e., $(f_i, F_i) = 1 $.
    
- **Bézout's Identity**: There exists $ u(x), v(x)$ such that $ u(x)f_i(x) + v(x)F_i(x) = 1 $.
    
- **Operator Form**: Replace $ x $ with the matrix $ A $, acting on an arbitrary vector $\alpha \in V_i \cap (\sum_{j \neq i} V_j)$:
    
    - $\alpha = u(A)f_i(A)\alpha + v(A)F_i(A)\alpha $- Because $\alpha \in \text{Ker } f_i(A)$, then $ f_i(A)\alpha = 0 $.
        
    - Because $\alpha $ is in the sum of the other kernel spaces, and $ F_i(A)$ contains all other factors, then $ F_i(A)\alpha = 0 $.
        
    - **Conclusion**: $\alpha = 0 $, so the sum is a direct sum.
        

#### 2. Proving the inclusion relationship of the kernel spaces

We need to prove $\sum \text{Ker } f_i(A) = \text{Ker } f(A)$.

- **Taking $ s=2 $ as an example**: Given $(f_1, f_2) = 1 $, then $ u(A)f_1(A) + v(A)f_2(A) = I $.
    
- For $\forall \alpha \in \text{Ker } f(A)$:

$$
\alpha = I\alpha = \underbrace{u(A)f_1(A)\alpha}_{\in \text{Ker } f_2(A)} + \underbrace{v(A)f_2(A)\alpha}_{\in \text{Ker } f_1(A)}
$$

(Note: $ f_2(A) \cdot [u(A)f_1(A)\alpha] = u(A)f(A)\alpha = 0 $)
    
- **Generalization to the general case**: Using induction, we treat $ f(x)$ as the product of $ f_1(x)$ and $(f_2 \dots f_s)(x)$, peeling it layer by layer.

Combining the two aspects of the proof completes it. That is, from a polynomial decomposition, we can obtain a space decomposition, which is very beautiful.

If we want to decompose the entire space, what polynomial should we consider?

### Annihilating Polynomial and Space Decomposition

#### I. Annihilating Polynomial

- **Definition**: Let $ A \in M_n(K)$. If there exists a polynomial $ f(x) \in K[x]$ such that $ f(A) = 0 $, then $ f(x)$ is an **annihilating polynomial** of $ A $.
    
- **Classic Examples**:
    
    - **Idempotent Transformation (Projection)**: $ x^2 - x $ is its annihilating polynomial (because $ A^2 = A $).
        
    - **Anti-involution Transformation (Mirror)**: $ x^2 - 1 $ is its annihilating polynomial (because $ A^2 = I $).
        

#### II. Space Full Decomposition Driven by Annihilating Polynomials

**Core Theorem**:

If the annihilating polynomial $ f(x)$ of the matrix $ A $ can be factored over the field $ K $ into a product of pairwise coprime factors $ f(x) = f_1(x) \dots f_s(x)$, then the entire space $ V $ has a direct sum decomposition:

$$
V = \text{Ker } f_1(A) \oplus \dots \oplus \text{Ker } f_s(A)
$$

> **Logical Leap**: Here, because $ f(A)=0 $, so $\text{Ker } f(A)$ is directly the entire space $ V $.

---

#### III. Necessary and Sufficient Condition for Diagonalizability

This is one of the most elegant conclusions in linear algebra:

**Matrix $ A $ over a field $ K $ is diagonalizable if and only if $ A $ there exists a minimal polynomial that can be factored into a product of distinct linear factors: $ K $ **

$$
f(x) = (x - \mu_1)(x - \mu_2) \dots (x - \mu_s), \quad \mu_i \in K \text{ and distinct}
$$

- **Necessity Derivation**: If $ A $ is diagonalizable, then the entire space is the direct sum of its eigenspaces. Applying the operator $(A - \lambda_1 I)\dots(A - \lambda_s I)$ to any vector yields $ 0 $, which shows that this product is the annihilating polynomial.
    
- **Sufficiency Derivation**: If such an annihilating polynomial exists, by the Primary Decomposition Theorem, the entire space $ V $ decomposes into the direct sum of its eigenspaces (or kernels):

$$
V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^s \text{Ker}(A - \mu_i I)
$$

This means that $ V $ has a basis formed by eigenvectors, i.e., $ A $ is diagonalizable.
    

---

#### IV. Case Application: Decomposition of the Anti-involution Transformation

**Scenario**: Let $ V = M_n(K)$, and consider the transpose transformation $ T: A \to A^T $.

1. **Characteristic Equation**: Obviously $ T^2 = I $, so the annihilating polynomial is $ f(x) = x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)$.
    
2. **Space Direct Sum**: Since $ 1 $ and $-1 $ are distinct, the space $ V $ can be decomposed as:

$$
V = \text{Ker}(T - I) \oplus \text{Ker}(T + I)
$$

3. **Geometric Meaning**: 
    
    - $\text{Ker}(T - I)$ is the set of all matrices satisfying $ A^T = A $ (**Symmetric matrices** $ V_1 $).
        
    - $\text{Ker}(T + I)$ is the set of all matrices satisfying $ A^T = -A $ (**Skew-symmetric matrices** $ V_2 $).
        
    - **Conclusion**: Any square matrix can be uniquely decomposed as the sum of a symmetric matrix and a skew-symmetric matrix: $ A = \frac{A+A^T}{2} + \frac{A-A^T}{2}$.

## Complementary Subspace and Outer Direct Sum

### I. Complementary Subspace

If a subspace is a part of the space, then the complementary subspace is the perfect complement of the "remaining part".

- **Definition**: Let $ W, W'$ be a subspace of $ V $. If it satisfies $ V = W \oplus W'$ (i.e., $ V $ is their direct sum), then $ W $ and $ W'$ are **complementary subspaces**.
    
- **Existence Theorem**: Every linear subspace has a complementary subspace.
    
- **Construction Logic**: 
    
    1. Take a basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r $ for $ W $.
        
    2. Extend it to a basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r, \alpha_{r+1}, \dots, \alpha_n $ for the entire space $ V $.
        
    3. The subspace spanned by the augmented vectors $\langle \alpha_{r+1}, \dots, \alpha_n \rangle $ is a complement space of $ W $.
        
- **Note.** A complementary subspace is usually **nonunique**. Extending the basis in different ways gives complementary subspaces with different positions in the ambient space.
    

---

### II. External Direct Sum

When we have several independent linear spaces and want to combine them into a larger space, we need to use the external direct sum.

- **Definition**: Let $ V_1, \dots, V_s $ be a linear space over the field $ K $. Its external direct sum is defined as the Cartesian product set:

$$
V_1 \oplus \dots \oplus V_s = \{ (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_s) \mid \alpha_i \in V_i \}
$$

- **Operation Rules**: 
    
    - **Addition**: Component-wise addition, $(\alpha_1, \dots) + (\beta_1, \dots) = (\alpha_1 + \beta_1, \dots)$.
        
    - **Scalar Multiplication**: Scalar multiplication on each component, $ k(\alpha_1, \dots) = (k\alpha_1, \dots)$.
        

---

### III. Unification of External and Internal Direct Sums

Although the "external direct sum" constructs a new set from multiple sets, it can essentially be seen as an "internal direct sum".

- **Embedding and Isomorphism**: 
    
    Construction $ V $ of a subspace $ V_i'$such that $ V_i'$ the elements in $ i $ have non-zero vectors only at the $ 0 $:

$$
V_i' = \{ (0, \dots, \alpha_i, \dots, 0) \mid \alpha_i \in V_i \}
$$

- **Conclusion**: 
    
    1. $ V_i' \cong V_i $ (each $ V_i'$ is isomorphic to the original space $ V_i $).
        
    2. The entire external direct sum space $ V $ is exactly the **internal direct sum** of these subspaces $ V_i'$:

# Isomorphism of Linear Spaces

### Definition of Linear Map and Isomorphism

position, and zero everywhere else. $ U, V $ Let $ K $ be two linear spaces over a field. If the mapping $\mathcal{A}: U \to V $ satisfies:

1. **Additivity**: $\mathcal{A}(\alpha + \beta) = \mathcal{A}\alpha + \mathcal{A}\beta, \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in U $
    
2. **Homogeneity**: $\mathcal{A}(k\alpha) = k\mathcal{A}\alpha, \quad \forall k \in K $, then $\mathcal{A}$ is called a **linear map** from $ U $ to $ V $.

If $\mathcal{A}$ is **bijective** (both injective and surjective), then $\mathcal{A}$ is called a **linear isomorphism** from $ U $ to $ V $, denoted as $ U \cong V $.

---

### 2. Key Criteria and Theorems for Isomorphism

- **Corollary**: Let $\mathcal{A}: U \to V $ be a linear map, and $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $ be a basis for $ U $. If $\mathcal{A}$ is a linear isomorphism between $ U $ and $ V $, then $\iff \mathcal{A}\alpha_1, \dots, \mathcal{A}\alpha_n $ is a basis for $ V $.
    
- **Fundamental Theorem**: A necessary and sufficient condition for a finite-dimensional $ K $-linear space $ U $ to be isomorphic to $ V $ is that their dimensions are equal:

$$
U \cong V \iff \dim U = \dim V
$$

---

### 3. Key Points for Proofs of Properties

- **Surjectivity Test**: $\mathcal{A}$ is surjective if $\iff \mathcal{A}\alpha_1, \dots, \mathcal{A}\alpha_n $ spans $ V $.
    
    That is, any vector in $ V $ can be expressed as a linear combination of $\mathcal{A}\alpha_i $: $\text{Im} \mathcal{A} = L(\mathcal{A}\alpha_1, \dots, \mathcal{A}\alpha_n)$.
    
- **Injectivity Test**: $\mathcal{A}$ is injective if $\iff \mathcal{A}\alpha_1, \dots, \mathcal{A}\alpha_n $ is linearly independent.
    
    Proof idea: From $\sum k_i \mathcal{A}\alpha_i = 0 $, we deduce $\mathcal{A}(\sum k_i \alpha_i) = 0 $. If $\mathcal{A}$ is injective, the kernel is zero, and we then use the independence of $\alpha_i $ to prove $ k_i = 0 $.

### 4. Coordinate Isomorphism

In an $ n $-dimensional linear space $ V $, after choosing a basis $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n $, a one-to-one correspondence is established between every element $\alpha = \sum k_i \alpha_i $ in $ V $ and its coordinate vector $[k_1, \dots, k_n]^T \in K^n $.

**Conclusion**: This correspondence preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication, meaning $ V $ is linearly isomorphic to the vector space $ K^n $.

The isomorphism relation for linear spaces satisfies:

- **Reflexivity**: $ V \cong V $- **Symmetry**: If $ U \cong V $, then $ V \cong U $- **Transitivity**: If $ U \cong V $ and $ V \cong W $, then $ U \cong W $
    
    Therefore, isomorphism is an **equivalence relation** on the set of linear spaces.

## The core of linear mappings: Kernel and Image

for any linear map, $\mathcal{A}: U \to V $there are two key subspaces:

- **Image Space**, $\text{Im}\,\mathcal{A}$**:$ V $ the set of all vectors that can be mapped to. It reflects the map's "breadth".
    
- **Kernel Space** $\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A}$**:$ U $ the set of vectors mapped to $ 0 $ in the middle. It reflects the "amount of information" lost by the mapping.
    
- **Canonical Mapping**: $\mathcal{A}: V \to V/W $ In special cases, the mapping $\alpha + W $maps a vector to its coset $\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A} = W $,$\text{Im}\,\mathcal{A} = V/W $.
    

## The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Mappings

This is the highlight of linear algebra. The theorem states: **Any linear mapping can be 'decomposed' into an isomorphic mapping.**

- **Isomorphism Relation**: 

$$
U/\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A} \cong \text{Im}\,\mathcal{A}
$$

- **Intuitive Understanding**: 
    
    , at this time $ U $ if we "bundle" the vectors in the domain $\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A}$that map to the same point (i.e., form the quotient space, modulo
    
- ) then the set of these "bundles" is in one-to-one correspondence with the image space. $\varphi: \alpha + \text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A} \mapsto \mathcal{A}\alpha $ is well-defined, and is both injective and surjective.
#### 1. Construction of the Induced Map

Define the mapping $\varphi: U/\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A} \to \text{Im}\,\mathcal{A}$, whose rule is:

$$
\varphi(\alpha + \text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A}) = \mathcal{A}\alpha
$$

- **Algebraic Compatibility**:$\varphi $ perfectly preserves the addition and scalar multiplication of vectors.
    
- **Geometric Mapping Relationship**: $ U $ vectors that originally mapped to the same image point $\beta $ in $\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A}$ (which form a coset), are now viewed as a whole in the quotient space. This means the mapping changes from a "many-to-one" to a one-to-one **linear isomorphism**.

#### 2. Dimension Conservation: Rank-Nullity Theorem

based on $ U/\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A} \cong \text{Im}\,\mathcal{A}$ of isomorphism, we must obtain a balance of dimensions:

$$
\dim U = \dim(\text{Ker}\,\mathcal{A}) + \dim(\text{Im}\,\mathcal{A})
$$

This can be seen as the 'energy conservation' of the domain space: some dimensions collapse to zero (the kernel), and the remaining dimensions unfold into the image of the mapping (the image space).
