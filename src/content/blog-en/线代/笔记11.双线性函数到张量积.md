# Measuring Linear Spaces: Algebraic Manifestation of Bilinear and Multilinear Forms

After understanding how linear functionals (the dual space) extract one-dimensional features of vectors through "selectors," we naturally face a more practical algebraic problem: **how to introduce "measure" in a linear space?** Traditional vector spaces only define addition and scalar multiplication; they are inherently "blind" and cannot directly discuss length, angle, or projection. In order to establish this geometric intuition in the abstract $ K $-linear space, $ V $ we need a mapping that takes two vectors and produces a scalar. This is the logical starting point for a bilinear function (Bilinear Function).

## Basic Concepts and Representation
### Deconstructing the Concept and Motivation: Linear Generalization from Univariate to Bivariate

We cannot directly feed two vectors into a regular linear functional because a linear functional takes only one input. If we want the mapping $ f: V \times V \to K $ to be compatible with the linear structure of the original space, the most naive intuition is "controlling variables"—when one vector is fixed, its behavior with respect to the other vector must be linear.

This leads to the strict definition of a bilinear function. If the mapping $ f: V \times V \to K $ satisfies the following two distributive laws and scalar multiplication homogeneity:

1. **Linear in the first variable (left linear):**
    
    $ f(k\alpha + l\beta, , \gamma) = kf(\alpha, \gamma) + lf(\beta, \gamma)$ 2. **Linear in the second variable (right linear):**$ f(\gamma, , k\alpha + l\beta) = kf(\gamma, \alpha) + lf(\gamma, \beta)$ For any vector $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in V $ and scalar $ k, l \in K $ If it holds for all $ f $ is called $ V $ a **bilinear function** on.

**Construction Motivation:** This two-way linear maintenance ensures that we can expand complex vector combinations, much like polynomial multiplication. It is the algebraic abstraction of the Inner Product, stripping away geometric constraints like positive definiteness, and retaining only the most basic algebraic compatibility.

### A Further Perspective: Natural Extension of Multilinear Functions

When we are no longer satisfied with the interaction of "two vectors," but rather wish to explore the multidimensional linear dependency among $ r $ vectors, the bilinear function naturally evolves into a **Multilinear Function**.

Let $ f: V^r = V \times V \times \dots \times V \to K $ be a function defined on the Cartesian product of $ r $ linear spaces. If it remains linear in **each individual variable** (i.e., when the remaining $ r-1 $ variables are fixed, it behaves as a linear functional with respect to the remaining one), then $ f $ is called a **$ r $-multilinear function** on $ V $.

In algebraic terms, this means that at the $ i $ position, it satisfies:

-

$$
f(\dots, , \alpha + \beta, , \dots) = f(\dots, , \alpha, , \dots) + f(\dots, , \beta, , \dots)
$$

-

$$
f(\dots, , k\alpha, , \dots) = k f(\dots, , \alpha, , \dots)
$$

When we extrapolate the "two variables" of a bilinear function to $ r $ variables, we are facing a mapping $ f: V^r \to K $. How do we record such a macroscopic mapping?

#### Complete Representation Derivation

Let $ V $ be a finite-dimensional linear space with a basis $\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n\}$. For $ r $ arbitrary input vectors $\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_r \in V $, their coordinate expansions with respect to this basis are:

$$
\beta_k = \sum_{i=1}^n x_k^{(i)} \alpha_i \quad (1 \le k \le r)
$$

where $ x_k^{(i)}$ represents the coefficient of the $ k $-th vector with respect to the $ i $-th basis vector $\alpha_i $.

Now, let $ f $ act on this set of vectors. Since $ f $ is linear in every variable, we can extract the summation signs and coordinate coefficients layer by layer, like peeling an onion:

$$
f(\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_r) = f\left(\sum_{i_1=1}^n x_1^{(i_1)} \alpha_{i_1}, \, \sum_{i_2=1}^n x_2^{(i_2)} \alpha_{i_2}, \, \dots, \, \sum_{i_r=1}^n x_r^{(i_r)} \alpha_{i_r}\right)
$$

By utilizing the complete distributive law (multilinearity), all the summation signs are stacked to the outermost layer, and all the coordinate coefficients are multiplied in cascade:

$$
f(\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots, \beta_r) = \sum_{1 \le i_1, \dots, i_r \le n} x_1^{(i_1)} x_2^{(i_2)} \cdots x_r^{(i_r)} f(\alpha_{i_1}, \alpha_{i_2}, \dots, \alpha_{i_r})
$$

**Construction Motivation:**

At this point, the behavior of the macroscopic function collapses entirely onto these $ n^r $ fixed scalar values:

$$
b_{i_1 i_2 \dots i_r} = f(\alpha_{i_1}, \alpha_{i_2}, \dots, \alpha_{i_r}) \in K
$$

In the case of bilinear functions ($ r=2 $), these constants form a two-dimensional matrix $ a_{ij}$; and in the case of $ r $-multilinear functions, these $ n^r $ constants form a **high-order array—this is the algebraic embodiment of a "Tensor" in modern mathematics and data science**. It shows that, just as the space formed by all bilinear functions constitutes space $ T_2(V)$, the entire system of arithmetic operations also maintains compatibility with addition and scalar multiplication, forming the multilinear function space $ T_r(V)$.

### The Dual Bridge: The Birth of the Induced Mapping

Returning to the bilinear function $ f: V \times V \to K $. If we look at it from a different perspective: not as a "melting pot" of two vectors, but as a "transformer"—when I feed in a fixed vector $\alpha $, doesn't the remaining position become a linear functional that only accepts one vector?

This leads to the construction of the Induced Mapping. For each fixed $\alpha \in V $, we define two linear functionals on $ V $ (i.e., elements in $ V^*$):

- **Left Functional (Fixing the left variable):** $\alpha_L: V \to K, \quad \alpha_L(\beta) := f(\alpha, \beta)$ - **Right Functional (Fixing the right variable):** $\alpha_R: V \to K, \quad \alpha_R(\beta) := f(\beta, \alpha)$ From this, we naturally induce two macroscopic linear mappings from the original space $ V $ to the dual space $ V^*$:

$$
\psi_L: V \to V^*, \quad \alpha \mapsto \alpha_L
$$

$$
\psi_R: V \to V^*, \quad \alpha \mapsto \alpha_R
$$

These two mappings are called the **left map** and the **right map**. They are the unique "appendages" of the bilinear function, transforming concrete vectors into functionals in the dual world.

### The Blind Spot of the Space: The Left Radical Space and the Right Radical Space

Since $\psi_L $ and $\psi_R $ are linear mappings, based on the core intuition of linear algebra, we must immediately examine their **Kernel**. What do the vectors that are mapped to the zero element of the dual space represent?

#### 1. Definition of the Radical Space

- **Left Radical:** If $\alpha \in \text{Ker}\,\psi_L $, it means that for any $\beta \in V $, there exists $\alpha_L(\beta) = f(\alpha, \beta) = 0 $. We call $\alpha $ as $ f $ a **left radical** of a matrix. The subspace formed by all left radicals $\text{Ker}\,\psi_L $ called $ f $ the **left radical space**.
    
- **Right Radical:** If $\alpha \in \text{Ker}\,\psi_R $, it means that for any $\beta \in V $, there exists $\alpha_R(\beta) = f(\beta, \alpha) = 0 $. We call $\alpha $ as $ f $ is a **right radical**. $\text{Ker}\,\psi_R $ called $ f $ The **right radical space** formed by all right radicals.
    

**Geometric Intuition:** Vectors in the radical space are like "invisible people" or "absolutely orthogonal" in a measure-theoretic sense. A left radical vector $\alpha $, when placed on the left, results in a measure of $ 0 $ regardless of what vector is chosen from the entire space. It is "isolated" from the whole space.

#### 2. Explicit Representation in Matrix Form

If we fix a basis and write the bilinear function as a measure matrix $ A $, then finding the radical space is perfectly equivalent to solving the classic homogeneous linear equation system.

Let the vector $\alpha $ have coordinates $ X $ in the basis.

- Since for all $ Y $ have $ X^T A Y = 0 $then necessarily have $ X^T A = 0 $and transposing gives **$ A^T X = 0 $**.
    
- Similarly, for the right radical, all $ Y $ have $ Y^T A X = 0 $, there must be **$ A X = 0 $**.
    

This leads to an extremely beautiful measurement theorem:

- **The left radical space** $= \{(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n)X \mid A^T X = 0\}$ - **the right radical space** $= \{(\alpha_1 \dots \alpha_n)X \mid A X = 0\}$ because the matrix $ A $ has equal ranks ($\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}(A^T)$). By the Rank-Nullity Theorem, the dimension of the solution space for the homogeneous equation is instantly locked:

$$
\text{Left root dimension} = \text{Right root dimension} = n - \text{rank}(A)
$$

**The Key Insight:** Although the left and right radical spaces might be geometrically different subspaces because $ A $ might be non-symmetric, their **dimensions are always equal in absolute value**.

### Perfect Measurement: Non-degenerate Bilinear Functions and Isomorphism

If a measure space contains "radical vectors" that yield $ 0 $ when operated with anything (except the zero vector itself), then this measure is flawed (degenerate). To establish a perfect geometric structure, we hope that the blind spot of the space is as small as possible.

**Definition:** If both the left and right radical spaces of $ f $ are the zero subspace (meaning only the zero vector can be orthogonal to the entire space), then $ f $ is called a **Non-degenerate Bilinear Function**.

#### Core Equivalence Theorem
	
Combining all the above derivations, the following four conditions achieve algebraic equivalence:

$$
f \text{ Non-degenerate} \iff f \text{ Metric matrix } A \text{ Full rank } (\text{rank}(A)=n)
$$

$$
\iff \psi_L: V \to V^* \text{ Is a linear isomorphism}
$$

$$
\iff \psi_R: V \to V^* \text{ Is a linear isomorphism}
$$

**Confidence Rating: High** (The equivalence between the dual map and full rank forms the cornerstone of the metric theory of finite-dimensional linear spaces).

#### Structural Evaluation and Dual Deficiency

This theorem is extremely profound. In general, the linear space $ V $ and its dual space $ V^*$, although having the same dimension, **do not possess a natural (basis-independent) isomorphism**.

However, once we specify a **non-degenerate bilinear form $ f $** on $ V $, this form endows the space with an intrinsic metric structure, thereby **forcing a natural isomorphism $\psi $** between $ V $ and $ V^*$ that does not depend on external bases:

$$
f(\alpha, \beta) = \big(\psi(\alpha)\big)(\beta)
$$

Conversely, given any isomorphism $\psi: V \to V^*$, we can naturally define a non-degenerate bilinear metric.

## Change of Basis
In the algebraic framework of linear spaces, the non-degenerate bilinear form successfully builds a bridge between the original space and the dual space. However, coordinate representation always depends on the choice of a specific basis. Once we extend our perspective from a single, fixed basis to the **dynamic transition of bases (basis transformation)**, the deep algebraic properties inherent in the bilinear form trigger a dramatic symmetry division in the space of matrices. This prompts us to re-examine the geometric essence of symmetry and anti-symmetry, ultimately completing a macroscopic construction of the ultimate algebraic invariant, the "determinant," using multilinear forms.

### Basis Transition: The Congruence Law for Metric Matrices

When we change the basis of the space $ V $, the bilinear form $ f $ itself remains unchanged as a geometric entity, but its "identity card"—the metric matrix—must follow specific rules of evolution.

#### 1. Derivation of the Complete Congruence Formula

Suppose there exist two different bases for the linear space $ V $, which are connected by a **transition matrix $ P $**:

$$
(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n) = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n) P
$$

Let the column vector coordinates of the vector $\alpha, \beta \in V $ in the new basis $\{\beta_i\}$ be $ X, Y $. Due to the inverse relationship between basis transformation and coordinate transformation, its coordinates in the old basis $\{\alpha_i\}$ will be linearly mapped by $ P $, taking the form of $ PX $ and $ PY $, respectively.

Now, let the bilinear form $ f $ act on this pair of vectors. We expand it in the framework of the old basis:

$$
f(\alpha, \beta) = (PX)^T A (PY)
$$

Using the distributive property of matrix multiplication's transpose $(PX)^T = X^T P^T $, the algebraic expression naturally combines into:

$$
f(\alpha, \beta) = X^T (P^T A P) Y
$$

**Motivation for Construction and Algebraic Elevation:**

Because the metric matrix $ B $ in the new basis must satisfy the definition $ f(\alpha, \beta) = X^T B Y $, and this matrix representation is unique in a finite-dimensional space, we are thus forced to obtain the transition law between the old and new metric matrices:

$$
B = P^T A P
$$

This matrix relationship, taking the form of $ B = P^T A P $, is called **Congruence**. This is distinctly different from the Similarity relationship $ P^{-1}AP $ for linear transformations under basis change. It profoundly shows that: **the bilinear form is a deeper geometric structure than the linear transformation; its congruence law preserves the metric invariant of the interaction between two vectors, not the eigenvalues of the map.**

### The Division of Symmetry: Algebraic Deficiency of Symmetric and Anti-symmetric Forms

Under the evolution of the congruence law, two types of matrices exhibit unparalleled structural stability: **symmetric matrices** and **anti-symmetric matrices**. These correspond to the two core physical forms of the bilinear form.

#### 1. Definitions of Symmetric and Anti-symmetric Forms

- **Symmetric Bilinear Form:** Satisfies $ f(\alpha, \beta) = f(\beta, \alpha), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in V $. The metric matrix in any basis satisfies $ A = A^T $.
    
- **Anti-symmetric Bilinear Form:** Satisfies $ f(\alpha, \beta) = -f(\beta, \alpha), \quad \forall \alpha, \beta \in V $. The metric matrix in any basis satisfies $ A = -A^T $.
    

#### 2. Algebraic Constraints in Characteristic Non-2 Fields ($\text{ch } K \neq 2 $)

A technical prerequisite frequently appears in the lecture notes: $\text{ch } K \neq 2 $ (the field characteristic is not 2, i.e., $ 1+1 \neq 0 $). Over this healthy field, anti-symmetry has a perfect equivalent characterization:

$$
f \text{ Antisymmetric} \iff f(\alpha, \alpha) = 0, \quad \forall \alpha \in V
$$

- **Proof in the $\Longrightarrow $ direction:** Let $ f $ be anti-symmetric. Let $\beta = \alpha $, then $ f(\alpha, \alpha) = -f(\alpha, \alpha)$. Rearranging gives $ 2f(\alpha, \alpha) = 0 $. Because $\text{ch } K \neq 2 $, we have $ 2 \neq 0 $, and thus must have $ f(\alpha, \alpha) = 0 $.
    
- **Proof in the $\Longleftarrow $ direction (Motivation from Polarization Identity):** If the self-action is $ 0 $ for any vector, consider $ f(\alpha + \beta, \alpha + \beta) = 0 $. Using the full bilinear expansion:

$$
f(\alpha, \alpha) + f(\alpha, \beta) + f(\beta, \alpha) + f(\beta, \beta) = 0
$$

Substituting the condition that the self-action is $ 0 $ forces the middle two terms to satisfy: $ f(\alpha, \beta) + f(\beta, \alpha) = 0 \implies f(\alpha, \beta) = -f(\beta, \alpha)$.
    

### In-depth Example: Metrics in Continuous Spaces and Matrix Spaces

To ground the abstract symmetry, the lecture notes provide two crucial examples in analysis and quantum algebra:

#### 1. Inner Product on the Function Space $ C[a, b]$

Let $ V = C[a, b]$ be the space of continuous functions on a closed interval, defining:

$$
f(h, g) = \int_a^b h(x)g(x)dx
$$

Due to the commutativity of real number multiplication $ h(x)g(x) = g(x)h(x)$, this function is clearly **symmetric**. Simultaneously, if $ h \neq 0 $, then $ f(h, h) = \int_a^b h^2(x)dx > 0 $, satisfying positive definiteness. This shows that **the concretization of a positive definite symmetric bilinear form in infinite dimensions is the inner product**.

#### 2. Frobenius Inner Product on the Matrix Space $ M_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})$

Let $ V = M_{m,n}(\mathbb{R})$, defining:

$$
f(A, B) = \text{tr}(A^T B)
$$

Using the property of the trace $\text{tr}(M) = \text{tr}(M^T)$, we have $\text{tr}(A^T B) = \text{tr}((A^T B)^T) = \text{tr}(B^T A) = f(B, A)$, proving its **symmetry**. When $ A \neq 0 $, $ f(A, A) = \text{tr}(A^T A) = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2 > 0 $, forming the natural energy metric within the matrix space.

### From Anti-symmetric $ n $-Multilinear Form to the Determinant

Now, we twist together the lines of basis transformation, anti-symmetry, and multilinear forms to witness the **natural birth of the determinant**.

Consider an **anti-symmetric $ n $-multilinear form** $ f: K^n \times \dots \times K^n \to K $ on the space $ K^n $. This means that if the input $ n $ column vectors contain any two identical columns, the function value instantly becomes zero.

#### 1. Derivation of the Complete Alternating Expansion

We arrange the $ n $ input column vectors as the columns of a square matrix:

$$
A = [a_{ij}] = [\mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2}, \dots, \mathbf{a_n}]
$$

Let each column vector in the standard basis $\{\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2, \dots, \varepsilon_n\}$ perform a linear combination expansion:

$$
\mathbf{a_j} = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ij} \varepsilon_i
$$

Substituting into the $ n $-bilinear form $ f $, utilizing the multilinear full expansion rule derived in the previous chapter:

$$
f(\mathbf{a_1}, \mathbf{a_2}, \dots, \mathbf{a_n}) = f\left(\sum_{i_1=1}^n a_{i_1 1}\varepsilon_{i_1}, \, \dots, \, \sum_{i_n=1}^n a_{i_n n}\varepsilon_{i_n}\right) = \sum_{1 \le i_1, \dots, i_n \le n} a_{i_1 1} a_{i_2 2} \dots a_{i_n n} f(\varepsilon_{i_1}, \varepsilon_{i_2}, \dots, \varepsilon_{i_n})
$$

**Strong Pruning due to Antisymmetry:**

In the summation of the massive $ n^n $ terms above, **if the index sequence $(i_1, i_2, \dots, i_n)$ contains any two identical numbers, due to the antisymmetry of $ f $ (the value is 0 if two columns are the same), that term immediately collapses to 0**. Therefore, the surviving terms must have an index sequence that is a **permutation** $\sigma $ of $ 1, 2, \dots, n $.

For any permutation $\sigma = (i_1, i_2, \dots, i_n)$, we can restore it to the standard order $(1, 2, \dots, n)$ through a finite number of transpositions. Since $ f $ is antisymmetric, each transposition changes the sign of the function value:

$$
f(\varepsilon_{i_1}, \varepsilon_{i_2}, \dots, \varepsilon_{i_n}) = \text{sgn}(i_1 i_2 \dots i_n) f(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2, \dots, \varepsilon_n)
$$

where $\text{sgn}$ is the sign of the permutation (1 for even permutations, -1 for odd permutations).

Substituting this result back into the summation formula, and factoring out the common constant term $ f(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n)$ to the outermost layer:

$$
f(\mathbf{a_1}, \dots, \mathbf{a_n}) = f(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n) \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \text{sgn}(\sigma) a_{\sigma(1) 1} a_{\sigma(2) 2} \dots a_{\sigma(n) n}
$$

#### 2. The Embodiment of the Determinant

Carefully observe the expression remaining inside the summation sign: $\sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \text{sgn}(\sigma) a_{\sigma(1) 1} \dots a_{\sigma(n) n}$. This is precisely the **Leibniz definition of the determinant** for the matrix $ A $!

This leads to an extremely profound algebraic conclusion:

$$
f(\mathbf{a_1}, \dots, \mathbf{a_n}) = f(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n) \cdot \det(A)
$$

**Core Structure Insight:**

If we normalize the output value under the standard basis to $ f(\varepsilon_1, \dots, \varepsilon_n) = 1 $, then **this unique antisymmetric $ n $-multilinear form $ f $ itself is the Determinant**.

This reveals the essential geometric motivation of the determinant: it is not a jumble of cross-products, but the **top-form** defined on the $ n $ dimensional vector space that measures the oriented volume of the $ n $ dimensional hyperparallelepiped.

### Destination and New Beginning: The Canonical Form of Symmetric Matrices

After transitioning from antisymmetry to the high-dimensional determinant, let's turn our attention back to symmetric bilinear forms. Since the ordinary metric matrix undergoes a congruence evolution of the form $ P^T A P $ under a basis change, we naturally seek the “simplest, cleanest” basis where the metric matrix achieves its utmost simplicity.

This is the **Theorem of the Canonical Form for Symmetric Bilinear Forms**:

Let $\text{ch } K \neq 2 $. For any symmetric bilinear form $ f $, in the space $ V $, there must exist a set of extremely perfect bases such that the metric matrix $ f $ becomes a **Diagonal Matrix** in that basis. At this point, all cross-terms vanish, and the bilinear form transforms into the purest weighted sum of squares:

$$
f(\alpha, \beta) = d_1 x_1 y_1 + d_2 x_2 y_2 + \dots + d_n x_n y_n
$$

Over the field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, there are three classic paths to diagonalizing the metric matrix:

1. **Orthogonal Transformation:** Utilizing the theory of eigenvalues of real symmetric matrices, which preserves the geometric axes (a feature unique to the real field) and is both congruent and similar.
    
2. **Lagrange's Method:** Elimination and perfect square construction at a purely algebraic level, possessing extremely high universality.
    
3. **Pairwise Elementary Row and Column Transformations:** When applying elementary row operations to the matrix $ A $, one must immediately apply **exactly the same** elementary column operations to maintain the congruence structure of $ P^T A P $.
    

So, after discussing the theoretical level, let's see how to calculate it specifically.

### Constructing the Algorithm Flow: Pairwise Elementary Transformations

To solve for the invertible transition matrix $ P $ such that $ P^T A P = D $ becomes a diagonal matrix, the best mapping of Lagrange's method into matrix language is the **Simultaneous Row and Column Operations**.

#### 1. Motivation for Constructing the Companion Matrix

In the case of similarity diagonalization of linear transformations, we use the augmented matrix $[A \mid I]$ to perform elementary row operations to find the inverse matrix. However, in the case of congruence transformation of a bilinear form, since $ A $ acts on $ P $ simultaneously by its transpose $ P^T $ (left multiplication, corresponding to row operations) and $ P $ (right multiplication, corresponding to column operations), the traditional single-sided augmentation loses effectiveness.

To simultaneously track the evolution of the metric matrix and the accumulation of the transition matrix $ P $ in a dynamic transformation, we must construct a **Double-Layer Vertical Companion Matrix**:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} A \\ -- \\ I \end{bmatrix}
$$

- **Upper Half:** Contains the metric matrix to be diagonalized $ A \in M_n(K)$.
    
- **Lower half.** Start with the identity matrix $ I \in M_n(K)$ and use it like a counter to record every **column operation**.
    

#### 2. The Law of

## Constructing a basis for the space of bilinear functions
### Motivation

Given the dual space $ V^*$ of a finite-dimensional linear space $ V $ (composed of all linear functionals), there exists a dual basis $\{\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*\}$. Now we consider the linear space $ T_2(V)$ formed by all bilinear functions on $ V $.

We hope to find a natural and intuitive basis for $ T_2(V)$.

A linear functional $\xi^* \in V^*$ can only take one vector. To make it take two vectors and produce bilinearity, the most straightforward idea is to **multiply the two linear functionals**.

### The functional tensor product $\xi^* \otimes \eta^*$

For $\xi^*, \eta^* \in V^*$, we define a function $\xi^* \otimes \eta^*$ on $ V \times V $, whose action rule is:

$$
(\xi^* \otimes \eta^*)(\alpha, \beta) = \xi^*(\alpha) \cdot \eta^*(\beta)
$$

Since both $\xi^*$ and $\eta^*$ are linear, when multiplied together, they satisfy the distributive law and the scalar multiplication axiom for $\alpha $ and $\beta $, respectively. Therefore, $\xi^* \otimes \eta^*$ must be a bilinear function (i.e., $\xi^* \otimes \eta^* \in T_2(V)$).

### Deriving the completeness of the basis and the rank of the metric matrix

Let $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n\}$ be a basis for $ V $, and $\{\alpha_1^*, \dots, \alpha_n^*\}$ be its corresponding dual basis.

We let the constructed element $\alpha_i^* \otimes \alpha_j^*$ act on the basis vectors pair $(\alpha_k, \alpha_s)$:

$$
(\alpha_i^* \otimes \alpha_j^*)(\alpha_k, \alpha_s) = \alpha_i^*(\alpha_k) \cdot \alpha_j^*(\alpha_s) = \delta_{ik} \delta_{js}
$$

This property is very beautiful; it means that $\alpha_i^* \otimes \alpha_j^*$ is a **precise selector**: it outputs 1 only when the input is exactly the $ i $-th and $ j $-th basis vectors, and 0 otherwise.

Therefore, if we consider $\alpha_i^* \otimes \alpha_j^*$ as an element in $ T_2(V)$, its metric matrix on the basis vectors is the **elementary matrix** $ E_{ij}$ (a matrix that is 1 only at $(i,j)$, and 0 elsewhere).

Because $ E_{ij}$ forms a basis for the matrix space $ M_n(K)$, and $ T_2(V)$ is isomorphic to $ M_n(K)$, we have:

$$
\{\alpha_i^* \otimes \alpha_j^* \mid 1 \le i, j \le n\} \text{ forms a basis for the space of bilinear functions } T_2(V) \text{ constitute}
$$

Any bilinear function $ f \in T_2(V)$ can be uniquely expanded as:

$$
f = \sum_{i,j} f(\alpha_i, \alpha_j) \alpha_i^* \otimes \alpha_j^*
$$

#### Supplementary thought: Matrix characteristics of a single product term

If we take any two ordinary linear functionals $\xi^* = \sum a_i \alpha_i^*$ and $\eta^* = \sum b_j \alpha_j^*$, they combine via the tensor product to form a special bilinear function $\xi^* \otimes \eta^*$. What is its metric matrix in the basis $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n\}$?

By definition, the result of acting on the $(i,j)$-th basis vector is $\xi^*(\alpha_i)\eta^*(\alpha_j) = a_i b_j $.

Thus, the metric matrix of the remaining sub-blocks is:

$$
[a_i b_j] = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ \vdots \\ a_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} b_1 & \cdots & b_n \end{bmatrix}
$$

This is a typical form of **column vector multiplied by a row vector**. **Its matrix rank must be 1**. This means that a single functional product like $\xi^* \otimes \eta^*$ can only express extremely special, degenerate bilinear functions, and general bilinear functions must be generated by the linear combination (sum) of these rank 1 matrices.

For this reason, we also call the space $ T_2(V)$ the tensor product space of $ V^*$ and $ V^*$, denoted as $ V^* \otimes V^* = T_2(V)$.

#### Part I: Explicit Definition of the Tensor Product Space $ U \otimes V $

##### 1. Motivation for Construction: How to Multiply Two "Pure Vectors"?

In our previous discussions, we learned how to multiply two linear functionals to obtain a bilinear function. But the task now is the reverse: given two ordinary linear spaces $ U $ and $ V $, how can we make the vectors $\alpha \in U $ and $\beta \in V $ within them perform a "multiplication" $\alpha \otimes \beta $ without relying on any extraneous external structure?

Mathematicians utilized the idea of "double dual": a vector $\alpha $ in a vector space $ U $ can naturally be viewed as a linear functional on its dual space $ U^*$ (i.e., $\alpha(\xi^*) = \xi^*(\alpha)$).

Since $\alpha $ and $\beta $ can be disguised as linear functionals, we can mimic the procedure from the previous section and "multiply" them as functionals.

##### 2. Explicit Definition

We define the **tensor product space** of $ U $ and $ V $ as:

$$
U \otimes V \triangleq T_2(U^*, V^*)
$$

In other words, $ U \otimes V $ is essentially the space of all **bilinear functions** defined on the dual space $ U^* \times V^*$.

For any pair of vectors $(\alpha, \beta) \in U \times V $, we construct a specific bilinear function $\alpha \otimes \beta \in T_2(U^*, V^*)$, whose action rule is defined as:

$$
(\alpha \otimes \beta)(\xi^*, \eta^*) \triangleq \xi^*(\alpha) \eta^*(\beta), \quad \forall \xi^* \in U^*, \eta^* \in V^*
$$

This definition is made completely legitimate by viewing $\alpha, \beta $ as a linear functional on $ U^*$ and $ V^*$, respectively. We call the bilinear function $\alpha \otimes \beta $ the **tensor product** of the vectors $\alpha $ and $\beta $.

#### Part II: Properties and Basis Proofs of the Tensor Product Operation

##### 1. Verification of Bilinear Mapping Properties

We have the mapping $\otimes: U \times V \to U \otimes V, (\alpha, \beta) \mapsto \alpha \otimes \beta $. It is easy to verify that it is a **bilinear mapping**. This means it satisfies the distributive law and the scalar multiplication interchange law for each variable separately:

- **Left Distributive Law**: $(\alpha + \beta) \otimes \gamma = \alpha \otimes \gamma + \beta \otimes \gamma $
    
- **Right Distributive Law**: $\alpha \otimes (\beta + \gamma) = \alpha \otimes \gamma + \alpha \otimes \gamma $
    
- **Scalar Multiplication Associativity**: $(k\alpha) \otimes \beta = k(\alpha \otimes \beta) = \alpha \otimes (k\beta)$
    

##### 2. Derivation of Basis Completeness (Core Proof)

**Theorem**: Let $\{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m\}$ be a basis for $ U $, and $\{\beta_1, \dots, \beta_n\}$ be a basis for $ V $. Then $\{\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j \mid 1 \le i \le m, 1 \le j \le n\}$ forms a basis for $ U \otimes V $.

$$

3. **Isomorphism Transition**: Since the space of bilinear functions $ T_2(U^*, V^*)$ is isomorphic to the space of measure matrices $ M_{m,n}(K)$, and the identity matrix $ E_{ij}$ forms a basis for $ M_{m,n}(K)$, these $ m \times n $ tensor product elements $\alpha_i \otimes \beta_j $ must also form a basis for $ U \otimes V $.

$$
