# Volume and Area of a Sphere
We have a fairly good feel for vectors in the familiar spaces of two or three dimensions. In a data space, though, a vector can have a great many dimensions. Think of a parameter vector for a large language model ($llm$). If we want to understand distributions in these vast data spaces, it might be a good idea to start by picking a nice object to study. (Choosing a nice object is a good thing anyway.)

Think of the spherical chicken in a vacuum from physics. Spheres have a lot of nice properties, so perhaps they're an easy place to start.

We examine the parameters of the sphere in order to find a pattern:

The unit circle in two dimensions: $dim=2, Area=2 \pi, Volume= \pi$ 
The unit sphere in three dimensions: $dim=3, Area=4 \pi, Volume=4/3 \pi$

Using the pattern-finding skills that were taught in second grade, $Volume=Area/dim$ holds here. Why is this? Because volume is area plus another layer $r$, so $dim$ is the result of some kind of differentiation, which is a very good explanation. But it would be best if we could provide a proof.

Calculating the volume is nothing more than integration, and based on our experience in two and three dimensions, it is convenient to transform to polar coordinates. So what about high-dimensional polar coordinates? Let's look for a pattern.

Two dimensions

$$
\int_r \int_\theta rdrd\theta
$$

Three dimensions

$$
\int_r\int_{\Omega}r^2drd\Omega
$$

On one hand, the increase in the power of $r$, this is the result of increasing the dimension, which is quite reasonable. The other parts remain unchanged, except that $\theta$ becomes the solid angle $\Omega=\sin{\theta}d\theta d\phi$. Then, in high dimensions, there is a mysterious solid angle $\Omega$, which should also be able to serve as this coefficient, in other words,

$$
Volume=\int_r\int_{\Omega}r^{d-1}drd\Omega
$$

Since $\Omega$ and $r$ are independent, we directly get

$$
Volume=\frac{Area}{d}
$$

In other words, our guess is quite reasonable. So how to calculate it specifically $Area$, or $Volume$? This is indeed not so easy to figure out. Let's explore the previous areas later~

# Points in high-dimensional space

We can view the samples as randomly distributed points, and the most commonly used assumption is the Gaussian distribution.

$$
p(x) \propto e^{-||x||^2}
$$

## Lemma of Probability Theory

When studying the distribution of these sample points, one cannot escape the Law of Large Numbers, and thus cannot escape Chebyshev's and Markov's

### Markov's inequality

$$
P(x\ge a) \le \frac{E(x)}{a}
$$

Here, we require $x$ to be non-negative. This describes an upper bound for the tail probability. A specific proof only requires two simple inequalities:

$$
E(x)=\int_0^\infty  xp(x)dx\ge \int_a^\infty xp(x)dx \ge \int_a^\infty ap(x)dx=aP(x\ge a)
$$

The first inequality is because the integrand is non-negative, and the second inequality is because the $x\ge a$ of the integration region. Moving the terms gives the conclusion.

### Chebyshev's inequality

$$
P(|x-E(x)|\ge c)\le \frac{Var(x)}{c^2}
$$

Using the variance can give a constraint on the deviation of a random variable from its expectation. Formally, this is very similar to $Markov$, and it is actually proven this way: the absolute value satisfies non-negativity, and squaring naturally satisfies non-negativity. Noticing $Var(x)=E((x-E(x))^2)$, the natural idea is to let a new random variable be $Y=(x-E(x))^2$, then:

$$
P(|x-E(x)|\ge c)=P(Y\ge c^2)\le \frac{E(Y)}{c^2}
$$

This is actually the form that needs to be proven.

### Law of Large Numbers

Consider several values of a random variable $x_1,...,x_n$, then their mean should not deviate too much from the expectation, especially when $n$ is large, quantitatively, there is

$$
P(|\frac{x_1+...+x_n}{n}-E(x)|\ge \epsilon)\le \frac{Var(x)}{n\epsilon^2}
$$

This looks very much like Chebyshev's. We make an equivalent transformation and use the linearity of expectation:

$$
\text{Left-handed}=P(|\frac{x_1+...+x_n}{n}-E(\frac{x_1+...+x_n}{n})|\ge \epsilon)
$$

This can be used with Chebyshev's

$$
\text{The above formula}\le \frac{Var(\frac{x_1+...+x_n}{n})}{\epsilon^2}
$$

Combined with

$$
Var(\frac{x_1+...+x_n}{n})=\frac{1}{n^2}Var(x_1+...+x_n)=\frac{Var(x)}{n}
$$

We get the conclusion

## Counter-intuitive phenomenon

### The quality distribution in the edge bubble

Whether in two or three dimensions, it seems that most of the area or volume of the Gaussian distribution is near 0, but we can calculate the high-dimensional Gaussian distribution with an expectation of 0 and a variance of 1 as follows.

$$
E(x_i^2)=E(x_i)^2+Var(x_i)=1
$$

Then the expected distance to the origin is $d$. Then, according to the Law of Large Numbers

$$
R^2\approx d,R\approx \sqrt d
$$

In other words, most points fall near $\sqrt d$, like a bubble, and not as in the intuition.

Another more direct method is to consider

$$
Volume((1-\epsilon)A)=(1-\epsilon)^d Volume(A)
$$

Then fixing $\epsilon$, there is, when $d$ is large enough, the volume decreases to 0 by narrowing the range a little.

### Any two points are almost mutually orthogonal

Similarly, we consider the random variable $D_i = X_i - Y_i$, then its expectation is 0 and its variance is 2, so

$$
E(Dist^2)=2d
$$
In other words, approximately

$$
Dist\approx \sqrt{2d}
$$

It is known to be almost isometric, and since both are almost at a $\sqrt d$ angle to the origin, it is also almost orthogonal by the Pythagorean theorem.

It is quite interesting, although unintuitive.

# Volume of the High-Dimensional Sphere

Although some interesting facts have been unlocked, we will not calculate the volume. But thinking of probability, since we assumed a Gaussian distribution, we can also calculate the probability, which is nothing more than an integral. Skipping the normalization constant that is not easy to remember, in short, we need to calculate

$$
\int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}r^{d-1}dr\cdot Area
$$

This is the form of the Gamma function

$$
Area\cdot \Gamma(d/2)\cdot1/2
$$

There is also $Area$ that we won't calculate, maybe we need to find another way, directly using Cartesian coordinates

$$
\int\int...\int e^{-x_1^2-...-x_d^2}dx_1dx_2...dx_d
$$

Due to independence, this can actually be split into

$$
(\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-x^2}dx)^d
$$

This integral is actually familiar to us, and after squaring, we can transform it to polar coordinates, and the result is $\sqrt \pi$. Then we suddenly get an equation

$$
Area\cdot \Gamma(d/2)/2=\pi^{d/2}
$$

Two algorithms for probability, and we found $Area$ by calculating twice

$$
Area=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}
$$

Quite beautiful, so we also know $Volume$

$$
Volume=\frac{Area}{d}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{d\ \Gamma(d/2)}
$$

It can also be written more cleanly using the recurrence property of the Gamma function

$$
Volume=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}
$$

Let's calculate what a dimension super conference would be like. We consider the limit, so we only need to consider

$$
\frac{\pi^d}{d!}
$$

The factorial is of a higher order, so $Volume$ will tend to 0. It is also an counter-intuitive miracle.

Although I don't know what relation it has with information science, it is quite fun.
