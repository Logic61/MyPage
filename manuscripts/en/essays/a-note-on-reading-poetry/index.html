I’ve been reading a bit of poetry again. It’s been a while.

When I first got to campus, I resolved to read a poem a day and put a copy of the Book of Songs beside my pillow. For two or three months I never opened it. Rather funny, really. I thought I ought to take up poetry again after so long away, but lately things have got busy again, so I read whatever I can in the odd spare moment. Tomato Free Novels was surprisingly decent for this, and so here I am reading poetry again.

Come to think of it, what finally got me started, apart from the feeling that it had been too long, had something to do with Li Xinye. In “Hello World” I mentioned following Ren Yue to New Fields. There I read a few poems he’d recommended. One of them was by Li Qingzhao, to the tune of “Dabbing Crimson Lips”:

She runs off shyly, looks back from the gate,
and lifts a green plum to her nose.

That girl’s lively, endearing spirit fairly leaps from the page, and carries me into a little gladness of my own. One could get all mystical and call it vitality, perhaps. Something about a life so full gives me a sense of fullness too, of beauty, of having had enough. So I started browsing Yi’an’s poems. They came up at once when I searched Tomato, and I began at the beginning. I even read the whole preface properly. It’s an account of her life, and actually worth reading. Then I came to her poem about the Tower of Eight Odes:

“On the Tower of Eight Odes” · Li Qingzhao

Down the ages, the Tower of Eight Odes holds its grace;
these rivers and hills it leaves to those who come after, to grieve over.
The waters run three thousand li through the southern lands;
its breath bears down on fourteen prefectures by the river.

It rolls through you. No wonder people called her the Su Shi and Xin Qiji of the women’s chambers. “These rivers and hills it leaves to those who come after, to grieve over.” I suppose she’d done some grieving herself first, then fallen under the spell of those rivers and hills and forgotten her grief for a moment. To join the affairs of a country to a poem about a tower was, if my rather unreliable memory of the preface serves, a rare thing. But perhaps I don’t care quite so much about that. What strikes me is the force of those last two lines. Some of it is in the sound, I suppose. For want of a better word I can only call it breath. It runs all the way through on one breath, free as clouds and flowing water, and with the weight of a mountain or a river behind it. It kept sounding familiar. Then I remembered reading a line like it before:

Three thousand guests in the hall, drunk on flowers;
the frost of one sword chills fourteen prefectures.

That’s from “Presented to Lord Qian” by the Tang poet Guanxiu. The lines are probably quite well known. I think I came across them in a web novel, or maybe a martial arts novel. Perhaps Yi’an’s lines grew out of these. The numbers, the breath, and the movement of the sound have something in common. Well, clearly that’s where they came from. She kept both three thousand and fourteen. I suppose they were already good.

I might have written a bit more, but there’s a lecture for my ideological and moral education class I have to leave for right now, and I hardly ever take my laptop out with me. It’s heavy. So I’ll put down my pen here for the time being. I still don’t understand the point of some things.
