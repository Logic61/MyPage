Is it a mockery of fate, or an inevitable tragedy driven by free will?

This piece does not contain much supplementary historical fact, so it focuses on the discussion of the plot. If you wish to read this article, it is recommended that you first read the story of Oedipus.

We can consider the following question: Based on the story of Oedipus, how can we give a concise and powerful definition?

Fate

Fate, as a perennial topic, has been discussed countless times. There are quite a few theories, but let’s limit ourselves to the script for now. Generally speaking, the simplest concepts that come to mind are that it was fated so, or it was self-inflicted.

1. The Determinists

The determinists believe that the world is an already compiled .exe file, where all inputs and outputs have been pre-set.

  • Core Logic: Necessity (Portion/Moira). The oracle and the scales determine the share.

  • Manifestation in Oedipus: No matter how hard Oedipus tries (whether it is physically fleeing Corinth, or intellectually solving the mystery), he ultimately falls into the cage set by fate.

  • Essence: Under this view, human beings are merely executors of a logical path.

  • Thought: Its advantage is that it explains the sense of order in the universe, but its disadvantage is that it erases moral responsibility—if everything is predetermined, what fault does Oedipus have?

2. The Free Will Advocates

This school emphasizes the independence of individual choice, believing that humans are not puppets in a script, but real-time generated computational power.

  • Core Logic: The Dignity of Choice (Prohairesis).

  • Manifestation in Oedipus: Note that the oracle only stated, “You will kill your father and marry your mother,” but it did not mandate that Oedipus must investigate the truth, nor did it mandate that he must blind himself.

  • Essence: Oedipus’s choice at the end of the play (self-punishment, accepting responsibility) is completely free. This will does not change the “established fact,” but it rewrites the “meaning of the fact.”

  • Thought: This view grants dignity to humanity, but it seems somewhat powerless against such airtight coincidences, although we could also argue that what Oedipus did was entirely based on free will, which just happened to align with the prophecy. But as a script, such set coincidences are not necessarily unexplainable. However, this leads to an entirely unverifiable state, which cannot be discussed.

3. Possessing Both

Regarding , perhaps we can say this:

Oedipus’s fate is the logical necessity that he completes with his own free will while trying to escape the prophecy.

I am more inclined to believe that the prophecy induced Oedipus to fulfill the prophecy. From this perspective, it is the prophecy itself, rather than any oracle, that determines Oedipus’s fate. If the track were changed, the train would run in a different direction. The prophecy caused Oedipus to leave his adoptive parents, thus killing his father out of road rage, and later becoming king and marrying his mother. If there were no prophecy, we can imagine that it would not have happened; Oedipus would have stayed with his adoptive parents and would not have killed his father and married his mother. But conversely, if we believe in fate, then even if Oedipus did not believe the prophecy and did not leave, other factors would lead him to kill his father and marry his mother, but in today’s materialistic era, such a possibility is probably not very accepted.

Moreover, the prophecy did not create a factual necessity; rather, it was Oedipus’s character that caused everything to happen. It was just a small quarrel on the road, and he was going to kill everyone. If his character were gentle, killing his father would not have happened.

1. The “Self-referentiality” of Action

In the script, is not a passive “event” that happens, but a recursive function.

| Event | Motivation | Result |

|------|------|------|

| Laius abandoning the baby out of fear of prophecy | Escaping prophecy | Oedipus growing up without knowing his real father |

| Oedipus leaving Corinth out of fear of prophecy | Escaping prophecy | Directly leading him down the path to Thebes where he meets his real father |

| Oedipus insisting on finding the real culprit | Pursuit of truth | Self-discovery, self-destruction |

Core Insight: Every “Debug” action of yours is actually running the bug itself. Every attempt to escape fate becomes a step in fulfilling fate.

2. The “Entanglement” of Free Will and Fate

The most ironic thing is that Oedipus is not a puppet on strings. On the contrary, he is extremely intelligent, upright, and decisive.

  • If he were a coward, he would not have bravely solved the Sphinx’s riddle, nor would he have been established as the king of Thebes.

  • If he were mediocre, he would not have had the moral courage to insist on finding the real killer of the former king.

  • If he lacked rationality, he would not have approached the truth step by step through logical deduction.

Key Realization: Fate precisely utilized him as a catalyst. It is precisely because he tried so hard to be a “good person” and an “intellectual” that he perfectly closed that destructive logical loop.

In this sense, Oedipus is not a passive victim, but an active accomplice. His actions are, in a sense, not resisting fate, but executing it.

3. The Observer Effect and the Irony of Prophecy

The prophecy itself is part of fate. This is not mere aesthetic decoration, but the necessary foundation of the entire structure.

  • If there were no Apollo’s oracle, everyone would live their lives in an orderly fashion.

  • It is precisely because the future was “observed” that the characters began to “escape”.

  • And the escape itself becomes the mechanism by which the prophecy is realized.

Therefore, it can be said that:

A prophecy is not merely a statement about the future; it is a self-referential system that alters the trajectory of human action, and this alteration itself leads to the outcome described by the prophecy.

Of course, as mentioned before, it is possible that even without this prophecy, some mysterious hand would have accomplished the whole thing.

We can simply speculate on why the playwright wrote such a piece.

Sophocles’s Creative Intent

I. Revealing the Disjunction between “Knowledge” and “Truth”

Oedipus is a genius at solving the Sphinx’s riddle, representing the highest level of human rationality. But this is precisely where the paradox lies:

He solved the Sphinx’s riddle, yet he could never see through his own identity.

In contrast, the prophet Tiresias, who is blind in both eyes in the play, sees everything, yet is powerless to change anything. This contrast is perhaps the most profound irony in classical drama.

II. The Reshaping and Purification of Order (Catharsis)

Historical Background: Athens in 429 BC

The era when Sophocles created this play was precisely:

  • The early Peloponnesian War (431-404 BC)

  • The outbreak of the Athenian Plague (430 BC), which claimed about a quarter of the population

  • The death of Pericles, shaking the confidence of Athens’ elite class

The play begins by presenting the plague disaster that afflicted Thebes. This is not merely literary imagination, but a realistic metaphor projected from myth onto contemporary Athens.

The Author’s Warning

| Dimension | Pericles/Oedipus | Commonality |

|------|-------------------|------|

| Identity | The highest possessor of power, symbol of rationality | Confidence in one’s own wisdom |

| Turning Point | The plague (the caprice of nature) strikes | Forced to confront forces beyond one’s control |

| Outcome | The rational logic that believes “man can conquer fate” becomes the root of destruction | Rationality itself is not salvation |

Sophocles may be warning the Athenian elite—those who are obsessed with power, obsessed with rationality, and obsessed with their ability to control the course of history. The rational logic you are so proud of, when faced with laws in the universe that transcend human understanding, may very well be the root cause of destruction.

By evoking the audience’s “Pity and Fear,” Sophocles achieves emotional purification (Catharsis). He attempts to remind every citizen of the city-state: Reverence for Order () is the prerequisite for maintaining civil stability.

In Iliad, Achilles chooses the latter—to be famous through death in battle, though unknown in life. However, he is fully aware of the consequences of his choice, whereas Oedipus walks toward tragedy through repeated seemingly free choices.

Oedipus vs. Achilles in the Iliad

Both deal with fate, but the two classical works interpret very differently:

| Dimension | Oedipus Rex | Iliad |

|------|---------------|------------|

| Form of Fate | Logical Closed Loop () | Share System ( as ) |

| External Manifestation | Zeus’s golden scale, determining life and death | Even the gods must submit to such constraints |

| Core Conflict | Intellect vs. Blindness | Glory vs. Death |

| Hero’s Choice | Pursuing the truth without knowing what he is doing | Actively walking toward destruction in a choice of values to gain eternal glory |

| Final Outcome | Blinding himself to achieve spiritual clarity | Dying in battle, leaving an immortal name |

Core Difference:

  • Oedipus is “trying to escape but cannot” — He tries to rewrite the code, only to find himself compiled into an unchangeable Read-only script.

  • Achilles is “knowing he must die, yet willingly walking in” — He possesses the right to choose, but chooses the path leading to destruction.

Oedipus emphasizes the “inevitability of logic,” while Achilles emphasizes the “heroism of will.”

Tiresias: The “Anti-Symmetry” of Truth

The Metaphor of Blindness and Omniscience

In Oedipus Rex, Tiresias presents a contradiction:

  • Physical Level: He cannot see the material world before him (Matter).

  • Information Level: He has global access to the Time Stream.

When Oedipus presses him, he says:

Alas, when clarity brings pain, how terrible clarity is!

Omniscience, for him, only adds to his suffering in vain.

Tiresias knows all the Bugs, but the system rules forbid him from fixing them directly. He can only watch the program crash. His omniscience is accompanied by powerlessness—this powerlessness is the essence of his tragedy.

Two Dialogues of “Blindness”

| Feature | Tiresias | Oedipus |

|------|-----------|---------|

| Visual State | Physically blind, spiritually omniscient | Physically bright, spiritually blind |

| Thinking Model | Experience and Revelation (Top-down) | Logic and Deduction (Bottom-up) |

| Attitude towards Fate | Compliance and Awe | Challenge and Revision |

| Ultimate Gain | Maintaining Silence and Clarity | Blinding the Eyes, Gaining Clarity |

Profound Meaning: Oedipus undergoes an “evolution” in the end when confronting Tiresias. He realizes that seeing the world with mere physical eyes only leads to misjudgment, so he chooses Tiresias’s path—destroying physical sight to gain spiritual truth.

Conclusion

No matter what, Oedipus truly fell into the web woven by fate. Under the combined influence of prophecy and his own character, he committed patricide and incest. He was both the “greatest man” in the world and the stain spoken of by the gods.

But Oedipus chose to face the truth rather than escape it. This is the most shining aspect of humanity in the entire tragedy.
