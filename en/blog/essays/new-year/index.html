    New Year

    Has the savor of the world grown thin as gauze these past few years?

    I don’t know how long it’s been since I last watched the Spring Festival Gala. Six or seven years, probably. New Year still seemed full of things to enjoy back then. It hasn’t really changed much since. There are still fireworks all over the sky, and the fireworks are still beautiful. The firecrackers still go on and on and you still feel them in your chest, but somehow it doesn’t feel right.

    Well, then it must be the people who’ve changed.

    During the New Year, although fireworks and firecrackers are very representative symbols, perhaps they are not the main point. The main point might still be the people. When I was little, I had a big group of playmates, and there were countless things to play with. We could play with fireworks all day. A few of us would set up a firecracker array, assign one person to light them, and the rest would hide far away to watch. The person lighting them would run back quickly after striking a match, and after running far enough, the firecrackers would burst with a crackling sound. This scene occupied most of my heart. Even without fireworks, there were always games to play—climbing and jumping down small slopes, playing all sorts of small games, playing cards, and sometimes a group of people watching one person play a game. But no matter what, everyone seemed connected. Now? Suddenly, I feel disconnected from these people. Since returning from a certain year, I’ve been distant, suddenly feeling awkward, and I don’t even know why. By the time I got to high school, the connection was almost completely severed. Back then, there were no mobile phones, and they would run to my hometown every day to ask when I was coming back. When I returned, I followed them every day. By the time I had a mobile phone and added QQ, we hadn’t exchanged a single word. It’s really strange. As I grew up, my childhood playmates gradually drifted apart, and so did my relatives. So, even though I was in a family setting, I couldn’t feel any spiritual connection. Even though everyone was quite nice to me, treating me to food, fun, and entertainment, I just couldn’t get interested. The fireworks I used to love watching were boring after just one minute. Is it that the fireworks aren’t beautiful? No, it’s not that. This makes me feel a void that I don’t know how to repair. In the past, even just having an online friend to accompany me, I could happily watch the fireworks and spend the New Year happily.

    However, no one spent New Year’s Eve with me, so I could only find ethereal comfort in my imagination. Maybe I’ve seen too many novels and games, and I’ve seen this kind of virtual possibility.

    If I were to be under a sea of fireworks, I would know that the sound of the fireworks is so loud that it can almost erase most other sounds. Normal conversation is completely inaudible, unless you shout. Apart from the explosion sounds, it’s almost a silent world, but it’s completely different. The explosion sounds create a base that is enough to make anyone’s heart swell. If one wasn’t lonely, this swell could continue. Most houses in the countryside are detached. Just by working with my legs, I can get to the rooftop and take in the entire sea of fireworks nearby.

    Fireworks festivals are almost a must-see scene in romance dramas. Under the magnificent fireworks, people’s hearts are often more open. Although I didn’t see the noisy fireworks depicted in the drama, I think it’s a nice setting. Let’s imagine it on the rooftop of a house, or in some open area at a fireworks festival. A young couple leaning on the railing, their two bright pairs of eyes sparkling with the splendor of the fireworks. At some moment, the man gradually turns his head towards the woman, and the woman glances over, then turns her head so their eyes meet. In the moment their eyes meet, a smile naturally creeps onto both their lips.

    How beautiful! The annual fireworks show, so wonderful.

    But the woman can’t hear, so she has to shout

    I can’t hear! What did you say?

    The man hears it, but he smiles and still says to the woman in a normal voice:

    I like you

    I can’t hear! Speak up, you fool!

    But the man just smiles and has no intention of repeating his words.

    Say it, say it louder!

    The man grabs the woman’s hand and, while she is still a bit dazed, looks seriously into her eyes, and shouts those four words loudly—

    I like you!

    At this moment, a firework doesn’t happen to bloom behind them, because at all times, their surroundings are filled with blooming fireworks.

    Though just that is a little dull, after all. There ought to be some hair-raising adventure first. The two of them narrowly escape some danger, and once the crisis is over you put this scene in as the ending. That would be enough, of course, but you could go on. One of them has to leave for reasons beyond their control and keeps it from the other, who finds out by chance. In the end there’s no slipping away. They have to say goodbye, and it hurts. Better sprinkle a little sweetness into the adventure and the discovery too. Flit back and forth between sweet and painful and you can probably get quite a lot out of your readers. Tie the adventure and the parting to some loftier ideal, and there’s your depth. Yes, I’m talking about you, Kuro 😡

    Though Rover may lose one Aemeath and still have millions more.

    In the end I watched the fireworks for a few minutes, went back inside, and played games online with my classmates. That might have been the most fun I had all New Year.

    I asked AI for suggestions and it went ahead and rewrote the whole thing. On the whole it does seem better than what I wrote (I can’t be bothered to dig into much of anything, but the AI can. Not bad. It’s been a while since I wrote anything a little more serious. There hasn’t been much reason to, and so I suppose I’ve forgotten how.

    New Year

    Does the taste of life feel as thin as gauze year after year.

    It’s been many years since I watched the Spring Festival Gala. Fireworks are still set off every year, the firelight illuminates half the sky, and the sound of firecrackers rolls down from the eaves, making people’s chests feel slightly tight. They haven’t changed—what is bright is still bright, and what is loud is still loud. It’s just that I always feel something is wrong.

    If I eliminate that, it can only be that people have changed.

    The New Year as a child was filled with human voices. A group of children from the afternoon until night, carrying firecrackers in their pockets, kicking up dust on the dirt road. A few of us would set up an array, nominate one person to light them, and that person would run away wildly as soon as they lit the match, while the rest waited breathlessly in the distance. After a moment, they burst with a crackling sound, and the sparks flew up, and our laughter was more scattered than the fireworks.

    It’s okay without fireworks. Down the slope and up the slope, board games, watching someone play a game—as long as we are together, time automatically burns away.

    The connection back then didn’t need to be maintained. As long as we walked out the door, each other was there.

    Later, one year I returned and suddenly felt estranged. There were no arguments, no misunderstandings, just a gradual drifting apart. Later, in high school, contact was almost completely severed. When I was little, I didn’t have a mobile phone, and they would come asking when I was coming back; by the time I had a mobile phone and added QQ, we hadn’t exchanged a single word.

    How strange.

    So, the New Year was still lively, but I felt as if I were separated by glass. Relatives still treated me to food and fun, and laughter floated in the house, but I couldn’t get interested. The fireworks curled in the sky, and the firework splendor covered the night sky. After watching for a minute, I felt bored. It’s not that the fireworks aren’t beautiful—it’s just that no one is looking up with me.

    I later understood something.

    If I were truly standing under a sea of fireworks, I would know that the roar can almost erase all sounds. Normal speech is inaudible; only the layered explosion sounds press down, like a huge base plate. When people are in it, their hearts will swell, and they will swell unconsciously. If there are people beside you, the swell can continue; if not, after the sound wave dissipates, only a clearer emptiness remains.

    Most houses in the countryside are detached. Climbing to the rooftop, it only takes a few steps to take in the fireworks of the surrounding area. All the lights of the homes, set off in all directions, as if only light and sound remain in the world.

    Such a scene is most suitable for a confession.

    So I often imagine—on the rooftop, by the railing, at a time when the fireworks fill the night sky. Two people standing side by side, the firelight sparkling in their eyes. At a certain moment, the man turns his head, and the woman happens to glance over. Their eyes meet, and a smile floats up before words.

    “How beautiful.”

    She can’t hear, so she has to shout: “What did you say?”

    He clearly knows she can’t hear, but still says in a normal voice:

    “I like you.”

    “I can’t hear! Speak up!”

    He still smiles.

    Until finally grabbing her hand and shouting those four words forcefully amidst the sky full of explosions. The sound is swallowed, yet it seems to penetrate everything.

    The fireworks won’t bloom exactly at that moment—because at all times, they are blooming.

    I know this plot point is too cliché. Maybe I should add some suspense beforehand, have them embrace after danger; or arrange an unavoidable parting afterward, to make the confession heavier. Only when sweet and pain intertwine will the readers be moved.

    But reality has no screenwriter.

    In reality, I watched the fireworks for a few minutes, felt my ears ringing, so I went back inside and opened my computer to play games online with classmates. The light on the screen felt closer than the light in the sky, and the sound in my headphones was clear, not muffled by the explosions.

    At that moment, I truly felt joy.

    Perhaps the meaning of the New Year has not disappeared. It’s just that I no longer look for it under the fireworks.

    The sound waves are still rolling across the sky, and I sit inside, confronting the world across the wall.

    Firecrackers shake the heavens, the house is quiet.

    The New Year has arrived.
