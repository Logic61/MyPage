    §1 — Verse 1

    [English quotation] But the queen, long since smitten with a grievous love-pang, feeds the wound with her lifeblood, and is wasted with fire unseen. Oft to her mind rushes back the hero’s valour, oft his glorious stock; his looks and words cling fast to her bosom, and longing withholds calm rest from her limbs.

    Queen Dido was already tormented by a violent love-sickness, feeding her wounds with blood, gradually withering in an invisible flame. The hero’s valor, his noble bloodline, repeatedly flooded her mind; his appearance and smile tightly gripped her heart, craving to rob her of any moment’s peace.

    [Notes]

    • grievous love-pang: Intense love pain—a metaphor in classical literature for amor ut morbus (love as disease).

    • fire unseen: The invisible fire, alluding to the love-fire (secretum ignem), the passion in Dido’s heart consuming her in secret.

    §6 — Verse 6

    [English quotation] The morrow’s dawn was lighting the earth with the lamp of Phoebus, and had scattered from the sky the dewy shades, when, much distraught, she thus speaks to her sister, sharer of her heart: “Anna, my sister, what dreams thrill me with fears? Who is this stranger guest who has entered our home? How noble his mien! How brave in heart and feats of arms! I believe it well – nor is my confidence vain – that he is sprung from gods. It is fear that proves souls base-born. Alas! by what fates is he vexed! What wars, long endured, did he recount! Were the purpose not planted in my mind, fixed and immovable, to ally myself with none in bond of wedlock, since my first love, turning traitor, cheated me by death; were I not tired of the bridal bed and torch, to this one fault, perhaps, I might have yielded! Anna – for I will own it– since the death of my hapless lord Sychaeus, and the shattering of our home by a brother’s murder, he alone has swayed my will and overthrown my tottering soul. I feel again a spark of that former flame. But rather, I would pray, may earth yawn for me to its depths, or may the Almighty Father hurl me with his bolt to the shades – the pale shades and abysmal night in Erebus – before, Shame, I violate you or break your laws! He who first linked me to himself has taken away my heart; may he keep it with him, and guard it in the grave!” So saying, she filled her breast with upwelling tears.

    The next dawn, the lamp of Phoebus illuminated the earth, dispelling the dew mist in the sky. At this time, Dido, whose mind was in turmoil, said to her trusted sister: “Anna, my sister, what dream frightens and makes me tremble? Who is this strange guest who has entered our home? How noble is his bearing! How brave is his heart and skill! I believe—my confidence will not be disappointed—he is a descendant of the gods. Only cowardice makes one of low birth. Alas! What fate torments him! How many battles has he endured for so long! If I had not established a firm and unwavering purpose in my heart—the bond of never marrying anyone after my first love was betrayed and killed—if I were not weary of the marriage bed and the wedding candle, perhaps I would succumb to this lapse! Anna—I must confess—since my unfortunate husband Sychaeus died, since the murder of my brother destroyed my home, only he has guided my will, overturning my wavering soul. I feel the spark of that old flame again. But I would rather pray for a chasm to split open beneath my feet, or for the almighty father to strike me into the underworld—the pale, dark abyss of Erebus—than violate your law before you, Goddess of Shame! He first stole my heart, so let him take my heart and guard it in the grave!” As she spoke, tears welled up in her chest.

    [Notes]

    • Phoebus: An epithet for Apollo, meaning “the bright one”.

    • Sychaeus: Dido’s first husband, murdered by his brother Pygmalion. See anecdote #1.

    • Erebus: The underworld in Greek mythology, a dark place.

    • Shame: (Pudicitia), the Roman goddess of virtue. Dido emphasizes her vow of chastity to her deceased husband.

    Note: Dido falls in love.

    §31 — Verse 31

    [English quotation] Anna replies: “O you who are dearer to your sister than the light, are you, lonely and sad, going to pine away all your youth long, and know not sweet children or love’s rewards? Do you think that dust or buried shades give heed to that? Grant that until now no wooers moved your sorrow, not in Libya, not before then in Tyre; that Iarbas was slighted, and other lords whom the African land, rich in triumphs, rears; will you wrestle also with a love that pleases? And does it not come to your mind whose lands you have settled in? On this side Gaetulian cities, a race invincible in war, unbridled Numidians, and the unfriendly Syrtis hem you in; on that side lies a tract barren with drought, and Barcaeans, raging far and wide. Why speak of the wars rising from Tyre, your brother’s threats … ? I certainly believe that it was with the gods’ favour and Juno’s aid that the Ilian ships held their course hither with the wind. What a city you will see rise here, my sister, what a realm, by reason of such a marriage! With Teucrian arms beside us, to what heights will Punic glory soar? Only ask favour of the gods and, with sacrifice duly offered, be lavish with your welcome, and weave pleas for delay, while at sea winter rages fiercely and Orion is stormy – while the ships are shattered, and the skies intractable!”

    Anna replied: “Ah, you are more precious than your sister’s life; are you going to waste your youth in lonely sorrow, unaware of the sweetness of children’s joy, or the reward of love? Do you think dust and the dead buried underground will care for these things? Even if no suitor moves your sorrow to day—whether in Libya or in Tyre—even if you refuse Iarbas, and refuse the other lords who have nurtured this rich land of Africa; must you also refuse a love that moves the heart? Can you not think of the land where you settled? Here is the city of the Gaetuli—a people of unconquered might, and the unruly Numidians, and the unfriendly Syrtis surround you; there is the dry, barren land and the roaming Barcaeans. Why speak of the battles of Tyre and your brother’s threat… I deeply believe that it is the grace of the gods and the help of Juno that the ships of Ilion came here by the wind. Sister, you will see a city rise here, a kingdom born of this marriage! With the military alliance of the Gaetuli, how high will the glory of Carthage rise? Just pray to the gods, offer the proper sacrifice, receive them generously, and weave reasons for delay when the winter rages on the sea, and when Orion brings storms—when ships break and the sky is unpredictable!”

    [Notes]

    • Iarbas: A tribal chief of North Africa who once proposed to Dido and was rejected. See anecdote #7.

    • Gaetulian / Numidian / Syrtis / Barcaeans: All are geographical names in North Africa. Carthage is surrounded on all sides—to the west are wild tribes, to the east is the desert.

    • Punic: Of Carthage, referring to the Carthaginians (Phoenician colonies). The “Punic Wars” are the wars between Rome and Carthage.

    • Orion: The constellation of Orion, a winter constellation, marking the stormy season.

    Question for thought: In what aspects does Anna analyze the situation for her sister?

    §54 — Verse 54

    [English quotation] With these words she fanned into flame the queen’s love-enkindled heart, put hope in her wavering mind, and loosed the bonds of shame. First they visit the shrines and sue for peace at every altar; duly they slay chosen sheep to Ceres the law-giver, to Phoebus and father Lyaeus, above all to Juno, guardian of the bonds of marriage. Dido herself, matchless in beauty, with cup in hand, pours libation midway between the horns of a white heifer, or in presence of the gods moves slowly to the rich altars, and day by day renews her gifts, then, gazing into the opened breasts of victims, consults the quivering entrails. Ah, the blind souls of seers! Of what avail are vows or shrines to one wild with love? All the while the flame devours her tender heartstrings, and deep in her breast lives the silent wound. Unhappy Dido burns, and through the city wanders in frenzy – even as a hind, smitten by an arrow, which, all unwary, amid the Cretan woods, a shepherd hunting with darts has pierced from afar, leaving in her the winged steel, unknowing: she in flight ranges the Dictaean woods and glades, but fast to her side clings the deadly shaft. Now through the city’s midst she leads Aeneas with her, and displays her Sidonian wealth and the city built; she begins to speak and stops with the word half-spoken. Now, as day wanes, she seeks that same banquet, again in her madness craves to hear the sorrows of Ilium and again hangs on the speaker’s lips. Then when all have gone their ways, and in turn the dim moon sinks her light, and the setting stars invite sleep, alone she mourns in the empty hall, and falls on the couch he has left. Though absent, each from each, she hears him, she sees him, or, captivated by his look of his father, she holds Ascanius on her lap, in case she may beguile a passion beyond all utterance. No longer rise the towers begun, no longer do the youth exercise in arms, or toil at havens or bulwarks for safety in war; the works are broken off and idle – great menacing walls and cranes that touch the sky. Note the metaphor in this passage; what is its meaning? Which lines resonate with you the most?

    [From the Chinese translation] These words ignited the love that had already begun to bloom in the queen’s heart, sowing hope in her wavering soul and releasing the shackles of shame. They first visited the temple, praying for peace before each altar; they offered lambs to the legislator Ceres, Phobos, and the father of the wine god, as required, especially to Juno, the guardian of marriage. Dido herself, incomparably beautiful, held a cup and poured libations between the white horns of the cow, or slowly walked toward the magnificent altar in the presence of the gods, renewing her offerings day after day, then gazing at the exposed chest of the sacrifice, observing the trembling entrails. Ah, the soul of the blind prophet! What use are vows and temples to a person consumed by love? Meanwhile, flames consume her tender heartstrings, and silent wounds are hidden deep in her heart. Unfortunate Dido burns, wandering madly in the city—like a doe struck by an arrow, unprepared, in the woods of Crete, shot from a distance by a shepherd hunting with javelins, leaving behind an iron arrow with wings, and she is completely unaware: she crosses the woods and open spaces of Dictae in her flight, but the fatal arrow clings to her body. Now she passes through the city with Aeneas, displaying her Cyrenean wealth and the city she has built; she begins to speak, but stops halfway. As the daylight fades, she searches again at the banquet, again yearning in her madness to hear the song of Ilium, again hanging on the lips of the speaker. Then, when everyone has left, as the moon lowers its light and the starlight invites sleep, she mourns alone in the hall, collapsing onto the sofa he left behind. Although separated, she hears him, sees him, or is drawn by the face of his father; she holds Ascanius on her lap, lest she be deceived by an inexpressible passion. The tower begun no longer rises, the young men no longer practice their arms, nor do they labor for the harbor or the fortress in war; construction has ceased—the great walls and the cranes that reach the sky are idle.

    [Notes]

    • Ceres: The goddess of agriculture and law.

    • Lyaeus: An epithet of Dionysus, the god of wine.

    • libation: The act of pouring liquid in ancient religious rituals to the gods.

    • entrails: The internal organs. Ancient diviners predicted the future by observing the entrails of sacrifices.

    • hind: Doe. This metaphor suggests Dido is like a wounded prey, beautiful and fragile, destined to escape.

    • Dictaean: Of Dictae, a sacred mountain on Crete.

    • Sidonian: Of Sidon, a Phoenician city, the motherland of Carthage.

    • Ascanius: Ascanius, son of Aeneas, later named Iulus, the ancestor of the Roman Julian family.

    §90 — Verse 90

    [English quotation] Soon as the loved wife of Jove saw that Dido was held in a passion so fatal, and that her good name was now no bar to her frenzy, the daughter of Saturn accosts Venus thus: “Splendid indeed is the praise and rich the spoils you win, you and your boy; mighty and glorious is the power divine, if one woman is subdued by the guile of two gods! Nay, it escapes me not how, in fear of our city, you have held in suspicion the homes of high Carthage. But what shall be the end? And what is the point of all this contest now? Why do we not rather strive for an enduring peace and a plighted wedlock? What you sought with all your heart you have; Dido is on fire with love and has drawn the madness through her veins. Let us then rule this people jointly with equal sovereignty; let her serve a Phrygian husband and yield her Tyrians to your power as dowry!”

    [From the Chinese translation] Juno, the wife beloved by Jupiter, soon discovered that Dido was controlled by a fatal passion, and that her good reputation was no longer a crazy restraint. The daughter of Saturn thus said to Venus: “What glorious praise and rich spoils you and your son have won; how mighty and glorious is the power when a woman is conquered by the schemes of two goddesses! I have not forgotten, also, that out of fear for our city (Rome), you harbor doubts about the lofty Carthage. But how will all this end? What is the meaning of this struggle? Why do we not strive for lasting peace and certain marriage? You have attained all you desire; Dido burns for love, and madness has flowed into her veins. Then let us rule this people together, with equal power; let her serve a Phrygian husband, and let her Tyrian people be given to you as a dowry!”

    [Notes]

    • wife of Jove / daughter of Saturn: Both refer to Juno (Hera). She is Jupiter’s sister and wife, and Saturn’s daughter.

    • guile: Deceit. Juno here mocks Venus for using the schemes of two goddesses to conquer a woman.

    • Phrygian: Of Phrygia, referring to the Trojans (Phrygia is a region in Asia Minor).

    §105 — Verse 105

    [English quotation] To her – for she knew that with feigned purpose she had spoken, to turn the empire from Italy to Libya’s shores – Venus thus began in reply: “Who so mad as to refuse such terms, or prefer to strive against you in war, as long as Fortune favour the fulfilment of your word? But the Fates send me adrift, uncertain whether Jupiter wills that there be one city for the Tyrians and the wanderers from Troy, or approves the blending of peoples and the league of union. You are his wife; it is lawful for you to try to persuade his heart with entreaty. Go on; I will follow!” Then queenly Juno thus replied: “With me shall rest that task. Now in what way the present purpose can be achieved, hearken and I will explain in brief. Aeneas and unhappy Dido plan to go hunting together in the forest, as soon as tomorrow’s sun shows his rising and with his rays unveils the world. On them, while the hunters run to and fro and gird the glades with nets, I will pour down from above a black rain mingled with hail, and wake the whole welkin with thunder. The company shall scatter and be veiled in gloom of night; to the same cave shall come Dido and the Trojan chief. I will be there and, if I can be sure of your good will, will link them in sure wedlock, sealing her for his own; this shall be their bridal!” Yielding to her suit, the Cytherean gave assent and smiled at the guile discovered. Juno and Venus plot together.

    [From the Chinese translation] Venus knows that Juno is speaking falsely—the purpose is to shift the empire from Italy to the Libyan coast—so she replies: “Who is mad to refuse such terms, or rather fight with you in war, as long as fate supports the realization of your words? But fate has made me restless, and I am uncertain whether Jupiter will allow the Tyrians and Trojan wanderers to become residents of a city, or whether he will approve the fusion and union of the people. You are his wife; you have the right to try to persuade his heart with entreaties. Go; I will follow!” Then the noble Juno replies: “This task is mine. Now, what is the way to achieve the current purpose? Listen to me, I will explain briefly. Aeneas and the unfortunate Dido plan to hunt together in the forest, at dawn tomorrow, when his light reveals the world. When the hunters run about, enclosing an open space in the woods, I shall pour down a black rain mixed with hail from above, and awaken the whole sky with thunder. The crowd will disperse, shrouded in the gloom of night; Dido and the Trojan leaders will enter the same cave. I will be there, and if I can ascertain your goodwill, I will bind them firmly in marriage, making her forever his; this will be their wedding!” Venus agrees to her request, the Cythereans gladly consent, and smile slightly at the exposed trick.

    [Notes]

    • Cytherean: Of Cythera, an epithet for Venus (named after the mountain Kythira on the island of Cyprus, her birthplace).

    • Juno’s plot: To use a storm to force the two into the same cave, thereby creating the fait accompli of a marriage. This is one of the most famous schemes in classical literature.

    §129 — Verse 129

    [English quotation] Meanwhile Dawn rose and left the ocean. When sunlight has burst forth, there issues from the gates a chosen band of youth; with meshed nets, toils, broad-pointed hunting spears, there stream forth Massylian horsemen and their strong, keen-scented hounds. As the queen lingers in her bower, the Punic princes await her at the doorway; her prancing steed stands brilliant in purple and gold, and proudly champs the foaming bit. At last she comes forth, attended by a mighty throng, and clad in a Sidonian robe with embroidered border. Her quiver is of gold, her tresses are knotted into gold, a buckle of gold clasps her purple cloak. With her pace a Phrygian train and joyous Iulus. Aeneas himself, goodly beyond all others, advances to join her and unites his band with hers. As when Apollo quits Lycia, his winter home, and the streams of Xanthus, to visit his mother’s Delos, and renews the dance, while mingling about his altars Cretans and Dryopes and painted Agathyrsians raise their voices – he himself treads the Cynthian ridges, and with soft foliage shapes and binds his flowing locks, braiding it with golden diadem; the shafts rattle on his shoulders: so no less lightly than he went Aeneas, such beauty shines forth from his noble face! When they came to the mountain heights and pathless lairs, wild goats dislodged from the rocky peaks ran down the ridges; in another part stags scurry across the open moors and amid clouds of dust mass their bands in flight, as they leave the hills behind. But in the midst of the valleys the young Ascanius glories in his fiery steed, galloping past now these, now those, and prays that amid the timorous herds a foaming boar may be granted to his vows or a tawny lion come down from the mountain. Dawn arrives, and people in fine clothes enter the forest to hunt.

    [From the Chinese translation] Dawn rises, leaving the sea. As the sunlight shines down, a select group of young men emerges from the city gates; the cavalry of Massalia, with net-like fishing nets, hunting gear, and pointed spears, and their sharp hounds, rush out. The queen still wanders in her chambers, and the Phoenician nobles wait for her at the entrance; her horses are adorned with purple-gold trappings, proudly chewing on the flying bits. Finally, accompanied by a large retinue, she emerges, wearing a Sidonian robe embroidered with borders. Her quiver is gold, her hair is bound with gold threads, and her purple cloak is fastened with gold clasps. With her are the Phrygian attendants and the joyful Iulus. Aeneas himself, the most outstanding among all, steps forward to meet her, joining his company with hers. Like Apollo leaving Lycia—his winter home—and the stream of the Xanthos River, to visit the temple on Delos to his mother, to renew the dance—and at his altar, the Cretans, the Drupians, and the painted Agatireans sing in unison—he himself treads upon the ridge of Mount Xanthus, smoothing and binding his flowing hair with tender branches, and adorning it with a golden crown; his quiver rattles on his shoulder: he walks so lightly, and such beauty shines on his noble face! When they reach the mountaintop and the lair of wild beasts, the wild goats startled from the rocky peaks descend along the ridge; elsewhere, the stags run in the open plain, gathering into a fleeing group in the dust, casting the mountains far behind. But in the center of the valley, the young Ascanius gallops proudly on his spirited horse, passing between these and those, praying for a wild boar foaming at the mouth among the timid herd as a response to his vow, or a golden lion descending from the mountain.

    [Notes]

    • Massylian: A resident of the Massalian kingdom in North Africa.

    • Sidonian robe: A robe from Sidon. Sidon is the motherland of Phoenicia, and the world-famous source of purple dye (Tyrian purple).

    • Apollo / Lycia / Delos / Xanthus / Cynthian: All refer to Apollo. Lycia is his winter sanctuary, Delos is his birthplace, the Xanthos River flows through Lycia, and Mount Cynthus is his sanctuary on Cythera. This description portrays Aeneas as being like the sun god, highly praising his appearance.

    • Ascanius / Iulus: Ascanius, later called Iulus, the ancestor of the Julian family.

    §160 — Verse 160

    [English quotation] Meanwhile in the sky begins the turmoil of a wild uproar; rain follows, mingled with hail. The scattered Tyrian train and the Trojan youth, with the Dardan grandson of Venus, in their fear seek shelter here and there over the fields; torrents rush down from the heights. To the same cave come Dido and the Trojan chief. Primal Earth and nuptial Juno give the sign; fires flashed in Heaven, the witness to their bridal, and on the mountaintop screamed the Nymphs. That day the first of death, the first of calamity was cause. For no more is Dido swayed by fair show or fair fame, no more does she dream of a secret love: she calls it marriage and with that name veils her sin. As Juno planned, it begins to rain in the sky, mixed with hail. This day witnesses Dido’s marriage, and also the beginning of Dido’s misfortune.

    At this moment, a wild chaos began in the sky; a downpour followed, mixed with hailstones. The scattered armies of Tyre and the Trojan youth, along with the descendants of Venus’s grandson Dardaneus, fled in fear to various fields to take shelter; the torrent poured down from high places. Dido and the Trojan leaders entered the same cave. Primal Earth and the goddess Juno sent out signals; flames ignited in the sky as witnesses to their wedding, and the nymphs on the mountaintops cried out. On that day, the first day of death, the root of disaster began.

    [Notes]

    • Primal Earth: The original earth (Terra/Gaia), one of the earliest deities born in mythology.

    • nuptial Juno: Juno, the goddess of marriage. Here it alludes to Juno’s aspect as the guardian of marriage.

    • Nymphs: Nymphs. Screaming on the mountaintops, suggesting that this marriage violates the natural order and is destined for misfortune.

    §198 — Verse 198

    [English quotation] He, the son of Hammon by a ravished Garamantian Nymph, set up to Jupiter in his broad realms a hundred vast temples, a hundred altars, and had hallowed the wakeful fire, the eternal sentry of the gods. The ground was fat with the blood of beasts and the portals bloomed with varied garlands. Distraught in mind and fired with the bitter tale, they say, before the altars and amid the divine presences he often besought Jove in prayer with upturned hands: “Almighty Jupiter, to whom now the Moorish race, feasting on embroidered couches, pour a Lenaean offering, do you see these things? Is it vainly, father, that we shudder at you, when you hurl your thunderbolts? And do aimless fires amid the clouds terrify our souls and stir murmurs void of purpose? This woman who, straying in our bounds, set up a tiny city at a price, to whom we gave coastland to plough and terms of tenure, has spurned my offers of marriage, and welcomed Aeneas into her realm as lord. And now that Paris with his eunuch train, his chin and perfumed locks bound with a Lydian turban, grasps the spoil; while we bring offerings to your temples, yours forsooth, and cherish an idle story.”King Aelbas questions Jupiter as to why he allowed the love between Dido and Aeneas.

    He, Hammon god and son of a captured Maëan nymph, has built hundreds of magnificent temples and hundreds of altars for Jupiter in the vast domain of Hammon, and consecrated the eternal sentinel fire—the never-extinguishing fire of the gods’ watch. The land is covered with the blood of livestock, and the doors are adorned with all kinds of wreaths. Aelbas, distraught and ignited by this bitter news, is said to often raise his hands before the altar, in the divine presence, praying to Jupiter: “All-powerful Jupiter, now the Moorish race sits on the embroidered sofa, offering sacrifices to the god of wine to you. Have you seen these things? Father, are our trembling before you in vain? Are our trembling when you throw down lightning in vain? Does the aimless fire in the clouds frighten our souls, stirring up meaningless rumors? This woman, wandering on our border, establishing a small city with a little cost. We gave her the coast to cultivate and the right to dwell, yet she refused my proposal, bringing Aeneas into her land as her master. Now Paris, with his eunuch attendants, his chin, and his perfumed hair wrapped in a Libyan headcloth, controls the spoils; and we offer sacrifices to your temple—your temple—guarding an empty story.”

    [Notes]

    • Hammon: Hammon, a local god of Zeus worship in North Africa. Aelbas calls himself the Hammon god and son of a nymph, suggesting his divine lineage. See anecdote #7.

    • Lenaean: Of the god of wine (named after the god of wine Dionysus, Lenaean).

    • Paris: Paris, the Trojan prince, who caused the Trojan War by stealing Helen. Here Aelbas implicitly compares Aeneas to Paris—a man who plunders another’s land.

    • Aelbas questions Jupiter: See anecdote #7. Aelbas is a local ruler of North Africa, and his grievances represent the dissatisfaction of the peoples around Carthage towards the Phoenician colonists.

    §219 — Verse 219

    [English quotation] As with such words he pleaded, clasping the altars, the Almighty gave ear and turned his eyes on the royal city and the lovers forgetful of their nobler fame. Then thus to Mercury he speaks and gives this charge: “Go forth, my son, call the Zephyrs, glide on they wings, and speak to the Dardan chief, who now at Carthage is looking forward to Tyrian cities, unmindful of those granted him by the Fates; so carry down my words through the swift winds. Not such as this did his lovely mother promise him to us, nor for this twice rescue him from Grecian arms; but he it was who should rule Italy, a land teeming with empire and clamorous with war, hand on a race from Teucer’s noble blood, and bring all the world beneath his laws. If the glory of such a fortune fires him not and for his own fame’s sake he shoulders not the burden, does he, the father, grudge Ascanius the towers of Rome? What is his plan? In what hope does he tarry among a hostile people and pays no heed to Ausonia’s race and the Lavinian fields? Let him set sail; this is the sum; be this the message from me. Zeus issues the command.

    Saying this, holding the altar, the all-powerful god listens, turning his gaze towards this royal city and the lover who forgot greater glory. So he says to Mercury and issues this command: “Go, my son, summon the West Wind, gliding on your wings, to speak to the leader of Dardaneus, who now awaits the city of the Tyrians in Carthage, forgetting what fate has given him; so carry my words down with the swift wind. His beautiful mother did not promise us such an ending, nor did she save him twice from the hands of the Greeks; but he should rule Italy—a land full of imperial and war clamor, a race descended from the noble blood of Teucros, and place the whole world under his law. If this glorious destiny has not ignited him, if he does not bear the heavy burden for his own glory, would his father be stingy with the towers of Ascanius in Rome? What is his plan? In what hope does he linger among an enemy people, disregarding the race of Ausonia and the fields of Lavinium? Let him set sail; this is all; this is my message.”

    [Notes]

    • Zephyrs: The West Wind. Mercury, as a divine messenger, harnesses the wind to deliver Jupiter’s command.

    • Ascanius: Ascanius, Aeneas’s son, the true founder of the city of Rome (through his descendants, Romulus).

    • Ausonia / Lavinian fields: The fields of Ausonia and Lavinium, both referring to southern Italy. This is Jupiter’s reaffirmation of Aeneas’s mission.

    • Zeus issues the command: Original Chinese annotation. Jupiter represents fate (fatum), the supreme force driving the plot throughout the epic.

    §238 — Verse 238

    [English quotation] He ceased. The god made ready to obey his mighty father’s bidding, and first binds on his feet the golden shoes which carry him upborne on wings over seas or land, swift as the gale. Then he takes his wand; with this he calls pale ghosts from Orcus and sends others down to gloomy Tartarus, gives or takes away sleep and unseals eyes in death; relying on this, he drives the winds and skims the stormy clouds. And now in flight he descries the peak and steep sides of toiling Atlas, who props heaven on his peak – Atlas, whose pine-wreathed head is ever girt with black clouds, and beaten with wind and rain; fallen snow mantles his shoulders while rivers plunge down the aged chin and his rough beard is stiff with ice.     Afterword: Themes and Structure of Book IV of the Aeneid

    Theme

    1. Duty vs. Love: The core conflict of the entire book. Pietas (duty to the gods, homeland, and family) represented by Aeneas is opposed to amor (love/passion) represented by Dido. Virgil sympathizes with Dido, but ultimately allows fate to prevail.

    2. Fatum / Fate: Jupiter is the enforcer of fate, and Mercury is the messenger of fate. Individual will cannot defy fate—no matter how much Aeneas loves Dido, he cannot change his mission to found Rome.

    3. Female Passion vs. Male Reason: Dido is portrayed as a woman completely consumed by passion, while Aeneas represents restrained reason. However, Virgil’s sympathy clearly leans toward Dido—her humanity is more real than Aeneas’s “perfect hero image”.

    4. Political Foreshadowing: Dido’s curse foreshadows the later Punic Wars (the generational hatred between Rome and Carthage), and Hannibal’s invasion.

    Structure

    • Lines 1-143: Dido falls in love, Anna’s persuasion

    • Lines 144-275: Juno and Venus conspire, the “wedding” in the storm-swept cave

    • Lines 276-383: Rumors spread, Arbalaes’ complaint

    • Lines 384-505: Jupiter sends Mercury, Aeneas is forced to decide to leave

    • Lines 506-650: Confrontation between Dido and Aeneas, Dido’s curse

    • Lines 651-705: Dido builds the pyre, immolates herself in love

    • Lines 706-758: Anna’s grief, Iris releases Dido’s soul

    Literary Influence

    Book IV has a profound influence on later literature:

    • Dante’s Divine Comedy places Dido in the second circle of Hell (the lustful)

    • Geoffrey Chaucer quotes the story of Dido

    • Monteverdi’s opera Dido and Aeneas and Purcell’s opera Carmen were both inspired by Dido’s tragedy

    • The story of Dido is one of the sources for the prototype of the “abandoned heroine” in Western literature.
