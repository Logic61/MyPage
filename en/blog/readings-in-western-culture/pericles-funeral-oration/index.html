    This is the speech gave at Athens’ winter public funeral. It overflows with a confidence and hope unusual in a funeral speech. Pericles spends most of it praising Athens’ institutions and the Athenians’ way of life and courage.

    The Athenian public funeral (patrios nomos) was an extremely solemn ceremony:

    • Time: The winter after the first year of war.

    • Process: The remains of the fallen are placed in a tent for viewing. On the day of the funeral, ten caskets of cypress wood (representing the ten tribes of Athens) are transported by chariot to the public cemetery (Kerameikos) outside the city.

    • Empty Coffins: There will be an empty coffin at the site, symbolizing the missing persons whose bones could not be recovered. This method of handling reflects Athens’ extremely high respect for every individual citizen.

    What war was this? The Peloponnesian War broke out between the Athenian-led and the Spartan-led . Pericles spoke in its first year, when the Athenians were sure they would win. In the end, the war brought Athens’ golden age to a close. Athens tore down its Long Walls, dissolved its alliance, and briefly fell under the Thirty Tyrants. Years of fighting weakened the city-states and displaced the poor, clearing the way for the rise of Macedon in the north in the generation before Alexander the Great.

    Peloponnesian War

    In fact, Athens and Sparta were not opposed from the beginning. A brief summary is given below.

    Phase One: Working side-by-side, defending against the Persians (499 BC – 479 BC)

    Facing the expansion of the massive Persian Empire (), Athens and Sparta set aside their differences and formed the Hellenic League.

    • Clear Division of Labor: Sparta possessed an invincible army and served as the league commander; Athens, relying on its powerful navy, became the maritime pillar.

    • Iconic Battles: * Battle of Marathon (490 BC): The Athenians proved that Greek heavy infantry could defeat the Persian army.

      • Battle of Thermopylae (480 BC): The three hundred Spartans held back the massive Persian army, buying time for the Greeks to retreat.

      • Battle of Salamis (480 BC): The Athenian navy decisively destroyed the Persian fleet, turning the tide of the war.

    • Result: The Greeks successfully defended their freedom, but the victory also sowed the seeds of competition.

    Phase Two: The Rise of Empires and Cold War Standoff (478 BC – 432 BC)

    After the war, the Greek world was divided into two major camps, and this bipolarization of power directly led to the deterioration of relations.

    1. The Delian League and Athenian Hegemony

    To continue clearing out the remaining Persian forces, Athens formed the Delian League.

    • From Allies to Subjects: Athens gradually controlled the league’s finances and moved the headquarters from Delos Island to Athens. Allies became tributaries.

    • Confidence of the Era: Athens used the league’s funds to rebuild the Long Walls. This rapid expansion of wealth and naval power aroused the suspicion of Sparta.

    2. The Vigilance of the Peloponnesian League

    Sparta, as the traditional Greek hegemon, united the oligarchic city-states on land through the Peloponnesian League.

    • The Thucydidean Trap: Sparta feared that Athens’ expansion would eventually swallow all of Greece.

    Phase Three: The Peloponnesian War and Internal Collapse (431 BC – 404 BC)

    Due to irreconcilable conflicts of interest and ideology (democracy vs. oligarchy), internal strife erupted.

    • Stalemate Period: Sparta burned crops on land, while Athens blockaded supplies by sea. Neither side could deliver a fatal blow to the other.

    • Turning Point and Radicalization: With the death of from plague, Athenian politics became radical and chaotic. The disastrous Sicilian Expedition severely weakened Athens.

    • Luring the Wolf In: In order to win, Sparta eventually accepted funding from their former enemy—the Persian Empire—and built a navy to defeat Athens at sea.

    What made the Peloponnesian War inevitable was the growth of Athens’ power and the fear it inspired in Sparta (). After the Persian Wars, Athens used the Delian League to make the Aegean its own inland sea. Its wealth and naval strength grew at an extraordinary rate. Sparta, a conservative power on land, saw Athenian democracy and sea power spread and felt that its position and security were directly threatened.

    Delian League,

    • leading to exponential growth in wealth and naval power. Sparta, as a conservative land power, felt its leadership and survival safety were directly threatened by the continuous expansion of Athenian democratic ideology and maritime hegemony.

    • In fact, the conflict between the two had inherent causes. They were also completely opposed in their social foundations:

    • Navy vs. Army: This asymmetrical military structure meant that both sides felt they could not defeat the other in the short term, leading to strategic misjudgment and psychological preparation for a long war.

    Heliots

    Expansion vs. Conservatism: Athens was a trade-based state that needed constant expansion to acquire resources and markets; Sparta was an agriculture-based state that tended to maintain the status quo and stability within the peninsula.

    Navy vs. Army: This asymmetrical military structure meant that both sides felt they could not defeat the other completely in the short term, leading to strategic misjudgment and psychological preparation for a long war.

    • Brief Analysis of War Causes (The Immediate Pretexts):

    • Siege of Potidaea: This was a city-state belonging to the Delian League but closely related to Corinth. Athens demanded that it dismantle its walls and expel its officials, which led to armed resistance, with Sparta’s allies secretly participating in the defense.

    • Megarian Decree: Issued a ban prohibiting Megarian merchants from entering any port or market of Athens and its allies. This was equivalent to an economic blockade, and since Megara was a member of the Peloponnesian League, Sparta considered it a provocation to the entire league.

    There is a small story regarding the Megarian Decree. Sparta was initially reluctant to conflict, so they sent envoys to mediate, hoping to lift the decree. Faced with the Spartan envoys, Tripoillus said that Athens had a law that strictly forbade removing any wooden plaque inscribed with a decree. However, the mediator was very clever and told : You can turn the plaque over. But this was still refused, and they resolved to go to war.

    The underlying reasons

    Some argue that the war owed much to the predicament of . As the 430s BCE drew to a close, faced unprecedented opposition in Athens. His opponents set aside direct attacks and began stripping away his support by targeting those close to him.

    • Phidias: The great sculptor responsible for rebuilding the Acropolis, accused of misappropriating public funds.

    • Aspasia: A companion of extraordinary intelligence, accused of “impiety” and “seduction.”

    • Anaxagoras: His philosophical tutor, accused of promoting atheism and exiled.

    These attacks directly targeted ‘s administrative competence and moral authority. Ancient historians like Plutarch believed that knew well that if he continued to maintain peace, he might become the next subject of impeachment.

    Comedians like Aristophanes publicly mocked in plays of the time (such as The Acharnians and The Peace): lighting the small fire of the Megarian Decree was to create a great conflagration, so that people could not see his political scandals in the thick smoke. Once war began, people would rely on ‘s outstanding talents.

    However, if we look at Thucydides’ account, appeared like an extremely rational geopolitical strategist. He believed:

    • Inevitability: War was bound to happen sooner or later. Since Athens was at its peak now (strongest navy, most funds), it was better to fight now rather than wait until Sparta was better prepared.

    • The survival of the empire. Athens had set out on the road to hegemony. Unless it used war as a deterrent, defections by its allies could topple its finances like dominoes.

    was taking advantage of forces already in motion:

    1. National Level: The structural contradiction between Athens and Sparta was saturated (the Thucydides Trap).

    2. Individual Level: was trapped in a political quagmire.

    For him, war could accomplish two things at once. It could settle the struggle over Athenian sea power and save his own political life.

    ’s plan, although it transferred the conflict in the short term, the plague that broke out in the first year of the war completely destroyed his prestige. The Athenians eventually impeached him and fined him. Shortly after his brief return, he died of the plague, failing to see the outcome of the war he himself had started.

    Athenian Democracy

    Our constitution is called a democracy because power is in the hands not of a minority but of the whole people. When it is a question of settling private disputes, everyone is equal before the law; when it is a question of putting one person before another in positions of public responsibility, what counts is not membership of a particular class, but the actual ability which the man possess. No one, so long as he has it in him to be of service to the state, is kept in political obscurity because of poverty.

    This is ‘s account of Athenian democracy, which seems quite ideal even in contemporary times. How did the Athenians establish such a system thousands of years ago?

    1. Draco’s Reforms (Draco, c. 621 BC): The Codification of Law

    Before Draco, laws existed only in the minds of the aristocracy, who could interpret them arbitrarily to oppress the common people. In the late 7th century BC, Athens was on the verge of collapse. Society was extremely turbulent at the time:

    • Aristocratic Strife: For example, the previous “Cylonian Conspiracy,” where the nobles fell into chaos fighting for power and advantage.

    • Class Conflict: The aristocracy judged arbitrarily based on unwritten customs, and the common people were extremely dissatisfied, leading to rampant private vengeance and blood feuds (Vendetta).

    To keep Athens from sliding into civil war, its archons commissioned Draco to write down its laws. The plan was to end the disorder through clear rules that applied to everyone.

    • The first written laws. Draco was to inscribe the laws on wooden tablets and put them on public display. The aristocracy lost its monopoly over their interpretation.

    • The cost of severity. The laws were so harsh that almost any crime, even stealing an apple, could be punished with death. People said Draco’s laws were written in blood instead of ink.

    • What this meant for democracy. The laws were harsh and yet established an early form of equality before the law. Aristocrats and commoners were liable to punishment according to the same written rules.

    Equality before the law

    • From private vengeance to public justice. Before Draco, if you killed someone, their family would take revenge on yours. He required homicide cases to be heard by a court such as the Areopagus. This transferred the power to punish from private hands to the state.

    • Distinguishing kinds of homicide. Draco made a legal distinction between premeditated murder and manslaughter, a striking advance in the history of law. Still, his rules concentrated on punishment and left social justice alone.

    • Growing conflict. Severe penalties and debt bondage left ordinary Athenians even less room to make a living.

    • Replacement. Just over thirty years later, Solon repealed almost all of Draco’s laws, keeping the provisions on homicide.

    Because the law code was too cruel, later English appeared such a word, referring to cold-heartedness and severity.

    2. Solon’s Reforms (594 BCE): Reshaping Athenian Society

    Draco’s harsh laws had failed to settle the conflict between rich and poor. Solon was brought in to find a peaceful settlement.

    • Economic emancipation. Solon issued the seisachtheia, or shaking off of burdens. By abolishing debt bondage, he restored the freedom and citizenship of Athenians enslaved for debt.

    • Property Class System: Abolished bloodline privilege, and instead divided citizens into four classes based on property income. This allowed wealthy commoners (like merchants) to enter the decision-making level.

    • Early institutions. Solon established the Council of Four Hundred and popular jury courts, giving ordinary citizens a part in public justice.

    Solon significantly reduced the severity of punishments:

    • Fines Replacing Death Penalty: Except for extremely rare crimes like treason or murder, most offenses against property and persons were replaced with fines (usually assessed in oxen or currency).

    • Introduction of ‘Public Prosecution’: This was a huge advancement. Previously, only the victim could bring a lawsuit. Solon stipulated that any citizen had the right to sue on behalf of a victim if they witnessed injustice. Logically, this meant that a crime was not just an injury to an individual, but a disruption to the polis’s order (System).

    Solon even enacted a very interesting law to force citizens to participate in politics:

    • Crime of Neutrality: If one did not join any side during internal strife or factional struggle in the polis, one would be stripped of citizenship.

    • His aim. Solon saw apathy toward public affairs as the city’s greatest enemy. He hoped to use law to make everyone an active participant in political life.

    1. Positive Significance

    • Laying the Foundation for Democracy: By linking rights to property rather than bloodline, he paved the way for later reforms by Cleisthenes.

    • Protecting citizenship. The abolition of debt bondage established civic freedom as a basic principle and helped make Athenian citizens a more cohesive political community.

    2. Limitations (The ‘Doctrine of Moderation’)

    Solon considered himself a balancer, and his slogan was “impartiality.”

    • Noble Dissatisfaction: Lost debt income and hereditary privileges.

    • Disappointment among the poor. The reforms fell short of the change ordinary people wanted most: redistribution of land.

    Famous saying: “I carry a large shield, protecting the front of both factions, so that neither side can unfairly gain an advantage.”

    Solon’s reforms left many conflicts unresolved and were followed by tyranny. Yet the principles of government by law and participation by citizens that he established earned him a place among the Seven Sages of Greece.

    Solon, after completing his legislative reforms in Athens, chose self-exile for ten years to ensure the stability of the law and avoid getting entangled in the vortex of power.

    3. Cleisthenes’ Reforms (508 BC): The Establishment of the Democratic System

    After an intervening period of tyranny, Cleisthenes broke the power of the old aristocracy.

    • Ten Tribes: Tribes were reorganized according to residential area (administrative district) rather than bloodline family. This reduced the influence of nobles in their own territories to zero, making neighbors judge by citizenship rather than surname.

    • Council of Five Hundred: Fifty representatives were chosen from each tribe by lottery to handle the daily affairs of the citizen assembly and prepare bills. Because selection was by lot and there were term limits, this greatly increased the opportunities for ordinary citizens to participate in high-level politics. Furthermore, the rotating presidency (Prytany) was adopted: the year was divided into 10 periods, and the 50 people from each tribe took turns serving as executive committee members, ensuring that power would not remain with one group for too long.

    • Ostracism Law: To prevent the re-emergence of a tyrant (dictator), Cleisthenes established this special defensive mechanism:

    1. Voting: The citizen assembly voted annually on whether ostracism was necessary.

    2. Process: If necessary, citizens would write the names of those they believed posed the greatest threat to the democratic stability on pottery shards.

    3. Result: If the votes exceeded 6000, the person with the highest number of votes must be exiled from abroad for 10 years.

    4. Characteristic: This was not a criminal punishment; it did not confiscate property or harm family members. Its essence was a political isolation, used to protect the stability of the democratic system.

    4. Era (Pericles, 461–429 BC): The Golden Age of Democracy

    This was the most radical and most perfect stage of Athenian democracy.

    • Universal Lottery: Most public offices were no longer based on wealth or prestige, but were chosen by lot, ensuring that every citizen had a chance to govern the state. Except for positions requiring specialized military skills, such as the ten generals, almost all administrative posts were changed to be selected by lot.

    • Stipend for Public Office: Wages were paid to citizens who participated in politics. This was crucial because it solved the problem of poor citizens being unable to participate in politics due to their need to earn a living. Before this, although the poor had the right to participate in politics, they could not leave their fields or workshops to attend meetings or serve as judges because they needed to work to support their families.

    • Sovereignty of the Assembly: (with its ally Ephialtes) stripped the old nobility of their last stronghold—the right to supervise and judge laws in the Areopagus Council. All major state affairs (going to war, forming alliances, budgeting) were decided by a show of hands from all citizens in the Agora.

    While expanding internal democracy, passed the strict Citizenship Law in 451 BC.

    • Requirement: Only adult males whose parents were both Athenian citizens could obtain Athenian citizenship.

    • Purpose: 1. To limit the number of people receiving public stipends, easing the financial burden. 2. To enhance the sense of superiority and cohesion of the citizen body. 3. To prevent foreign nobles from interfering in Athenian politics through marriage.

    Thoughts on stipends

    1. It is the “fuel” of the democratic system

    During the times of Solon and Cleisthenes, democracy was open in law but exclusionary in economics. A farmer living far away, if he went to participate in the citizen assembly, it meant his whole family might go hungry for a day.

    • Logical Shift: realized that democracy without economic compensation was merely a “luxury for the rich.” The stipend system transformed participation in politics from an “expensive obligation” into a “paid profession.”

    • Result: It truly activated the political enthusiasm of the fourth class (the hired laborers), leading Athenian democracy from “formal democracy” to “deep democracy.”

    2. It is an appendage of Athenian hegemony

    A frequently overlooked fact is: where did Athens get so much money to pay salaries?

    • Contribution of the Delian League: The main source of funds for the stipends was not Athens’ local tax revenue, but tribute paid by the allies.

    • Controversy: This democracy carried a strong “imperialist” color. The democratic benefits enjoyed by Athenian citizens were largely built upon the exploitation of other Greek city-states. This led to a contradiction: extremely democratic internally, but extremely hegemonic externally.

    3. Voices of Opposition

    Critics at the time (such as Plato and Aristotle) held strong doubts about the stipend system:

    • Moral Criticism: The Sophist school believed that stipends made citizens “lazy, timid, and greedy,” and participation in politics was no longer out of a sense of duty to the city-state, but for those two or three obols.

    • Mediocre Politics: When everyone could govern the state through lot and salary, professional decision-making was often replaced by radical emotion. Athens’ several blind decisions during the Peloponnesian War are often attributed to this “populist democracy of getting paid to do things.”

    Life in Athens

    And, just as our political life is free and open, so is our day-to-day life in our relations with each other. We do not get into a state with our next-door neighbour if he enjoys himself in his own way, nor do we give him the kind of black looks which, though they do no real harm, still do hurt people’s feelings.

    The daily life of the Athenians was as free and open as their political life. They did not give people bad looks just because their way of life was different.

    When our work is over, we are in a position to enjoy all kinds of recreation for our spirits. There are various kinds of contests and sacrifices regularly throughout the year; in our own homes we find a beauty and a good taste which delight us every day and which drive away our cares.

    Athens’ was very rich:

    1. Athletic Contests (Gymnic Contests)

    This is the project closest to modern Olympics, mainly held during the Panathenaic Games:

    • Running (Stadion & Diaulos): Including a sprint of about 192 meters and a return run. The most unique was the Hoplitodromos (armed race), where participants had to run wearing helmets, greaves, and carrying shields.

    • Pentathlon: Including long jump, javelin, discus, sprint, and wrestling, considered the peak of showcasing perfect male physical proportions.

    • Combat Sports: Including wrestling, boxing, and the extremely violent Pankration—with almost no rules limiting eye gouging or biting.

    • Equestrian and Chariot Races: The arena for Athens’ wealthy class, grand in scale and extremely dangerous.

    2. Musical & Dramatic Contests

    For the Athenians, this was more spiritually appealing than athletic contests, mainly held during the Great Dionysia:

    • Tragedy: Three playwrights were selected every year, each submitting three tragedies and one satyr play. Masters like Sophocles and Euripides emerged from this competition.

    • Comedy: Full of satire and humor; the judges were randomly selected from the citizenry, and the winner could gain extremely high social status.

    • Dithyramb: A competition performed by male or children’s choirs of 50 people sent from various Athenian Tribes, representing the collective honor of the city-state.

    • Musical Instruments and Recitation: Including performances on the Lyre, playing the Aulos, and professional recitation contests of the Homeric epics.

    Around 429 BCE, Sophocles’ Oedipus Rex premiered at the Dionysia Theatre, and it only won second prize.

    The winner of the first prize that year was Philocles, who was the nephew of the great tragedian Aeschylus.

    We can look at the underlying operational logic of the Athenian dramatic festival to see why this perfect masterpiece was “second place” at the time:

    I. Selection Mechanism: Randomness and “Lottery”

    The core of Athenian democracy was lottery, and the dramatic festival was no exception.

    • Process: One judge was selected from each of the ten tribes, and their names were placed in a jar. After the performance, each judge wrote down a ranking on a wooden board.

    • Random Elimination: The archon randomly drew 5 votes from the jar as the final score.

    • Conclusion: This algorithmic design was intended to prevent bribery and factional manipulation, but it also introduced massive random noise. Even if most judges favored Sophocles, if the 5 drawn votes happened to favor others, the result would reverse.

    II. Historical Context: The Peloponnesian War and the Plague

    The opening of Oedipus Rex is the city of Thebes being struck by a plague.

    • Historical Reflection: During 430-429 BCE, Athens was suffering through a devastating plague, and also perished because of it.

    • Psychological Defense: For the Athenian audience of the time, the theater was supposed to be a place for catharsis and seeking comfort. However, Sophocles brought the extremely tragic, almost desperate scenes of the plague directly onto the stage. This kind of “overly realistic, hardcore simulation” might have caused both physical and psychological discomfort to the judges who were themselves in the midst of a disaster.

    III. Aesthetic Tendencies: Conservatism vs. Innovation

    • Philocles (Winner): His style was considered to inherit the solemnity and tradition of his uncle, Aeschylus.

    • Sophocles: Although he was the darling of Athens (winning the first prize about 20 times in his lifetime), Oedipus Rex is structurally extremely tight and cold, like a precise process of logical deduction. The judges might have felt that while his work was technically perfect, in the context of the political turmoil at the time, it was less aligned with the traditional “religious ritualistic feeling” than Philocles’ work.

    Athenian dramatic culture was very rich and also served the function of recording history. For example, the change in law mentioned earlier appeared in Aeschylus’s trilogy of tragedies, the Oresteia. Son kills mother, so the goddesses of vengeance seek trouble for the son, but the son says it is because the mother killed the father, and he must take revenge. What about the mother? The mother is seeking revenge for her daughter because the father killed her daughter. And what about the father? The father killed the daughter as a sacrifice to the gods. So, should the son be guilty of death? The son flees to Athens and seeks asylum. Athena does not directly use divine power to pronounce judgment, but rather:

    1. Forming a Jury: Twelve Athenian citizens were selected to form the court (this is a literary projection of Athenian jury system).

    2. Conducting Debate: Apollo served as the defense attorney, and the goddesses of vengeance served as the prosecutors.

    3. Voting: The result was a tie of 6 to 6.

    4. Athena’s Vote: Athena cast the crucial “vote of not guilty” (Athena’s Vote), establishing the principle of “innocent until proven guilty” or “a tie means no guilt.”

    3. Civic & Intellectual Contests

    This reflects the unique aspect of Athens as a “school for all Greece”:

    • Rhetoric and Oratory: In the law courts (Heliaia) or the assembly (Ekklesia), debate was essentially an intellectual contest. Athenians enjoyed this, and they would even crown outstanding speakers with a golden wreath.

    • Beauty Pageant (Euandria): This was not about looks, but about selecting the group of men from each tribe with the strongest physical fitness and best discipline, carrying a strong military flavor.

    • Torch Relay (Lampadedromia): Combining speed and teamwork, the finish line was usually at the altar, and the winner was responsible for lighting the sacred fire.

    Spartan Training

    In contrast, the core goal of the Spartan education system was singular: to forge boys into killing machines, placing the interest of the city-state above individual will.

    1. Elimination from Birth

    In Sparta, a child belonged not to its parents, but to the city-state.

    • Infant Inspection: After birth, the baby underwent a physical inspection by the elders. Weak or deformed children were abandoned at the place of abandonment on Mount Taygetus (Apothetae).

    • Preliminary Screening: Legend has it that the Spartans bathed the infants in strong liquor to test their constitution and nerve reactions.

    2. Age Seven: Entering the Agoge System

    Boys left the family at the age of 7 and lived in a communal barracks.

    • Starvation Marketing: Instructors deliberately did not give the children enough food, forcing them to steal.

      • Logic: It was not to encourage crime, but to train their stealth, courage, and observational skills.

      • Punishment: If stealing was caught, the punishment was not for the act of “stealing,” but for being “stupid enough to be discovered.”

    • Barefoot and Single Garment: Regardless of whether it was winter or summer, the children could only wear a thin cloak (Chiton) and must walk barefoot, to temper the resilience of their skin and their ability to withstand the cold.

    • Reed Bed: They had to bend reeds themselves from the Eurotas River to lay down their beds, and in winter, they even mixed thorns into the reeds for warmth.

    3. Extreme Violence and Obedience

    • Combat Training: This contest in Sparta had no referees, only winners and losers.

    • Whipping Ceremony (Diamastigosis): In front of the temple of Artemis, the youths were whipped and forbidden from making a sound. This was both a religious sacrifice and the ultimate test of endurance.

    • Crypteia: The final stage of training, where the most outstanding young men were sent into the wilderness with only a dagger. Their task was to infiltrate late at night and hunt the Helots (slaves) who might rebel, completing the transformation from student to killer.

    In proudly say that Athenians do not need to be like the Spartans in order to possess courage and strength that can rival, or even surpass, those of the Spartans.

    Curiosity: The Spartans had the girls publicly evaluate the boys undergoing training. In the eyes of the Spartans, shame was a more powerful motivator than physical pain. This public evaluation by women regarding men mainly occurred during festivals and athletic competitions:

    • Motivating Competition: When boys were lax in training or lost in competition, the girls would be organized to compose satirical songs and sing them publicly to humiliate them.

    • Shaping Collective Honor: To avoid being mocked by their peers (future spouses), the boys trained desperately.

    • Reverse Praise: Similarly, outstanding boys received songs of praise from the girls; this immense sense of honor was the core of the Spartan warrior spirit.

    In terms of women’s rights, Sparta was much better than Athens. The most substantial power for Spartan women came from land ownership.

    • Inheritance Rights: Spartan law allowed women to inherit land. If a family had no male heir, the daughter would receive the entire estate.

    • Concentration of Wealth: By the 4th century BCE, Aristotle recorded with astonishment that nearly 40% of the land in Sparta was held by women.

    • Management Power: Boys entered the barracks at age seven and were busy with warfare and training for life. All family assets, including farmland, slaves (Black laborers), and financial expenditures, were decided solely by the wife. They were the de facto “CEO” of the household.”, 12⟧

    In other city-states, the standard of feminine beauty was fair skin and fragility; but in Sparta, strength was a female duty.

    • State-Level Training: Spartan girls and boys alike received physical training funded by the state. They practiced running, wrestling, javelin throwing, and discus throwing.

    • Logical Closed Loop: The Spartans held a simple biological view—only strong parents could bear strong warriors.

    • Freedom of Dress: For ease of movement, Spartan women wore short skirts slit to the thigh (called the Peplos), which was mocked by other Greeks of the time as the “bare-legged tribe,” but also reflected their disdain for physical restraint.
