    The One Who Buries Flowers

    Feb 14, 2022 Shane Lorien

    I think I wrote this in my last year of middle school. Clumsy, self-important adolescent wish-fulfillment literature (does it count as literature?), and rather endearing, or so I feel now. I once handed it in as an assignment and got a telling-off. Looking back, I can quite understand the teacher. I can understand myself, too.

    Blossoms fade, blossoms fly, blossoms fill the sky.
    When the red is gone and the fragrance fails, who will pity them?

    On a whim, I went wandering about the public grounds.
    In a place out of the way I found fallen flowers heaped on the ground. A faint fragrance came to me all at once, and I followed it in.

    Green trees grew on either side. A little stream flowed slowly between them, with a wooden bridge across it.
    I had somehow never come upon this quiet place before.

    As I stepped onto the bridge, I caught sight of a woman under a tree beside it, digging with a spade.

    She was slight, her shoulders as if carved, her waist as if bound with white silk;
    her long, graceful neck showed fair as jade.
    Bent over that spade, scraping away at the earth, she made so passing strange a sight that I wanted to laugh and found I could not.

    So I went nearer and asked her.

    “What are you, what are you doing?”

    She lifted her head, and only then did I see her face:

    Two mist-veiled brows, drawn together and yet not quite;
    two tender eyes, smiling and yet not quite.

    “I’m burying flowers.”

    “Why bury flowers? There are so many. Can you bury them all?”

    She answered:

    “I come here often to look at the flowers and breathe their scent.
    Now that they’ve fallen, I lay them in the earth.
    What could be wrong with that?
    There are flowers enough in the world. Today I bury the ones I happened to meet.”

    She bowed her head and dug on.

    I thought it very strange.
    So there really was such a person in the world.

    Watching her clumsy strokes with the spade, I felt there was something clear and natural about her:
    like a river, like a mountain,
    like the still flame of a bright candle,
    like the faint grace of the morning star.

    For some reason I wanted to bury flowers too.
    I went about picking up fallen blossoms and laying them in the hollow she’d dug.

    She stared for a moment, and stopped.

    “… Thank you.”

    Then she left the spade and gathered flowers beside me.

    For an hour or two of the old reckoning,
    we did nothing else,
    only gathered and buried flowers.

    My mind felt clearer and emptier for it.

    When we were done, there was not a fallen blossom in sight.
    She went back to the hollow and put the earth back in, handful by handful.
    She sighed long, as though a weight had lifted:

    “At least they’ll have some quiet now.
    They had the knives of the wind and the swords of the frost to contend with in life.
    In death, they won’t be trodden on over and over.”

    I heard it, and thought I understood.

    “So you’re burying flowers the way Daiyu did.”

    She looked up at me.

    “How is that imitation? Have you never felt what someone in a book feels?”

    Then I knew I’d spoken out of turn.

    “… I’m sorry.”

    She sighed softly.

    “You’re not so bad. Most people would call me stupid. They wouldn’t even think to call it folly.”

    Before I knew what to say to that,
    she began, suddenly, to sing:

    “At the end of the sky, where is a sweet-scented grave?”

    She broke off in a cough.
    It was slight at first, and grew harsh very quickly.

    “Are you all right?”

    She took no notice. With what voice she had, she sang on:

    “At the end of the sky, where is a sweet-scented grave?
    You die today, and I will see you buried.
    I cannot tell what day will see me laid away.
    I bury flowers, and people laugh at this fool.
    Who will there be to bury me some year?”

    The song came and went, the coughing in between.
    Suddenly a dark red thread ran from the corner of her mouth.

    Where the fallen red petals had lain,
    red fell again, drop by drop.

    “You—”

    She only smiled sadly and gave a few more small coughs.

    A long silence.

    She said softly:

    “Will you stay and look at the flowers with me?”

    “… All right.”

    We sat together on the bridge,
    listened to the stream running,
    and looked at the blossoms about to fall, still on the branch.

    Some were white,
    some red,
    all small and delicately made.

    A little while later she stood.
    I stood too.

    She took my hand quite suddenly.
    I was startled. Then I understood.

    I felt stiff inside, unused to it,
    and followed her woodenly along the path to look at the flowers.
    We walked up and down a few times
    and sat again on the bridge.

    She just looked toward the end of the path
    and said nothing.

    As dusk came on
    she rose quite suddenly
    and kissed me lightly on the forehead.

    A faint scent came over me, and I was dazed.

    I looked up.
    Her lashes were already wet with tears.

    “Thank you … goodbye.”

    She stumbled away,
    came before a tree, and bowed low.

    She plucked a blossom just short of falling,
    put it in her hair,
    and went on.

    She began to sing:

    “You die today, and I will see you buried—”

    In the dusk
    a solitary, cool figure drifted away.

    I thought I saw
    a lone cuckoo
    in the wan, stricken sunlight,
    going farther and farther away.

    There was still warmth in my hand.
    The wet mark was still on my forehead.

    All that was left:
    a patch of blood-red,
    spent blossoms and running water,
    the silent face of a fool.

    I covered the blood with flowers and earth.

    I came here often after that.

    I never saw her again.

    When I had time, I looked at the flowers.
    When I found them fallen, I buried them.

    I cannot quite tell
    whether I was burying flowers,
    or water,
    or time,
    or that person.

    One who buries flowers,
    burying flowers,
    burying a person,
    burying folly.

    Today you bury flowers.
    Another day, one buries you.
    A flower too, I suppose.
